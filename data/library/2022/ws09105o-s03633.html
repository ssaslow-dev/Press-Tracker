Antisemitic incidents fuel outrage at Kanye West

Holocaust Museum L.A. received online threats after inviting Kanye West to visit the museum for a dialogue, and has hired additional security. (Beverly Press/Park Labrea News file photo)

For weeks, Jewish advocacy groups have warned that Kanye West’s hateful rhetoric could inflame antisemitism rising across the country.

Their predictions have beared out. Extremists giving Nazi salutes draped a banner above an overpass on the San Diego (405) Freeway on Oct. 22 that read “Kanye is right about the Jews.” The next day, residents in Beverly Hills and Los Angeles awoke to antisemitic propaganda scattered across their neighborhoods.

Holocaust Museum L.A. has had to hire additional security officers after receiving hateful threats and messages following their invitation to give West a tour of the museum, a museum spokeswoman said. She added that the invitation still stands.

“Hate groups … who are tracked and followed by the [Anti-Defamation League] are gleeful right now,” said Jeffrey Abrams, regional director of ADL Los Angeles. “They have Kanye West amongst their supporters.”

While West, also known as Ye, may be gaining followers among extremists, his behavior has alienated him from the businesses that have made him one of the richest, most famous entertainers in the world. Prominent advocacy organizations and politicians have denounced him, his talent agency and lawyer have dropped him as clients, and Adidas, which partnered with West to produce his Yeezy shoes, has cut ties.

With the effects of West’s hate speech becoming increasingly clear, it is not enough for powerful people and groups to condemn his brand of hatred, Abrams said. Everyone must do their part. “People of good faith must act. People of good faith must use their voice,” he said.

In Los Angeles, the flyer drops and freeway demonstration appear to have been perpetrated by the same hate group, Abrams said. With leadership based in Northern California, the group is national in scope and growing in size, though it has a small number of dedicated members, he added.

The hate group’s guiding mission is to evoke a response in the victims they target, disseminating flyers similar to those found on Oct. 23 and harassing Jews heading into synagogues, filming footage that they often stream live. The biggest threat is not from the group itself, but from how many others are adapting and adopting their methodology, Abrams said.

About 25 flyers were recovered near homes north of Sunset Boulevard and west of Benedict Canyon Drive in Beverly Hills, said Beverly Hills Police Department Sgt. Scott Dibble. Police have not made any arrests or identified any suspects, he added.

Los Angeles Police Department officers are investigating as well.

“The [LAPD] is aggressively investigating a series of flyer drops that preliminarily appears connected to incidents in Beverly Hills. The investigation is being handled by [the] Major Crimes Division and I understand we have some video footage of a vehicle that was involved,” said LAPD spokesman Drake Madison.

Hate crimes in general are trending upward in L.A., with a 71% increase recorded since 2020, LAPD Chief Michel Moore said. During that same time, hate crimes against Jews increased by 76%, he added.

“These types of hate instances and hate crimes are not to be tolerated,” Moore said, adding that the department will, “pursue every enforcement lever possible to identify those persons responsible and ensure the full prosecution of [those] individuals.”

The incidents in Los Angeles and Beverly Hills elicited outrage locally and around the world. L.A. City Council candidate Sam Yebri, who was one of the Westwood residents who found propaganda at their homes, blasted elected officials and the press for not doing more to curb the hatred, and posted a photo and video to Twitter of what he claimed was one of the vehicles used to distribute the flyers.

“My wife and I were shocked and horrified to see the hate that has been swirling around L.A. appear on our own doorstep a mere few feet from where our kids were,” Yebri said. “As Angelenos, we must link arms and speak up in one voice to condemn the rise of hatred and bigotry in our city.”

Beverly Hills Mayor Lili Bosse, Beverly Hills City Councilman John Mirisch and L.A. City Councilmembers Mitch O’Farrell, 13th District, and Paul Koretz, 5th District, were among the elected officials who decried the rising antisemitism.

Bosse, a child of Holocaust survivors, said that while the group responsible for the antisemitic incidents sought to sow division, it has actually united people against hatred.

“This is about humanity. This is hate speech and we need to stand up for each other and say that this is wrong,” Bosse said.

Like Bosse, Koretz is also the child of Holocaust survivors.

“As a son of a Holocaust survivor who has made ‘never forget’ central to my personal mission to lead with compassion and treat all others with dignity, I am incredibly dismayed that this kind of discrimination and hateful propaganda continues to grow in todays’ day and age,” Koretz said. “I wonder if this was the kind of thing my father was seeing in early 1930s Germany.”

To combat the uptick in antisemitism, Koretz introduced a resolution to adopt the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance’s working definition of antisemitism, which has been adopted by cities including West Hollywood and Beverly Hills. Due largely to West’s antisemitic tirades, his partnerships with Adidas, Gap and Balenciaga, some of his most lucrative, have fallen apart. After West pulled out of his Gap deal in September, Balenciaga cut ties on Oct. 21, and Creative Artists Agency followed suit on Oct. 24.

The ADL launched #RunAwayHate the same day to pressure Adidas into also ending its relationship. The campaign garnered support amongst thousands of Twitter users, celebrities like David Schwimmer and politicians including State Sen. Scott Wiener (D-San Francisco) and Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel (D-Los Angeles), who joined ADL deputy regional director Teresa Drenick in an Oct. 24 webinar to endorse the pressure campaign.

“Anyone who uses that kind of language needs to be ostracized, and that includes Adidas and any other company who works with [West],” Wiener said, “I understand Kanye is a very, very important commercial asset for Adidas, but there can be no gray area here, when he is literally calling for the killing of Jews.”

The next day, Adidas ended its partnership, a move that could cost the company more than $240 million, it said in a statement.
