Sacramento revisits a privacy debate

By SUSANNAH LUTHI

LABOR’S LOVE LOST: We’re watching an under-the-radar drama that could have huge repercussions in workplaces across California.

It revolves around the right to privacy Californians can expect when they’re working.

On a basic level, lawmakers will have to answer questions that are now commonplace in an age of almost constant surveillance.

  • For example: Can a trucking company track its drivers when they’re off the clock?
  • Do bosses have to tell their remote, Zooming employees if or when they are screening their emails or Slack messages? Recording their phone calls? Taking video recordings via work-owned laptops?

And then there are the complicated logistical matters.

  • If companies can’t collect or store their workers’ data, does that interfere with other functions, like cybersecurity shields on their computer networks?
  • In making sure they’re not storing personal data that their employees don’t want them to, do companies have to read workers’ personal emails so they know what to delete?

The deadline for a fix could be tight, depending on how lawmakers decide to pass their bill. Legislators carved the worker-privacy rules out of the state’s consumer privacy law that took effect in 2020, but only temporarily: those exemptions expire at the end of the year. Labor groups have made it clear they’re not OK with another punt.

Three lawmakers are rushing to cobble together a compromise: State Sen. Bob Hertzberg (D-Van Nuys), who helped steer the state’s landmark privacy law, the California Consumer Privacy Act; Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), who chairs the Assembly’s privacy panel; and Assemblymember Ash Kalra (D-San Jose), a key labor ally.

Kalra had to ax his own labor-backed proposal last month after panic from businesses and legislators alike. The worry was that in trying to beef up privacy measures for workers, the 33-page proposal would have unleashed even more surveillance on employees and contractors, while making it tough for businesses to comply.

For example, the California Chamber of Commerce argued the way Kalra’s bill was written could have required businesses to check their workers’ email accounts for personal messages — and then delete any they found.

The Hertzberg-Gabriel-Kalra trio has less than a month to reach a deal if they want to pass it in through policy committees before summer recess. With some of Sacramento’s most powerful and feisty lobbying powers involved, what could go wrong?

California energy regulators are reopening public input on a wildly unpopular proposal to cut rooftop solar benefits and impose a monthly grid charge.

The California Public Utilities Commission had originally planned a January vote on the draft decision, but postponed it following public outcry and criticisms from key lawmakers in the state’s congressional delegation — not to mention from former GOP Gov. Arnold Schwarzenegger and current Democratic Gov. Gavin Newsom.

Now, the CPUC wants Californians to weigh in on parts of its plan, with a June 10 deadline for feedback.


ABORTION ACCESS: A bill to create a state-administered fund that would help people, including those from outside of California, cover the costs of getting an abortion here was referred today to the state Senate Appropriation Committee’s “suspense file,” where its fate will be determined May 19. The brief hearing did draw some abortion opponents, but the committee’s chair, state Sen. Anthony Portantino (D–La Cañada Flintridge), countered with his support, describing himself as the father of two daughters.

Advocates and the Legislative Women’s Caucus are seeking $20 million to launch the fund. Senate Bill 1142, by state Sens. Nancy Skinner (D-Berkeley) and Anna Caballero (D-Salinas), would also create an online portal to provide information about where and how to seek abortion services in California.

MICRA ANALYSIS: An analysis released today by UCLA researchers suggests that California’s longtime cap on pain-and-suffering awards in malpractice cases could actually have contributed to an increase in malpractice cases over the past 50 years — potentially by weakening the deterrent effect of being sued.

UCLA researchers reviewed state Medi-Cal data on potential malpractice cases from huge screw-ups like mismatched blood-type infusions or objects left inside patients. The researchers found more of these preventable mistakes — about 16 percent more — in states where such caps exist.

Since California spent $1.5 billion on these kinds of medical mess-ups in 2018, the researchers suggested a 16 percent reduction could translate into a $245 million savings. The research comes as California gears up to adjust its 1970s cap of $250,000 to catch up with inflation.
