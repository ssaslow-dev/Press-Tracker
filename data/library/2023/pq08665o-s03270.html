Inside enviros’ last-minute push on oil bills

By Wes Venteicher; Blanca Begert

With help from Jordan Wolman and Allison Prang

WHAT DO YOU WANT: A lot happened over the weekend, and today we’re taking you behind the scenes on the forces that came together to make sure Newsom dealt Big Oil a few more major blows Saturday.

But first — take our poll on what we should cover next.

FOILING OIL: Environmental organizations launched a last-minute lobbying blitz late last week to get Gov. Gavin Newsom to go against his Finance Department and sign a bill imposing costly new well closure requirements on oil companies.

In a signing message, Newsom said AB 1167 helps minimize the risk that the state will become responsible for the costs of plugging abandoned wells. But he also called for lawmakers to revisit the legislation, saying that adding costs for well transfers could increase the risk that companies might abandon them.

Supporters feared a veto up until Saturday morning, so they mounted a campaign to present the governor’s office with data countering Finance Department fears from environmental nonprofits the FracTracker Alliance and the Carbon Tracker Initiative.

The Natural Resources Defense Council and Environment California, the bill’s co-sponsors, secured a last-minute support letter from Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara and met with Natural Resources Secretary Wade Crowfoot last week. On Friday afternoon, a small group of supporters met virtually with the Conservation Department’s Geologic Energy Management Division, including new oil and gas supervisor Douglas Ito, for an hour and 15 minutes.

“The results are that Gov. Newsom acted consistently with his stance that he wants to be a leader in not only moving toward clean energy but in addressing problems with the oil industry,” said Ann Alexander, a senior NRDC attorney.

Neither NRDC nor the bill’s primary opponent, the Western States Petroleum Association, knew what Newsom would decide until Saturday morning.

Newsom signed the bill, authored by Assemblymember Wendy Carrillo (D-Los Angeles), as part of a climate package that delivered across-the-board defeats for the oil industry, including vetoing SB 842 — a bill that would have diluted gasoline price-gouging legislation he championed early this year — and signing new emissions disclosure laws. (More on that below.)

“We’ve been his No. 1 villain, unfortunately,” said Western States Petroleum Association spokesperson Kevin Slagle. “But we’re always hopeful we can find a way to work together because energy and climate issues are way too important to be approached the way they've been approached for the last several years — with bans, mandates and name calling.”

COPYCATS: Now that Newsom has signed into law two groundbreaking corporate climate disclosure measures, lawmakers from New York to Washington state intend to follow with their own bills requiring companies to reveal their carbon footprints and their climate-related financial risks.

But influence groups on opposite sides of the issue are warning that a patchwork of state-level copycat rules could do more harm than good by increasing the cost and complexity of compliance, reports Jordan Wolman.

Ceres, a nonprofit that aims to align corporations around sustainability issues and supported California’s legislation, is recommending against other states pursuing their own climate disclosure measures.

Steven Rothstein, managing director of the Ceres Accelerator for Sustainable Capital Markets, said further state efforts may not be necessary given that the California laws will sweep in large publicly traded and private companies that operate across states. He also said companies shouldn’t be forced to navigate complex disclosure regulations that could vary across states.

That last point is seconded by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, which opposes the Securities and Exchange Commission’s proposed disclosure rules. Tom Quaadman, executive vice president of the Chamber’s Center for Capital Markets Competitiveness, said blue-state efforts to pass climate disclosure measures could boost the red-state backlash, leading to a competing set of requirements.

“Then layer on top of that the European [rules],” Quaadman said. “This creates a fragmented approach that becomes unworkable for American businesses.”

It’s a point many business groups made in opposition to California developing its own rules in addition to those longawaited from the SEC.

The lawmakers plan on forging ahead anyway, citing an absence of federal action.

“Is a 50-state patchwork of laws on a subject matter preferable?” asked Democratic New York state Sen. Brad HoylmanSigal. “No, but when there’s a vacuum at the federal level, states need to determine the best way forward.” ANOTHER SEC RUNAROUND: Here’s another place where Newsom got ahead of the Securities and Exchange Commission in requiring more corporate transparency: AB 1305. Signed Saturday, it will require companies to publicly disclose if they are using voluntary carbon offsets and mandate that offset developers provide information about their projects.

The law is believed to be the first-of-its-kind in the country. Once again, it puts the state ahead of the SEC, which in its proposed climate-disclosure rule also included language requiring companies to disclose their use of offsets. It’s unclear, however, whether that language will be included in the agency’s final rule, which, as noted above, we’re all still waiting on.

Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), the legislation’s sponsor, told POLITICO’s Allison Prang he wasn’t trying to bigfoot the SEC on the issue, but hopes that the offsets bill can serve as a model to other states. Moves by federal agencies can also be reversed from administration to administration, he said, whereas having something in a statute “is a very powerful way to drive change.”

“The idea here is to bring a little transparency and accountability to this market,” said Gabriel. Meanwhile, Newsom vetoed another bill to do even more on that front from Sen. Monique Limón (D-Santa Barbara). (More on that below.)

VETO TIME: In addition to the big ticket items above, Newsom signed a flurry of other environmental bills over the weekend, from legislation that allows the state to sign long-term contracts with offshore wind farms to a bill that turns the 30x30 conservation target into law. And the Right to Repair Act, to allow Californians to fix electronics more easily and keep them out of landfills, was just signed today.

Here are some big ones he didn’t sign:

— SB 390, by Sen. Monique Limón (D-Santa Barbara), would have made it illegal to sell carbon offsets that are not quantifiable, real and representing additional carbon storage. Newsom wrote that he supported the intent of the bill, but vetoed it with the logic that imposing civil liability for even unintentional mistakes “could inadvertently capture wellintentioned sellers and verifiers of voluntary offsets, and risk creating significant turmoil in the market for carbon offsets.” — For reasons he said were budget related, Newsom nixed SB 394 from Lena Gonzalez (D-Long Beach) which would have directed state agencies to create a master plan for “healthy, sustainable and climate-resilient schools” with clean energy technologies and updated cooling and air filtration systems. He did sign AB 579, from Assemblymember Phil Ting (D-San Francisco), to require most school districts to transition to electric bus fleets, despite Finance Department opposition.

— SB 420, from Sen. Josh Becker (D-Menlo Park), would have exempted medium-size transmission projects from a specific PUC permit in an effort to speed them up. In a veto message, Newsom, who has pushed other efforts to fast-track transmission infrastructure, said the change would have reassigned responsibilities to local agencies, making permitting more complex.

— SB 619, from Sen. Steve Padilla (D-Chula Vista), also sought to trim permitting times. The bill would have given utilities an option to get CEQA reviews from the Energy Commission instead of the Public Utilities Commission and limited the reviews to 270 days. In a veto message, Newsom said decentralizing the review process would only slow things down.

CALBIKE TO CALTRANS: California’s statewide bicycle advocacy coalition, CalBike, has some words for the California Department of Transportation. The organization issued a statement after Debra Kahn reported on how Caltrans demoted Jeanie Ward-Waller, a leading low-carbon mobility advocate, and former CalBike policy director, at the agency.

In addition to making California roads safe for walking and biking, “the agency is also tasked with moving our transportation network away from car dependency to support the state's climate change goals,” reads the statement. “As the recent firing of the person responsible for keeping Caltrans on track with those goals shows, the agency that used to be called the Department of Highways still sees its primary role as increasing California’s highway capacity, working against the transition to a green transportation future.”

A Caltrans spokesperson said the agency doesn’t comment on personnel matters
