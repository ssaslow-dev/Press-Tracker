CALIFORNIA BUDGET DEFICIT SPARKS FEARS OVER CLIMATE, CLEAN ENERGY FUNDING

By Curt Barry

Posted December 7, 2023 Analysts' projections that California faces a $58 billion budget deficit heading into 2024 is sparking concerns among environmental, equity and clean energy groups that funding for key state programs could be slashed and the state could falter on its numerous pledges to reduce greenhouse gas emissions across multiple sectors.

"We're concerned that the state's increasingly negative fiscal outlook could impair its ability to live up to the legislature and governor's commitments to climate investments in vital areas like zero-emission transportation," says one environmentalist.

A source with California Environmental Voters adds that lawmakers must prioritize climate spending despite the state's revenue reductions. "Even amid budget shortfalls, our state must commit to increasing investments in climate action. California is currently spending an insignificant percentage of the overall state budget on climate solutions and, regardless of budget surpluses or deficits, that has to change."

Stakeholders are reacting to a Dec. 1 alert from the state's Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) that says its updated revenue outlook anticipates collections to come in $58 billion below California's previous budget projections for fiscal years 2022-23 and 24-25.

"With the recent receipt of various postponed tax payments, the impact of recent economic weakness and last year's financial market distress on state revenues has become clearer," the paper states. "The postponed payments came in much weaker than anticipated. As a result, we now estimate 2022-23 revenues to be $26 billion below Budget Act projections." Further, "Historical experience suggests this weakness is likely to carry into this fiscal year and next. Overall, our updated revenue outlook anticipates collections to come in $58 billion below Budget Act projections across 2022-23 to 2024-25." At press time, LAO was scheduled to release a "Fiscal Outlook" that "will discuss the ramifications of this revenue shortfall for the state's budget," the alert says.

The state's FY23-24 budget approved in June contained roughly $48 billion over multiple years for a variety of climate and clean energy programs, which would be paid for mostly by the General Fund and Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund. However, that amount is $6 billion less than what was originally proposed by Gov. Gavin Newsom (D) in January, who was forced to make the cut due to a $32 billion budget deficit that emerged in the spring.

Now, Newsom and lawmakers may be forced to significantly reduce that $48 billion figure due to the new and higher budget deficit being predicted by LAO.

'More Crystalized Understanding' Democratic leaders in the California Legislature are generally downplaying the new budget warning, though they are not explicitly committing to protecting current and future funding levels for environmental, clean energy and climate programs. "California is more prepared than ever to navigate this latest challenge, and I remain committed to working with my Assembly, Senate and administration colleagues on a 2024 budget that protects classroom funding and prioritizes support for core health care, safety net and public safety programs," said Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas (D-Salinas) in a Dec. 1 press release.

"When the budget is due in June, we'll also benefit from a more crystallized understanding of our revenues and overall economic landscape," he added. "I'm also grateful we can rely on the state's record reserves. This, along with increased accountability and oversight of current spending, will ensure we deliver real results for all Californians." Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), chair of the Assembly Budget Committee, said in a Dec. 1 release: "Difficult budgets require difficult choices. But I am confident that the Assembly can help craft a budget that preserves classroom funding, protects our most vulnerable communities, and uplifts working families and small businesses. We can and will meet this challenge in a manner that reflects our values and addresses the most pressing issues facing our state." But leading Republicans are seizing on the LAO forecast to reiterate long-time criticism of Democrats' spending plans. "Despite all warnings that it was unsustainable, the Majority party has increased state spending by $116 billion over the last six years, nearly doubling the general fund budget in that short time," said Sen. Roger Niello (R-Fair Oaks), vice chair of the Senate Budget & Fiscal Review Committee, in a Dec. 1 press release from the party. "Republicans have cautioned that this level of spending will lead to greater deficits, and it would be more prudent to reduce the level of spending." But "California's tax-and-spend majority has joined this governor in budget decisions that are based on unrealistic revenue estimates and budgeting gimmicks," he added. "Hopefully, the majority will see it is time for a more realistic budget strategy, instead of throwing money at a laundry list of projects that sounds nice on the national television debate stage." Higher borrowing costs and reduced investment have "cooled" California's economy, the LAO analysts write. "In an effort to cool an overheated U.S. economy, the Federal Reserve has taken actions over the last two years to make borrowing more expensive and reduce money available for investment. This has slowed economic activity in a number of ways." For example, "home sales are down by about half, largely because the monthly mortgage to purchase a typical California home has gone from $3,500 to $5,400."

In addition, some effects of the Federal Reserve's actions have hit segments of the economy that have an outsized importance to California, the analysis continues. "In particular, investment in California startups and technology companies is especially sensitive to financial conditions and, as a result, has dropped significantly. For example, the number of California companies that went public (sold stock to public investors for the first time) in 2022 and 2023 is down over 80 percent from 2021. As a result, California businesses have had much less funding available to expand operations or hire new workers."

Economic 'Downturn' Apart from that, California's economy "entered a downturn" in 2022, according to the analysts.

"The number of unemployed workers in California has risen nearly 200,000 since the summer of 2022. This has resulted in a jump in the state's unemployment rate from 3.8 percent to 4.8 percent. Similarly, inflation-adjusted incomes posted five straight quarters of year-over-year declines from the first quarter of 2022 to the first quarter 2023."

And recent revenue collections show impacts from the downturn, LAO says. "The portion of income taxes collected directly from workers' paychecks was down 2 percent over the last twelve months compared to the preceding year. Sales tax collections have been essentially flat, despite above-average growth in consumer prices. The full extent of revenue weakness, however, came into full focus recently with the arrival of the postponed tax payments. With the deadline passed, collections data now show a severe revenue decline, with total income tax collections down 25 percent in 2022-23." This decline is similar to "those seen during the Great Recession and dot-com bust," the analysis adds. "While the slowdown of investment in California companies and corresponding broader economic weakness likely was a primary driver of this decline, another important factor was financial market distress in 2022."

There is also a significant risk that economic weakness could persist into 2024. "Past downturns similar to this recent episode have tended to be followed by additional weakness. For instance, an increase in the unemployment rate similar to the recent period has consistently been followed by an extended period of elevated unemployment. Similarly, in the past, years with large revenue declines typically have been followed by an additional year of lackluster revenue performance. History does not always repeat itself and may not this time. Nonetheless, there is a significant risk the current weakness could continue into next year."

Despite the gloomy outlook, the LAO analysts add that "this forecast is highly uncertain. It is entirely possible that revenues could end up $15 billion higher or lower than our forecast for 2023-24 and $30 billion higher or lower for 2024-25." -- Curt Barry (cbarry@iwpnews.com)
