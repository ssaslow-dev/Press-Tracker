NetChoice further alleges that SB 396 violates the supremacy clause of the U.S. consti- tution by conflicting with federal laws to protect children online and violates the Commerce Clause by regulating the Internet outside of the state’s borders. “AB 587 thus mandates X Corp. to speak about sen- sitive, controversial topics about which it does not wish to speak in the hopes of pressuring X Corp. to limit con- stitutionally protected content on its platform that the State apparently finds objectionable or undesirable. This violates the free speech rights granted to X Corp. under the First Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 2, of the California Constitution,” the lawsuit alleges. Signed into law earlier this year by Gov. Sarah Huck- abee Sanders (R.), the Social Media Safety Act (SB 396, Act 689) requires social media companies to verify the age of all new Arkansas users, and if an account holder is under 18, they’ll need parental permission to create an account. Social media companies subject to the law will be required to contract with a third party to verify a user’s age by uploading a digital ID such as digital copy of a driver’s license, a government-issued ID, or “any commercially reasonable age verification method.” Small social media companies that generate less than $100 million in revenue are exempt. The sponsor of AB 587, Assembly member Jesse Gabriel (D., District 46), said that if “Twitter has nothing to hide, then they should have no objection to this bill.” “AB 587 is a pure transparency measure that sim- ply requires companies to be upfront about if and how they are moderating content. It in no way requires any specific content moderation policies — which is why it passed with strong, bipartisan support,” Assembly mem- ber Gabriel said in a statement. Court Blocks Arkansas Law On Social Media Parental Consent A federal district court in Arkansas has granted NetChoice’s request to stop the implementation of a law that would require parental consent and age verification for children to create social media accounts. The law was set to go into effect Sept. 1. “We’re pleased the court sided with the First Amend- ment and stopped Arkansas’ unconstitutional law from censoring free speech online and undermining the priva- cy of Arkansans, their families and their businesses as our case proceeds,” Chris Marchese, director of the NetChoice Litigation Center, said in a statement. “We look forward to seeing the law struck down permanently.” Judge Timothy Brooks of U.S. District Court for the Western District of Arkansas granted NetChoice’s request for a preliminary injunction to prevent the law from going into effect Sept. 1. NetChoice in late June filed a lawsuit (NetChoice LLC v. Tim Griffin, in his Official Capacity as Attorney General of Arkansas, case 5:23-CV-05105) challenging the law and claiming, among other things, that the law violated the First Amendment by mandating that certain websites collect and verify sensitive personal Judge Brooks said in his Aug. 31 ruling that the state law was “unconstitutionally vague because it fails to adequately define which entities are subject to its re- quirements.” Judge Brooks also pointed out that there are certain ambiguities regarding the sort of proof that will be required to show parental consent. The law fails to de- fine what type of proof will be sufficient to demonstrate that a platform has obtained the “express consent of a parent or legal guardian,” he said. Judge Brooks also said the law burdened both adults’ and minors’ access to constitutionally protected speech. Requiring adult users to comply with age-verification requirements by providing state-approved documents to the platforms imposes “significant burdens on their abil- ity to view constitutionally protected speech,” according to the ruling. The law, according to Judge Brooks, “is not narrowly tailored to address the harms that the State contends are encountered by minors on social media.” While the state maintains that the exemptions are meant to precisely tar- get the platforms that pose the greatest danger to minors online, “the data do not support that claim,” he said. Further, “Age-gating social media platforms for adults and minors does not appear to be an effective ap- proach when, in reality, it is the content on particular plat- forms that is driving the State’s true concerns. The many exemptions in Act 689 all but nullify the State’s purposes in passing the Act and ignore the State’s expert’s view that parental oversight is what is really needed to insulate children from potential harms that lurk on the internet,” Judge Brooks added.
