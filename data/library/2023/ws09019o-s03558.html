California bill could ban Skittles, other treats that contain certain chemicals

ZACHARY ROGERS | The National Desk

SACRAMENTO, Calif. (TND) — A legislative proposal in California could ban the sale of the popular candy "Skittles" along with several other products.

The bill, named AB 418, proposes the state stop making, selling or distributing "processed foods" that "contain certain dangerous and toxic chemicals," according to a press release from assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, who introduced the bill along with assemblywoman Buffy Wicks.

Currently, there are thousands of chemicals added to food to make it last longer, taste better, and appear more enticing," the press release says. "Shockingly, most of these chemicals have never been independently evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or were last reviewed decades ago.
Instead, these chemicals have entered the nation’s food supply through a loophole in federal law—known as GRAS, or "generally recognized as safe”—that was intended to apply to common household ingredients like vinegar," the release adds. "As a result of this loophole, chemical companies have added new substances to the food supply with almost no meaningful federal oversight."

Specifically, the bill wants to prohibit "the manufacture, sale, or distribution" of any food items that contain the following:

  • Red Dye No. 3
  • Titanium Dioxide
  • Potassium Bromate
  • Brominated Vegetable Oil
  • Propyl Paraben

All of those chemicals are already banned in theEuropean Union, according to the press release, which adds that "scientific studies" have "demonstrated [the chemicals pose] significant public health harms," such as "increased risk of cancer, behavioral issues in children, harm to the reproductive system, and damage to the immune system."

While many perhaps will not immediately recognize those chemicals by name, products that contain them are very popular with consumers.

Affected products, according to TheDailyMail.com,could include "Hot Tamales" and "Sour Patch Kids" candies, "Sun Drop" soda, the ever-famous PEZ candies, Campbell's "Chicken Chunky Soup" and "Skittles."

"Skittles" containstitanium dioxide, according to its official ingredient list.The company that makes Skittlespromisedin 2016 to move towards not using it.

Gabriel notes in his release that many of the "dangerous additives" are found in processed foods "marketed to children, low-income consumers, and communities of color in the United States."

Californians shouldn’t have to worry that the food they buy in their neighborhood grocery store might be full of dangerous additives or toxic chemicals," Gabriel is quoted saying in the release. "This bill will correct for a concerning lack of federal oversight and help protect our kids, public health, and the safety of our food supply."
Why are these toxic chemicals in our food?" Susan Little, the Environmental Working Group’s Governmental Affairs Senior Advocate for California, is quoted saying in the release. "We know they are harmful and that children are likely eating more of these chemicals than adults. It makes no sense that the same products food manufacturers sell in California are sold in the EU but without these toxic chemicals. We thank Assemblymember Gabriel’s efforts to remove these toxic additives from California’s food supply."

The bill is "making its way through theCalifornia legislature," according to INSIDER. As of now, the bill has been "double referred," meaning that it first has to pass through the Assembly’s Environmental Safety and Toxic Materials Committee according to KTLA News.If passed through that committee, the bill will then reportedly head to theAssembly’s Health Committee. No hearing date has been set yet, according to reports.

Per TheDailyMail.com, if the bill goes into effect, companies of affected products would need to change their food's formula and ingredients, or they would not be able to make, distribute or sell them in California. Adding to that, the news outlet says Gabriel himself told them it's "likely" that if those companies change their products for sale in California, they'll apply those changes nationwide.

The idea here is for [companies] to change their recipes," Gabriel reportedly said. "It is unlikely they'll have one recipe in California and one in Oklahoma."

If the bill does pass, California will reportedly become the first state to have such a law.
