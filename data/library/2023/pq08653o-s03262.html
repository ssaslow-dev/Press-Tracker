NEWSOM SIGNS GHG OFFSET-DISCLOSURE BILL, BUT VETOES 'FALSE ADVERTISING' BILL

By Curt Barry

Posted October 12, 2023 California Gov. Gavin Newsom (D) has signed into law a bill requiring buyers and sellers of voluntary carbon offsets (VCOs) to disclose specific information about the offset projects and other data to help show the credits are valid, but he vetoed a measure that would have subjected company claims about VCOs to the state's False Advertising Law.

"AB 1305 is an important new tool to address the devastating impacts of climate change," said Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino) in an Oct. 9 press release, following Newsom's signing of the VCO-disclosure bill. "As we confront the climate crisis, we cannot afford to waste time with corporate greenwashing or bogus offsets that do nothing for our planet. This new law will ensure that corporations are living up to their promises when it comes to climate action and that we are protecting our planet for future generations."

Signed into law on Oct. 7, AB 1305 requires a company that is marketing or selling VCOs within the state to disclose on its website details regarding applicable carbon offset projects. These include the specific protocol used to estimate emissions reductions or removal benefits; the location of the offset project site; the project timeline; the date when the project started or will start; and the dates and quantities "when a specified quantity of emissions reductions or removals started or will start, or was modified or reversed," according to the text of the bill.

Other information the seller must disclose includes: the type of project, including whether the offsets are derived from a carbon removal, an avoided emission, or the breakdown of each category; whether the project meets any standards established by law or by a nonprofit entity; the "durability period" for any project that the seller knows or should know that the durability of the project's greenhouse gas reductions or GHG removal enhancements is less than the atmospheric lifetime of carbon dioxide emissions; and whether there is independent expert or third-party validation or verification of the project attributes.

In addition, companies must report the "emissions reduced or carbon removed on an annual basis," including details "regarding accountability measures if a project is not completed or does not meet the projected emissions reductions or removal benefits."

AB 1305 also requires purchasers of VCOs to disclose much of the same data about the credits on their websites. An Assembly floor analysis notes that the Senate amended the bill to reduce the civil penalty cap from $5,000 to $2,500 per violation, and to clarify that the disclosure requirements apply to entities marketing or selling VCOs within the state, and they do not apply to entities that do not operate within the state, do not purchase or use voluntary carbon offsets sold within the state, or do not make claims within the state.

Soaring Offset Market Gabriel's press release cites a recent Reuters report stating that the global value of the current VCO market is more than $2 billion and it is projected to grow to between $10 billion and $40 billion by 2030.

"Unfortunately, researchers have grown increasingly concerned with the impact of credits sold on the voluntary market. Indeed, a recent investigation found that greater than 90% of rainforest offset projects listed on one of the largest voluntary offset standards bodies did not represent genuine carbon reductions," the release says. "Given the absence of regulations for this market, there is a clear need for additional transparency to ensure that consumers are not misled by corporate 'greenwashing.'"

The California Chamber of Commerce and other industry groups opposed AB 1305, arguing that offsets are already subject to rigorous reporting requirements by voluntary GHG registries, according to a previous legislative analysis. "Duplicating that process [through AB 1305] is wholly unnecessary and could potentially diminish the value of the registry by imposing this requirement on staff that have not been trained in offset protocols by" the California Air Resources Board. Meanwhile, Newsom on Oct. 7 vetoed SB 390, which would have made it unlawful under the state's False Advertising Law for "a person to certify or issue a [VCO], to maintain on a registry a [VCO], or to market, make available or offer for sale, or sell a [VCO] if the person knows or should know that the greenhouse gas reductions or greenhouse gas removal enhancements of the offset project related to the voluntary carbon offset are unlikely to be quantifiable, real, and additional."

In addition, SB 390 would have made it unlawful for a person to verify a VCO if the person knows that the credit is unlikely to be valid.

Newsom said in his veto message that he supports the bill's intent to "bring greater transparency to the verification, issuance and sale of [VCOs] and to address the problem of so-called 'junk offsets.'" However, by imposing civil liability "for even unintentional mistakes about offset quality, this bill could inadvertently capture well intentioned sellers and verifiers of voluntary offsets, and risks creating significant turmoil in the market for carbon offsets, potentially even beyond California." -- Curt Barry (cbarry@iwpnews.com)
