CALIFORNIA BILL WOULD BAN FIVE TOXIC CHEMICALS IN PROCESSED FOOD PRODUCTS

By Curt Barry

Posted March 23, 2023 A California bill would ban five toxic chemicals from processed foods sold in the state, with backers claiming the move would in part help to prevent cancer, reproductive, and behavioral health problems in children -- in what they say would mark the first state action in the country to ban certain chemicals in foods.

"The goal of this bill is to protect kids and families and consumers across the state of California by banning the use of five toxic chemicals that have well-documented risks of harm," said Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino) during a March 16 online press conference. "These are chemicals that have links to cancer, reproductive issues, and particularly concerning to me as a parent, to developmental and behavioral issues in kids."

The bill, AB 418, was authored by Gabriel and Assemblywoman Buffy Wicks (D-Oakland). It would take effect Jan. 1, 2025, and bar "a person or entity from manufacturing, selling, delivering, distributing, holding, or offering for sale, in commerce a food product that contains" brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, propylparaben, red dye 3 or titanium dioxide. Supporters of the measure said during the press conference that those five chemicals are the "worst of the worst" in terms of posing health threats to consumers, and are used in many candies, baked goods and fruit sodas, often in products sold under grocery stores' own brand names.

They noted that some major health grocery store chains have voluntarily stopped selling products that contain the chemicals, and that some individual large food companies have already replaced them with less-harmful alternative ingredients.

AB 418 is being sponsored in part by the Environmental Working Group (EWG), and Scott Faber, the group's senior vice president of government affairs, noted during the event that the five chemicals have already been banned in processed foods in the European Union. He blamed the U.S. Food & Drug Administration (FDA) for failing to review and ban those and many other substances from foods in the United States.

"What we've seen, and what we've all learned, is that FDA is not making these decisions about safety -- they don't have the resources, they don't have the direction from their leadership, this is simply not a priority for the FDA," Faber said. "And, as a result, 99 percent of the chemicals that have entered commerce since 2000 have entered through a loophole that let the chemicals companies -- not the FDA -- decide what's safe."

While there are many safer substitute chemical additives available -- many of them less expensive than the five targeted by AB 418 -- food makers are choosing not to switch as a matter of "convenience -- it's inconvenient for food companies to change formulas," Faber claimed.

He added that enactment of the measure would make California the first state to ban chemicals that are "directly added to food as a food ingredient."

Wicks said she hopes that if California enacts AB 418 it will lead to other states following suit, or result in food makers deciding to replace the five chemicals with healthier substitutes in products sold nationwide.

Industry Opposition But a coalition of food makers and chemical industry groups is already lining up against AB 418, arguing in a March 13 letter that it "usurps the comprehensive food safety and approval system for these five additives and predetermines ongoing evaluations." The coalition addressed its letter to the Assembly Health Committee, which will be the first panel to consider the bill in the coming weeks.

The industry coalition is led by the Consumer Brands Association and includes the American Chemistry Council, International Association of Color Manufacturers, National Confectioners Association, California Manufacturers & Technology Association, California Grocers Association, California League of Food Producers, American Bakers Association, and California Chamber of Commerce.

It argues that in addition to FDA's chemical-review processes, California "has several laws that require removing chemicals from foods, attaching warning labels, and checking alternatives if those food additives are unsafe or expose consumers to allergies," and notes in particular the state's Proposition 65 toxic warning label law.

"All five of these additives have been thoroughly reviewed by the federal and state systems and many international scientific bodies and continue to be deemed safe," the coalition adds.

A spokeswoman for California's Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, which administers Prop. 65, says that three of the chemicals at issue are not listed under Prop. 65 -- brominated vegetable oil, propylparaben, and red dye 3. The other two -- potassium bromate and titanium dioxide in the form of "airborne, unbound particles of respirable size" -- are listed as carcinogens under Prop. 65.

The industry groups also say that several of the substances are the subjects of currently pending petitions to several government entities that were "initiated by many organizations supporting" AB 418. "Scientific regulators work through these processes and make determinations to establish recognized safe thresholds. Then, when appropriate and supported by peer-reviewed scientific evaluations, they require additional labels or removal from the market." -- Curt Barry (cbarry@iwpnews.com)
