Novel Chemical-Ban Bills Near Final Votes In California Legislature

By Curt Barry

As the California Legislature nears the end of its 2023 session next month, state lawmakers are eyeing final votes on bills that would mandate phaseouts of a wide range of toxic chemicals in food and personal-care products, along with a trio of measures targeting PFAS in several contexts.

Lawmakers are operating under a Sept. 1 deadline for bills to pass fiscal panels, while the last day of the legislative session is Sept. 14. Gov. Gavin Newsom (D) then will have until Oct. 14 to sign or veto bills that passed both chambers during the year.

Among the list of still-pending bills are at least five that would set new restrictions on toxic substances: one for cosmetics, another for food chemicals, and three governing per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) that lawmakers have touted as among the most stringent in the country.

The cosmetics bill, AB 496 by Assemblywoman Laura Friedman (D-Burbank), would take effect beginning Jan. 1, 2027 and ban "a person or entity from manufacturing, selling, delivering, holding or offering for sale in commerce any cosmetic product that contains any of the 26 intentionally added ingredients specified in this bill," a Senate floor analysis says.

It would apply to lily aldehyde, acetaldehyde, cyclohexylamine, cyclotetrasiloxane, phytonadione, sodium perborate, styrene, trichloroacetic acid, tricresyl phosphate, vinyl acetate, 2-chloracetamide, allyl isothiocyanate, anthraquinone, malachite green, oil from the seeds of Laurus nobilis L, pyrogallol, C.I. disperse blue 1, trisodium nitrilotriacetate, various boron substances, C.I. disperse blue 3, basic green 1, basic blue 7, 3(or 5)-((4-(benzylmethylamino)phenyl)azo)-1,2 -(or 1,4)dimethyl-1H-1,2,4-triazolium and its salts, basic violet 4, basic blue 3, and basic blue 9. Friedman's original proposal for AB 496 would have banned the chemicals from cosmetics beginning in 2025, but she amended it on March 8 to delay bans for about half of the target substances by two years.

The intent of the bill is "to be consistent with the approach of the EU's cosmetic regulation," according to a previous committee analysis. "All of the chemicals listed in AB 496 have been fully banned in the EU Directive and its Annexes and consequently have already been removed from cosmetic products sold in the EU."

The Senate is also poised to vote on AB 418 by Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino) and Assemblywoman Buffy Wicks (DOakland), which would ban five toxic chemicals from processed foods sold in the state, with backers claiming the move would in part help to prevent cancer, reproductive, and behavioral health problems in children -- in what they say would mark the first state action nationwide to ban certain chemicals in foods.

AB 418 would, beginning Jan. 1, 2027, bar "a person or entity from manufacturing, selling, delivering, distributing, holding, or offering for sale, in commerce a food product that contains" brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, propylparaben, red dye 3 or titanium dioxide. The bill was amended last month to delay the ban date from 2025 to 2027.

PFAS Legislation Legislators are also hoping to vote soon on three PFAS measures that would ban the chemicals in cleaning goods, artificial turf, and menstrual products.

Staff for Assemblywoman Akilah Weber (D-La Mesa) are "currently engaging with stakeholders and there may be one more round of amendments" to California's reportedly first-in-the-nation bill to phase out per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in household, industrial and institutional cleaning products beginning in 2026 before a Senate vote, according to a legislative source. "But the plan is to get the bill to the governor's desk as soon as possible."

That bill, AB 727, cleared the Senate Appropriations Committee through a consent rule after revisions that delay the ban until 2028 for floor sealers and finishes, in response to concerns from that sector.

And the Senate Appropriations Committee is expected to soon consider whether to approve AB 1423, which would ban beginning in 2024 the installation of artificial turf containing specified levels of PFAS "at certain public and private institutions, including local schools, and would completely ban the manufacture, distribution, and sale of the same type of turf," starting Jan. 1, 2024 according to an analysis by the committee.

The bill "would not affect" institutions if they "concluded the design and permitting of a covered surface, contracted for the installation of a covered surface, or purchased a covered surface on or before December 31, 2023," the analysis notes.

The measure would also require a manufacturer or installer of "covered surfaces" that proposes to sell, design or install a field with artificial turf containing intentionally added PFAS or PFAS at or above 20 parts per million (ppm) "to notify the recipient of the turf."

And beginning Jan. 1, 2026, AB 1423 would prohibit "any person or entity from manufacturing, distributing, selling, or offering for sale in the state any 'covered surface' containing intentionally added PFAS or PFAS at or above 20 ppm, effectively banning the product."

The committee analysis continues that manufacturers of "covered surfaces" would have to use the least toxic alternative when replacing PFAS and conduct their alternatives analysis "to determine the least toxic alternative in compliance with the requirements of the Green Chemistry Act."

However, the bill would repeal its prohibitions if the Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) adopts a "regulatory response governing the activity covered by it."

Another PFAS-ban bill is awaiting a vote on the Senate floor -- AB 246 by Assemblywoman Diane Papan (D-San Mateo). That measure would prohibit, beginning Jan. 1, 2025, "a person from manufacturing, distributing, selling, or offering for sale in the state any menstrual products that contain intentionally added" PFAS, or, beginning Jan. 1, 2027, "concentrations of PFAS at or above" 10 ppm, according to a Senate floor analysis.

Earlier this year, California lawmakers added specific enforcement and penalty provisions to all three PFAS bills, aiming to bolster their effectiveness in the absence of current state actions in those areas. -- Curt Barry
