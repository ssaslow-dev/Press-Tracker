California Assembly advances ballot proposal to reinstate affirmative action

Jeremy B. White

California voters are a step closer to reconsidering the state’s affirmative action ban under a constitutional amendment the Assembly passed on Wednesday.

A quarter-century after voters barred race-conscious college admissions by passing Proposition 209, Assembly Constitutional Amendment 5 would allow the electorate to repeal 209 and again allow public institutions to grant preferential treatment based on race and ethnicity in hiring, education and contracting. It passed the Assembly on an 58-9 vote, four more than the needed two-thirds majority.

Supporters said the change was long overdue, pointing to nationwide protests over racial inequity, spurred by George Floyd’s death, as a sign that California must act.

“We see how there is an urgent cry — an urgent cry for systemic change,” said Assemblymember Shirley Weber (D-San Diego). “After 25 years of quantitative and qualitative data we see can see that race-neutral solutions cannot fix problems steeped in race,” Weber added, noting how the coronavirus epidemic and resultant economic damage have disproportionately affected minorities.

Even within Democratic-dominated California, affirmative action remains a divisive issue. A similar effort in 2014, championed by Latino and African-American Democrats, ran aground in part because of a backlash from some Asian-American constituents concerned about losing coveted college spots. Some Asian-American lawmakers said they faced political retaliation as a result.

Assemblymember Evan Low (D-Campbell), who represents a heavily Asian-American district, said the feedback from his constituents was overwhelmingly against the measure, and he assailed African-American lawmakers for not reaching out to him. But in the end, he voted in favor.

“It’s about fear. It’s about race. It’s about the loss of power. Asian-Pacific Islanders, particularly the Chinese community, fear the loss,” Low said.

But while ACA 5 again drew opposition from some Asian-American groups and lawmakers, it won support from some within California’s large and diverse Asian-American community.

“Some of the opposition want you to believe Asian Americans unilaterally oppose affirmative action. That is simply not true,” said Assemblymember David Chiu (D-San Francisco).

The demographics of California’s electorate have changed markedly in the years since Proposition 209 passed, driving a broad shift to the left and helping to cement unified Democratic control over state government. Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino) argued that a new cohort of voters deserved a chance to lend their voices.

“Our generation has a different perspective on things and I think it’s time that we have the opportunity to weigh in on this issue,” Gabriel said. He added that at this point in time, with protests sweeping the nation, “it would be extraordinarily shortsighted of us and really tone-deaf for us not to permit voters to have an opportunity to weigh in on this.”

The measure must still pass the California Senate if it is to go before voters. Lawmakers will need to move fast if voters are to weigh in this year: June 25 is the deadline for the Legislature to place measures on the November ballot, though lawmakers have some leeway to alter that timeline.
