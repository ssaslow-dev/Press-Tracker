California - Bill aimed at homeless vetoed - Gov. Newsom says no to legislation aimed at better
tracking the funds spent for those without homes
October 2, 2020 | Orange County Register, The (Santa Ana, CA)
Author: By Teri Sforza tsforza@scng.com Section: News 684 Words
Page: 3
OpenURL Link

California has poured hundreds of millions of dollars into battling homelessness, and yet the number of people living
on the streets shot up 16% last year.
A bill by Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, D-Woodland Hills, aimed to better understand how those dollars are spent —
and zero in on what works and what doesn’t — by creating a uniform data-collection system that could be used to
craft better outcomes. It sailed through the Legislature without a single no vote and landed on the governor’s desk
in September with 20 bipartisan co-authors, awaiting the signature that would make it law.
On Tuesday, Gov. Gavin Newsom vetoed it.
“My Administration is exploring ways to increase transparency in our homeless assistance programs and improve
the efficiency in the delivery of those services to those who most need the help,” Newsom said in his veto message.
“This is important work, and we are dedicated to this effort. Unfortunately, the requirements of this measure are
duplicative of existing requirements and would create additional, unnecessary data collection costs.”
Gabriel was surprised and disappointed by the governor’s veto of Assembly Bill 2746. Newsom’s objection that the
bill would add data collection costs doesn’t make sense to him.
“You’re going to spend hundreds of millions of dollars on these programs, but object to spending a couple of
hundred thousand to gather data to see if they’re working? A lot of people would agree that’s a prudent and wise
investment,” he said.
“We didn’t want to do anything duplicative, and we actually said, ‘If you have existing reporting that gives the
information we’re seeking, you can submit that,’ “ he said. “We’re trying to determine what
are these costs, what are they accomplishing, how do these programs stack up to others? It’s this huge challenge.
Once we have some uniform data, we can have a better sense of where to target our money.”
According to legislative analyses, the bill would have mandated enhanced accountability and transparency by
creating a system of uniform reporting requirements across a broad set of state-funded homelessness programs.
That data would have been available to the Legislature, the media and the general public, allowing for stronger
oversight of state and local homelessness programs and better insight into the comparative effectiveness of various
programs and strategies, allowing officials to better use data to craft policy responses.
Comments in a Senate Committee on Human Services analysis flagged the concerns Newsom ultimately expressed:
“These programs currently face extensive reporting requirements that in many ways speak to information sought
by the author’s office.
It is possible that this bill will not result in additional reporting on each of these programs due to provisions allowing
funding recipients to utilize existing reports to meet reporting requirements imposed by this bill and to report
certain data as unfeasible to collect.”
Gabriel vowed to continue the push for stronger accountability and “data-driven policymaking.”

There’s extreme frustration in the community, and in the Legislature, about the lack of progress on homelessness,
Gabriel said.
“People in Los Angeles city and county have voted twice to tax themselves to fix this problem,” he said. “They said,
‘Take more of my money and please, please, do something to help the people suffering on our streets.’ Step one is
to get better data on what’s working and what isn’t to ensure that resources are being spent in the most efficient
and effective way possible.”
New reporting would have been required for those receiving funds from three programs: the homeless services
piece of Work Opportunity and Responsibility to Kids (CalWORKs), an enormous, $7.8 billion program that provides
cash aid to supplement household incomes; the Housing and Disability Advocacy Program, which offers disabled
homeless people help applying for benefit programs as well as housing support, and has received $90 million in
state and local funding a year over recent years; and Whole Person Care pilot programs that allow local
governments, health and hospital authorities to integrate care for Medi-Cal beneficiaries “who have been identified
as high users of multiple systems and continue to have poor health outcomes.” Many target those with mental
illness.
The state has invested more than $120 million in the programs.
