CapitolBeat by Jason Fox Election Fallout Plus: More Tax on Services, Tax Credit Bills W With the June primary election completed, campaigns across California will heat up now that the field of candidates has been narrowed. While there were no big surprises, there were a number of key races at the state and federal level that will impact California’s political landscape—and possibly the balance of power in Washington, D.C. As expected, Gavin Newsom was the top candidate in the governor’s race. The major focus was who would take the second spot to move to the November general election. John Cox, the leading Republican candidate, was able to capitalize on an endorsement from President Trump and move ahead of Democrat contenders Antonio Villaragosa and John Chiang. CPA and CalCPA member Fiona Ma took first place in the treasurer’s race and will face Republican Greg Conlon, also a CPA. Betty Yee and Xavier Becerra both moved to the general election and will likely be re-elected controller and attorney general, respectively. Orange County voters recalled Sen. Newman over his vote in support of the gas tax increase. Republican, and former Assemblymember Ling Ling Chang, was elected to replace him. With the swap from a Democrat to a Republican, this likely blocks the Democrat’s two-thirds supermajority in the Senate. The Democrats had used that supermajority to pass legislation that would enact new taxes, pass the state budget and submit constitutional amendments to the ballot without input from Republicans. In the Assembly Luz Maria Rivas (D) Jesse Gabriel (D) won special elections to fill vacant seats. For the primary races, most elections went as expected, except for Assemble District 76 in San Diego County. Republican Rocky Chavez left that seat to run for Congress and two Democrats were able to lock out Republicans from retaining the seat in November. On the federal level, Sen. Diane Feinstein cruised to the top spot for the U.S. Senate. Former Senate President pro Tempore Kevin De Léon, who is positioning himself as the progressive challenger to the moderate Feinstein, came in a distant second place. National political pundits speculated that California’s “top two primary” could result in democratic candidates splitting the vote and being shut out of the targeted congressional seats to strip control of the U.S. House of Representatives from the Republicans. That didn’t happen and those seats will be the focus of heavy campaigning this fall by Democrats. Sales Tax on Services A scheduled discussion in June on Senate Bill 993 (Hertzberg), which would enact a new 3 percent sales tax on business services and reduce the sales tax on goods by 2 percent, was postponed until later this summer. Legislative leaders plan to bring stakeholders together to provide additional information about the impact of a broad professional services tax on business activity. CalCPA and other business groups have opposed this measure and past services tax efforts; however, the topic continues to simmer. While it is unlikely something will move forward in the Legislature this year, with a new governor who will likely be more progressive taking office next year, there is a threat of tax reform—including a services tax—being discussed. CalCPA will continue to ensure CPAs have a seat at the table and provide the profession’s input. ‘Charitable’ Work-arounds Following the new federal tax reform, many high tax states, like California, continue to explore options for managing the impact on their taxpayers. Specifically, the $10,000 cap on state and local tax (SALT) deductions has inspired the introduction of Senate Bill 227 (De Léon) and Assembly Bill 2217 (Burke), which aim to circumvent this limit. Both bills have received bipartisan support and propose state sponsored charities that will collect taxpayer dollars in the form of a charitable contribution, rather than a tax payment, in exchange for a state tax credit. The expectation is that the taxpayer could then deduct the contribution from their federal taxes, which is not limited by the new SALT cap. SB 227 creates the California Excellence Fund, a state established charity, in exchange for a tax credit equal to 85 percent of the contribution. AB 2217 similarly allows a taxpayer to donate to a school district, which in turn funnels a portion of the money to the Treasurer’s office, which will them send the money to the general fund. In exchange, the treasurer provides the school district with credits and the district provides those credits to the donating taxpayer. The taxpayer may then claim a tax credit worth 80 percent of the face value of their initial contribution. Proposals such as these, which have passed in New York, Connecticut and New Jersey, raise many questions that would need to be addressed before tax practitioners could consider this option for their clients. Most importantly, it is unclear whether the IRS will allow these types of deductions. The IRS said it will be issuing specific guidance on how these types of credits will be treated and said “taxpayers should be mindful that federal law controls the proper characterization of payments for federal income tax purposes.” Many anticipate the IRS would not be willing to simply allow these types of deductions wholesale. Still, California leaders continue to explore options to skirt perceived negative impacts of the federal tax laws. This will continue to be a topic of discussion in 2018 and something tax practitioners should be mindful of when working with their clients in 2019. Jason Fox is CalCPA’s director of legislation.
