San Fernando Valley voters face key choices in Tuesday’s election. Here’s a roundup of local races

By Elizabeth Chou

Local San Fernando Valley voters going to the polls during Tuesday’s general election will get a taste of the midterm fight playing out between the two major political parties on the national stage through a hotly contested race between the 25th district’s Republican congressman and his Democratic challenger.

But also on the ballot are more locally consequential matters of whether to approve a tax measure or questions of who is the better person to represent them at the state Legislature and in their city.

The 25th Congressional District that touches the northern edge of the San Fernando Valley features one of the most highly watched match-up. The race between Democratic challenger Katie Hill and Republican incumbent Steve Knight has come to represent a bid by the Democratic party to capture one more seat to chip away at the Republican’s control of the U.S. House of Representatives.

Knight, 51, is former member of the Palmdale City Council and the California Legislature, with experiencing serving in the Army and the Los Angeles Police Department. He is seeking a third term in Congress.

This is the first run for office for Hill, 31, who previously led PATH, a homeless-services nonprofit. She is hoping to benefit from the shift in the district from Republican-leaning to one in which the demographics show a narrow edge on the side of registered Democratic voters.

Other Congressional races lined up for Tuesday include a slate of Democratic incumbents facing off against mostly lesser known Republic foes: They include Tony Cardenas defending his northeast San Fernando Valley’s 29th District seat against Republican Benito Bernal, an education transportation supervisor; Adam Schiff seeking re-election in a race against challenger Johnny J. Nalbandian, a food industry businessman, in the 28th District, which touches the east San Fernando Valley; Julia Brownley seeking retain her seat in a district on the western edge of the Valley against challenger Antonio Sabato, Jr., who is best known for his acting work, in the 26th District. Congressman Brad Sherman who represents the western swathe of the Valley is facing a challenge from Republican Mark Reed, a rancher and businessman.

The state Assembly races in the San Fernando Valley have shaken out into match-ups between Democrats and Republicans, even though California’s system advances the top two vote-getters in the June primary, no matter their party affiliation. This has given Valley voters an option to consider Republican candidates in a state in which the Legislature is controlled by Democrats.

But the equation is reversed in one of the most competitive state races in the San Fernando Valley, on its the northern edge. The 38th Assembly District race between Republican incumbent Dante Acosta challenger Christy Smith, a Newhall school board member, is taking place in a district where there is a closer distribution between the two parties. The pair were also foes in 2016, with Smith coming close to beating Acosta at the time.

Republicans are hoping to provide an alternative option in three other Assembly races in which Democrats have a stronger hold. In the 39th District, Ricardo Benitez, a business owner, is offering a Republican take in his challenge to freshly minted Assemblywoman Luz Rivas, who elected in June to finish a term that was vacated by Raul Bocanegra amid sexual harassment allegations.

College student Justin Clark, a Republican from Calabasas has his sights on the 45th District Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel‘s seat. He faces a steep battle, as Gabriel has support from local and state political leaders and has already been assigned a leadership role in the Assembly.

Roxanne Beckford Hoge is the Republican challenger to 46th District, where Assemblyman Adrin Nazarian seeking a third two-year term.

Also of interest in the eastern San Fernando Valley is the race between 44th District Assemblywoman Jacqui Irwin, a Democrat, and Republican challenger Ronda Baldwin-Kennedy, an attorney and mother.

Democratic Assembly members in two Valley seats are running unopposed. The 43rd District‘s Laura Friedman, who represents an area that includes Glendale, and the 50th District‘s Richard Bloom, who represents a portion of the eastern San Fernando Valley, have another two-year term guaranteed to them.

A number of municipal elections in the Valley feature tax measures, and City Council races.

In the city of San Fernando, Measure A asks voters to extend a temporary half percent sales tax approved in 2013 that raised the total sales tax in the city to 10 percent. With the added tax sunsetting in 2020, city officials are asking voters to keep it going indefinitely in order to pay for “essential city services.” If passed, voters would need to put a measure on a future ballot to put an end to the tax.

The annual revenue generated by the half percent sales tax hovers around $2.5 million. The city has an annual budget of just under $20 million, and city officials say that the loss of the extra financial padding would be felt, which is why they are going to voters again.

The half percent sales tax was put on the ballot in 2013 in response to a city-declared “fiscal emergency,” which resulted in layoffs, reductions in employee benefits, the discontinuation of retiree medical benefits for new employees, and other budget cuts.

Since 2013, Measure A has brought in a total of $10.78 million. Last year, the tax brought in $2.6 million, according to the city, but with the closure of one of the city’s largest retailers, Sam’s Club, in January, this year’s revenue is anticipated to dip down to $2.4 million.

The city has used the sales tax revenue to whittle away at its general fund deficit from $5.7 million to just under $500,000 million. Because the existing sales tax is temporary, city officials say they try to use the funds on mostly one-time costs, that do not necessarily repeat in the next year, such as buying police vehicles, making technological upgrades to its computer networks, paying for a median improvement project and improve park facilities.

While the city has weathered many of its fiscal challenges, officials say they anticipate the loss of the funds will result in a “significant gap in revenues” just as the city is about to get back onto its feet financially. The continued revenue is necessary to strengthen the city’s “rainy day” reserves, replace public safety and public works vehicles and equipment, create a buffer for future economic downturns or an unforeseen lawsuit or claims, and to pave residential streets, city officials say. The use of the revenue for city operational costs would be unrestricted.

Voters will also be picking council members to serve four-year terms in three of the San Fernando City Council’s five seats. Out of the six people who have thrown their hats into the ring, three are incumbents. They are Sylvia Ballin, a retired analyst for the Metropolitan Water District who is seeking a third term; Antonio G. Lopez, a director of an architecture firm who is also hoping for a third term; and Jaime Soto, a college history professor who is making a bid for a second term. The three challengers are Yvonne Mejia Pena, a planning and education commissioner who works in compliance and risk management; Hector Pacheco, an attorney focusing on business and entertainment law; and David Bernal, a union steward and AT&T technician.

Glendale is another city where voters will be considering a sales tax. Measure S that proposes to add a ¾-percent sales tax on top of the current 9-1/2 percent, to pay for city services. The tax is anticipated to raise about $30 million a year in sales tax revenue.

Measure P in in Burbank also calls for a ¾-percent sales tax increase. If approved, tax could generate about $20 million a year for police, fire stations, street repairs, parks and recreation and libraries. The city is forecasting a funding shortfall of more than $27 million a year, comprised of a $9.5 million operating deficit and $18 million in infrastructure needs. Three seats on the Calabasas City Council are on the ballot, and there are four candidates. They include three incumbents: David J. Shapiro, the mayor pro tem; James R. Bozajian, a councilman; Mary Sue Maurer, also a member of the council and an environmental educator. Vahid Naziri, an attorney and broker, is making a bid to unseat one of them.

In Agoura Hills, three seats are available, and five candidates are running. They are Chris Anstead, a biotech account executive; Deborah Klein Lopez, a community volunteer; Denis Weber, a council member and banker; Harry Schwarz, a manager and council member; and Jan Gerstel, a business owner.
