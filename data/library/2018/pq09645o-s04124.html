Election 2018: Jesse Gabriel holding onto seat in race for West San Fernando Valley’s 45th District Assembly seat

By Elizabeth Chou

A Democratic incumbent is outpacing a Republican college student in early election results released Tuesday for a western San Fernando Valley district that stretches from Chatsworth to Calabasas.

The 45th district Assembly race is between Cal State University of Northridge political science major Justin Clark, and constitutional rights attorney Jesse Gabriel, who was elected to represent the district in a June special election. Gabriel was comfortably ahead of Clark, and appeared headed to a regular two-year term beginning Dec. 3. The pair also faced off previously in the June special election. Gabriel won that election, while Clark, the sole Republican, in the race garnering a quarter of the votes in the district.

* See the latest election results Gabriel, 37, has sued the Trump administration in two cases aimed at continuing protections under President Barack Obama’s Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals policy for people who illegally immigrated to the United States at a young age. His priorities goals include increasing access to a good education and protecting the environment. Gabriel is completing the remainder of the 45th district term that was left unfinished when the area’s previous representative, Matt Dababneh, stepped down following allegations of sexual misconduct. Shortly after assuming office, he was assigned a leadership role as an assistant whip for the Democratic majority.

Clark, a 19-year-old Calabasas resident who works at a local ice cream shop, says his goals if elected include tackling waste and corruption in state government.

While he is likely to lose the race, Clark said Tuesday evening that he would be “very proud” if his campaign garners similar results as in the primary election, or better. In the last election, the percentage of votes he brought in “easily beat Republican voter registration,” he said.

This was due to “our message of bipartisanship and focusing on the issues that matter most to local residents,” he said. “We put people ahead of politics. My only regret is that we lacked the funding to spread our positive message further then we did.”

He said that while his opponent was able to send out several mailers to voters, he and his campaign relied on walking neighborhoods and knocking on doors.

He said the “traditional avenues” for soliciting donations are often available to Republican candidates in California, because some would-be donors may feel that such candidates are not likely to win or they feel the party’s leadership is not strong enough.

Clark networked and went to family members for donations, but he recently got a boost from the Los Angeles Police Protective League, a union that represents rank-and-file police officers. The organization gave his campaign $4,400, the maximum that such an organization is allowed to give, he said.

Clark said that while going door-to-door in a Democratic-leaning district, he found that there were those who did not identify with his party, but who nevertheless said they would vote for him.

“I find that interesting,” he said. “But you can’t knock on every door in the district.”

The district Gabriel and Clark are seeking to represent includes the city of Calabasas, and the Los Angeles city communities of Chatsworth, West Hills, Canoga Park, Winnetka, Reseda and Northridge.
