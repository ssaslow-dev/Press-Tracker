These 11 big stories happened in and around the San Fernando Valley in 2018

It’s been said the San Fernando Valley is that bucolic place — the L.A. suburb that gets short shrift and little attention from the moving and shaking, the hustle and bustle, over the hill in DTLA and the westside.

That may be true, but don’t let that fool you into thinking there’s little news or not much happening in this place of early 2 million people. Sure, the Valley’s got its slow days. We’ve had them here in the newsroom. But in the bigger picture, the place the Kardashians call home, where “all the vampires walkin’ through the Valley move west down Ventura Boulevard”

(thanks, Tom Petty. We love you … ) and where a journey on the 405 or the 101 can, let’s just say, test the inner depths of your soul, stuff happens. After all, it’s a friendly reminder that if the San Fernando Valley were a city, it would be the fifth largest in the United States of America, according to one recent report.

That means each year, stuff happens. Another word for what happens also starts with an s. But this is a family newspaper, so we’ll leave that word alone. Still, suffice to say, the year 2018 was certainly not lacking for news, some of which we wish to the depths of our hearts we never had to report. There was tragedy beyond our worst nightmares, triumph, drama, yelling, loss, victory … the worst and best of the human condition played itself out sometimes in stunningly short periods of time. So stunning, in fact, we’re still processing it.

So, here’s a look back at 2018’s moments, issues, stories, that made us think, cry, smile, mad, happy ….

Mass shooting at Borderline Bar & Grill It was a college dance night at a bar in one of the safest American towns we know of. And then, a sick man walked into the Borderline Bar & Grill in Thousand Oaks on Nov. 7, 2018, and turned it into hell. When his shooting was over, 12 were dead, including a Ventura County Sheriff’s sergeant. So was the shooter. Many others were injured, and a community was scarred forever. It is true, Thousand Oaks is not the San Fernando Valley. But this singular event just up the 101 was close enough to rattle an entire world, and the communities close to the epicenter of this tragedy. It could not go unspoken here, in our memory of 2018.

Woolsey fire death and destruction As grieving families and friends of the Borderline shooting victims were coming and going from the area’s family reunification center, smoke was rising. You could see it. You could smell it. You could hear fire engines in the distance. It wasn’t enough that an unspeakable tragedy had occurred the night before. Now, fire was spreading. Two, in fact. But the big one, what became the Woolsey fire, would grow into a giant.

Starting in Simi hills of Ventura County, it wouldn’t take long before Woolsey would grow out of control and do serious damage in Ventura and LA counties. That was Nov. 8. By Nov. 21, it was 100 percent contained. But by then it had led to three deaths, destroyed 1,643 structures, damaged 364 others and charred 96,949 acres, the Fire Department said.

Thousands had been evacuated, affecting communities across Ventura and LA Counties, including the western edge of the San Fernando Valley, in communities like West Hills and Calabasas.

Officials are still seeking a cause, so stay tuned in 2019.

Politics/#MeToo meltdowns That same week in early November, the week of the shooting and when the fires broke out, things started off busy enough in the San Fernando Valley.

The Valley played a role in Southern California Democratic candidates’ sweeping success in high-profile elections for Congress and the California Legislature. Katie Hill, 31, a homeless-services non-profit executive, was elected over Rep. Steve Knight, R-Palmdale, to represent the House district that includes the northern edge of the Valley.

Christy Smith, a Newhall school board member, unseated Dante Acosta, R-Santa Clarita, in the state Assembly district covering much of the same area as Hill’s.

The Valley felt the fallout from the #MeToo scandals that roiled California politics — and other industries. Special elections installed new state Assembly members Luz Rivas, D-North Hollywood, and Jesse Gabriel, D-Encino, to replace Raul Bocanegra and Matt Dababneh, Democrats who resigned amid sexual-misconduct scandals. State Sen. Bob Hertzberg, DVan Nuys, was ordered by Senate leaders to cut out his trademark hugging after women complained the hugs were unwanted.

Here’s what’s in store for 2019 in the Valley LAUSD teachers set a strike date After nearly two years of getting nowhere in negotiations, 2018 was the year that LA public school teachers and their district couldn’t agree on, well, anything.

Stymied over class size, salary and the rise of charter schools, and bitterly mistrustful of one another, they couldn’t even agree that they agreed. That was clear as late as December, when the district’s superintendent said the two sides had found agreement on a 6 percent raise for teachers and the union’s president flatly denied it.

So, here we are, at the end of 2018, going into 2019, when on Jan. 10, teachers are set to go on strike for the first time in nearly 30 years. 2018, apparently, paved the way.

Homelessness didn’t go away 2018 was the year when efforts to really house homeless people in L.A. ran into vocal opposition in all quarters of the city.

It was clear that in the San Fernando Valley, this debate was just as robust. As City Councilmembers and LA County officials moved to find shelter, neighborhoods raised concerns over safety and quality-of-life issues. In the meantime, even as communities complain about homelessness, the San Fernando Valley has just one year-round homeless shelter at the moment to address the immediate needs of the thousands who are living outdoors or in vehicles.

Shelters in Venice, downtown Los Angeles, Watts and South Los Angeles have already been approved or built, in response to a call by Los Angeles Mayor Eric Garcetti that at least one temporary shelter be set up in each of the city’s 15 council districts.

All that said, there are those in local communities who are open to living near a shelter.

Stay tuned in 2019.

$119.5 million settlement deal over Porter Ranch gas leak The ripple effects of the giant natural gas leak at SoCal Gas’ Aliso Canyon facility near Porter Ranch three years ago continued be felt in 2018.

Perhaps the biggest ripple was SoCalGas’ $119.5 million settlement over the leak.

Under the deal, announced in August, SoCal Gas will pay out $119.5 million, covering claims lodged against the company over the 2015 gas leak at its Aliso Canyon natural gas facility near Porter Ranch, the company said.

California Attorney General Xavier Becerra, Los Angeles Mayor Eric Garcetti, City Councilman Mitch Englander, City Attorney Mike Feuer, Supervisor Kathryn Barger and other officials expressed satisfaction with the settlement, which came as the result of lawsuits brought by the city and county of Los Angeles and the state.

Under the terms, SoCalGas also will reimburse city, county and state governments for costs associated with their response to the leak, including the costs of hundreds of evacuations and state and local air quality monitoring studies.

But stay tuned. Some residents continue to be unhappy with the deal and dubious about agency actions in the wake of the leak, and lawsuits for related damages haven’t gone away.

Beck retires. Moore in at LAPD Los Angeles police Chief Michel Moore, a 36-year LAPD veteran, was sworn in this past June following the retirement of Charlie Beck, who served nine years as the city’s top cop.

Moore is no stranger to the San Fernando Valley. In fact, he forged much of his rise in the force in the area, with tenure as the deputy chief of the LAPD’s Valley Bureau for about four years.

Moore was widely seen as the mayor’s pick because he would continue pushing the reform efforts Beck has pursued, including an emphasis on community policing and a focus on de-escalation tactics for officers.

He’s spent much time connecting with communities across the city, including a recent community meeting in Van Nuys, where he touted the area’s command staff and talked about the need to improve department technology and reiterated his Valley connections.

Valley LA City Councilman calls it quits Mitch Englander decided to step down from the LA City Council, moving on to what he calls a job of a “lifetime” with global sports and entertainment firm Oak View Group.

Englander, even as the lone Republican on the non-partisan council, forged alliances and policy through compromise and leveraging community sentiment. After a long career in city politics, his last day is Jan. 1, 2019. But that, of course, leave two years on his term, which ends Dec. 13, 2020.

Who will take over in the meantime? Englander’s former boss, former 12th District Councilman Greig Smith, has already been picked to fill-in until a permanent council person is elected. A June 4 special election has been set to tap a permanent replacement to fill out the term.

The district includes much of the northwest San Fernando Valley.

LA says yes to short-term rental regulated The City Council in December approved regulations, set to be effective July 1, 2019, that will limit short-term hosts who list their homes on sites such as Airbnb, to only renting out rooms in their primary residence for no more than 120 nights a year.

Hosts will be able to request extensions, but that will require petitioning for the right to do so, and paying more fees.

The action was a big deal, after a long time going without such regulations as such home-sharing arrangements grew.

Postal worker dies from relentless Woodland Hills heat Peggy Frank, 63, of North Hills was found unresponsive while seated in a non-air-conditioned postal truck in the 4800 block of Calderon Road in Woodland Hills.

Frank, a veteran postal worker, was working on a 117-degree July day, on which temperatures shattered the previous record of 106.

Employees at the Woodland Hills Post Office remembered Frank, a dog lover who had some 28 years working at the post office, as always upbeat and positive despite her health challenges.

By September, it was confirmed that heat killed Frank, and that prompted U.S. Rep. Tony Cardenas, D-Panorama City, to seek safety measures that would stop something similar from happening again.

The Van Nuys Neighborhood Council, an advisory body that advocates for its community with Los Angeles City Hall, voted to request that all U.S. Postal Service vehicles be equipped with air conditioning “to prevent dangerous health conditions for the employees delivering the mail.”

Burbank’s Disney getting bigger Looks like Disney is getting bigger. In July, Disney and 21st Century Fox shareholders approved a $71.3 billion merger deal between the two companies, paving the way for the takeover in 2019.

Burbank-based Disney is expected to complete its takeover of Fox’s movie and TV operations in the spring. That will not only make the Mouse House the largest by far of the five remaining major studios, but could profoundly affect employment in the creative, executive and blue collar ranks of the industry, though how many jobs will be created or lost remains unseen.

The merger is also a key step toward Disney+, the new streaming service the conglomerate hopes to have up-and-running by the end of the year. Not only do the Fox assets mean “X-Men” and “Fantastic Four” can return to their rightful cinematic home at Disney’s Marvel Studios, but shows spun off of everything from James Cameron’s “Avatar” to “The Simpsons” can be deployed against Netflix, which Disney will be the first traditional entertainment giant to challenge.
