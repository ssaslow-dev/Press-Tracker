California’s ‘orphaned’ ballot measures

By WILL MCCARTHY, JEREMY B. WHITE and NICOLE NORMAN

LEFT BEHIND: Over the last two and a half years, California lawmakers spent countless hours drafting, debating and voting on a group of government reform ballot measures to put before voters. The same energy has not been put toward campaigns to pass them.

Proposition 5, which would change how successors are chosen in recalls, was set for the ballot way back in August 2024. Proposition 4, which would allow for the public financing of campaigns, landed in October 2025. Proposition 2, which would double the amount the state can deposit into its reserves, was rushed to the November ballot earlier this spring.

All aim to reform a somewhat wonky aspect of how government operates, and none of them are easily explainable to voters in a single sentence. And yet each of them have a limited, or non-existent public presence and campaign.

“You get these orphaned initiatives,” said Thad Kousser, a professor of political science at the University of San Diego. “So voters are left with very few bits of information on what’s going to be the most crowded ballot in a decade.”

The recall measure’s travails underscore how quickly a political window can close. In the aftermath of the failed attempt to recall Gov. Gavin Newsom, triumphant Democrats said they were determined to change a system that would allow a challenger — like MAGA tribune Larry Elder — to become governor with fewer votes than the number cast against a recall. Secretary of State Shirley Weber vowed action, and election committees revved up.

But the momentum soon ebbed. Policymakers couldn’t agree on the changes. Polls showed voters were tepid on changing a process that many see as an important check on elected officials. By the time former State Sen. Josh Newman finally got his measure through, the Newsom recall — and the sense of urgency it engendered — were distant memories.

A spokesperson for the Prop 5 campaign did not reply to requests for comment, although Newman said in a recent interview that he was “not optimistic” about the recall reform measure’s chances.

Historically, the vast majority of measures placed on the ballot by the legislature have won — a full 69% between 1910 and 2025. In the past, that outcome has been largely a function of how many votes it takes for lawmakers to put measures before voters, a two-thirds majority of both houses traditionally meaning that the issue was highly popular.

But polling for the three government reform measures is scattershot. A September Public Policy Institute of California poll had Prop 4 and Prop 5 with only 42% and 35% support, respectively, while Prop 2 had the support of 60% of voters (a later Berkeley IGS poll put Prop 4’s support at 35% for, 35% against, and 30% undecided).

Prop 5 does not appear to have any financial backing or a campaign committee organized to pass the measure. The committee for Prop 4 has a single, $235,000 contribution from Movement Voter PAC, a progressive organizing group. Prop 2’s committee received a $150,000 contribution from Google last week.

Assemblymember Alex Lee, one of the coauthors of the legislation that placed Prop 4 on the ballot, said the measure was in a “good place” and that undecided voters are “ready to be persuaded.” The Prop 4 campaign has begun holding public events and is planning a bus tour at some point this fall.

“I knew the other marquee efforts would absorb the media attention,” Lee said. “Really it’s just about the voter education campaign.”

Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, author of Prop 2, said you likely won’t see large ad spends or public appearances to drum up support for expanding the state’s budget reserves. Given the solid polling and what he called the “common sense wisdom” of the measure, he’s confident in its passage.

Meant to shield the state’s budget from the volatility of the stock market, the measure has the support of a wide range of groups, from the California Chamber of Commerce to SEIU. Even so, Gabriel said he expected a “lean campaign” that will “rely a lot on member-to-member communication.”

Each of the government reform measures share at least one key advantage: None face organized opposition.

“Its not like we have to raise X millions of dollars,” Lee said.

A highly subjective ranking of the ballot measures — past and future, certain and possible — getting our attention this week.

1. Prop 37: The campaign for a measure to provide loans to help middle-income families purchase homes contributed $300,000 to the California Democratic Party last week, apparently viewing the measure in good financial standing.

2. Prop 38: The No on Prop 38 campaign is operating with a single six-figure check as it tries to run a campaign against billionaire Gary Michelson’s immunology bond ballot measure. The $3,000 contribution comes from Robert Kaplan, the director of research at the Stanford School of Medicine Clinical Excellence Research Center. Michelson has contributed over $10 million to the initiative, which has come under criticism in recent weeks.

3. Prop 45: A group of Republican county organizations came out against Prop 45 last week, even as the Yes campaign faced scrutiny for donations to Republican Assemblymember Carl DeMaio’s Reform California organization. The Yes campaign, meanwhile, released a report showing what it says would be the “significant economic benefits” of Prop 45, including adding 20,000 homes a year.

4. Fund scientific research (2028): Newsom climbed the Golden Gate Bridge with State Sen. Scott Wiener and a camera crew to sign a $7.5 billion research bond that aims to combat federal funding cuts. The measure will be on the ballot in 2028. “I’m proud to sign a bond to invest in the future of the state of California,” Newsom said in a video from the bridge.

5. Prop 40: Heavily outspent and facing enormous establishment resistance, the wealth tax campaign is hoping America’s most prominent progressive can carry it to victory. Sen. Bernie Sanders is rallying for Prop 40 in Los Angeles today after swinging through San Francisco on Saturday, where he described the campaign as a Democrat-defining battle over “the most important ballot initiative in the country.”

6. Prop 39: DeMaio is continuing on his “Barbecue, Beer, & Ballots” tour through California, attending his tenth barbecue of the last week in Ramona today as the campaign looks to help pass Prop 39. As Will wrote for POLITICO Magazine last week, DeMaio’s grassroots army has become a major factor in his rise in state politics.

7. Measure ER (Los Angeles County): A one-half cent sales tax narrowly approved by Los Angeles County voters in June took effect on Oct. 1. But it could be a while before the $1 billion it is expected to generate each year for health care can actually be spent. A lawsuit, brought by the Libertarian Party of LA County, will have to play out in LA County Superior Court before the proceeds can be used.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK — Californians for Voting Rights, one of the committees organized to defeat Prop 39, is launching a pair of advertisements in the Bay Area hammering on the “consequences voters will face” should the measure pass. In the first spot, the No campaign characterizes Prop 39 as a “MAGA power grab” led by President Donald Trump. The second warns people of longer lines in order to vote. The seven-figure buy is the committee’s first time on air.

HE’S BACK: Former Gov. Jerry Brown will tonight join journalist Miriam Pawel, the author of nonfiction works about the Brown family and Cesar Chavez, for a conversation launching her latest book: “California Dreams: The Making and Remaking of the University of California.” The discussion will “explore the history of the University of California, its relationship with California’s political and social life, and the continuing importance of public higher education,” and the event will run from 6 p.m. to 8 p.m. at the California State Library. The retired Brown’s appearance is his latest since he visited the state Capitol during end-of-session proceedings and penned a call for global nuclear disarmament. Register here.

THE BOOGEYMAN: Democrats’ top Senate super PAC is flooding the airwaves with ads hitting Republicans on Jeffrey Epstein, the late financier and convicted sex offender. They have data to back it up, POLITICO’s Andrew Howard reports.

Internal polling of over 4,500 voters from Senate Majority PAC, conducted by the Democratic firm Blue Rose Research late last week and shared with POLITICO, found that a 44% plurality of voters — including the same percentage of swing voters — think the Epstein issue is “serious” and that Republicans should be held responsible.

And while nothing trumps affordability as the issue du jour on the campaign trail this fall, state-level research from SMP found that the Epstein ads can shift races three to six points in Democrats’ direction.
