Kamala Harris’ wasted year

DRIVING THE DAY — Kamala Harris has kept the door open to running for president again in 2028. What she failed to do over the past year, even one-time supporters and allies of the former vice president say, is give voters a reason to reconsider her if she does.

For Harris, according to interviews with nearly 20 Democratic Party donors and strategists in California and across the country, it was a missed opportunity to redefine herself — and ultimately a wasted year.

“She totally blew it,” said Doug Herman, who was a lead mail strategist for Barack Obama‘s 2008 and 2012 campaigns. “She hasn’t gotten into the mix.”

Harris has offered voters almost nothing in the way of specifics about how she would remake a Democratic Party hungry for change after another term of Donald Trump. She hasn’t spoken to how she would expand her own appeal to the voters who rejected her and she remains as reticent of media as ever, continuing to favor appearances before friendly audiences and at times drawing fire for shutting out news organizations altogether. She has entirely sidestepped the ideological civil war consuming the Democrats and has acted more like a party elder at a time when voters and donors are demanding a break with the past.

“I don’t see anything different myself. It seems like more of the same to me,” said Jim Hodges, the former governor of South Carolina, the first state on the Democratic nominating calendar. “Right now Democratic primary voters … are very anxious about a Trump acolyte being in the White House in 2028. And they want to select someone who they think has the best chance to win, but who also meets these requisites: fresh, new. I hear a lot of talk about that — ‘we don’t need recycled candidates.’”

DEBATE NIGHT — Angelenos have their one and only chance to watch the two candidates for Los Angeles mayor debate on TV tonight — at a key moment in the race.

Incumbent Mayor Karen Bass has been on her heels for most of the summer, plagued by a string of bad headlines and polling showing her rival, Nithya Raman, either tied with the mayor or in the lead.

But the onstage face-off follows a rough week for Raman, who spent most of the week playing defense on public safety. Her interview with an online content creator set off a dayslong back-and-forth about whether she called Bass, Police Chief Jim McDonnell and the LAPD “fascist liars;” her campaign said she was referring to Trump’s immigration agents but quickly disassociated her from the clip. She reiterated her desire to replace McDonnell as chief, but then said she would do so when his term ends in 2030.

And Raman’s record got a tough look from the Los Angeles Times on Monday about how she has shifted her stances on major issues such as police hiring and so-called mansion tax voters approved in 2022.

In short, last week was a referendum on Raman, not Bass’ first term, which is exactly what Bass wants. Expect her to continue hammering Raman on public safety during the debate. Bass plans to keep the pressure on Raman offstage as well. Her campaign will launch a district-by-district tour of the city Wednesday, starting on Raman’s turf.

Raman, meanwhile, will likely seek to bring the focus back on Bass and her handling of the city’s homelessness crisis, budgetary straits and wildfire response — issues that have contributed to the mayor’s consistently high disapproval ratings. And she goes into the debate continuing to chip into Bass’ lock on support from Democratic power players, including recent endorsements from the Sierra Club and SEIU 121RN, a local representing nurses and licensed medical professionals that had backed Bass in 2022 and Raman’s opponent in her 2024 City Council race.

The debate will air from 7 p.m. to 8 p.m on NBC 4, Telemundo 52 and on the their respective websites.

“Twenty-two days away from the midterm election, another politicized ‘investigation,’’’ Newsom wrote in a post on X. A senior Trump administration official granted anonymity to discuss an ongoing investigation told POLITICO that Monday’s search is related to Omar’s immigration paperwork.

STANDING DOWN — Newsom, often one to relish confrontation with the Trump administration, sidestepped a fight over undocumented student workers, POLITICO’s Eric He reports, disappointing immigrant rights activists. The governor’s veto of a bill that would have required the state’s public universities and community colleges to make students in the country illegally eligible for campus employment prolongs a yearslong push for the first-in-the-nation policy that would have benefited roughly 60,000 students.

The governor’s unwillingness to force the issue with Trump stemmed, at least in part, from what he said in his veto message was a concern that the hiring requirement wouldn’t hold up under legal scrutiny since federal law prohibits employers from hiring undocumented workers.

Advocates for the idea that students living in the country illegally should be permitted to work campus jobs have relied heavily on a legal theory a group of UCLA law professors published in 2022 that argued the federal ban doesn’t apply to state agencies.

PARTY DIVIDE — The statewide insurance commissioner’s race has emerged as a flashpoint in the intraparty battle between the Democratic establishment and progressives, POLITICO’s Saqib Rahim writes.

For progressives, the insurance post represents a chance to transform policy in an influential — if little-known — role in the nation’s most populous state. And it offers an alternative route to power for a wing of the Democratic Party that flopped in this year’s gubernatorial race after billionaire Tom Steyer lost in the primary.

Jane Kim, representing the party’s left flank, says the insurance industry needs a “transformative shift,” and has proposed making the state a major provider of insurance against natural disasters for all homeowners. Ben Allen, a veteran Democratic lawmaker, said Kim’s platform was akin to “blowing something up with no plan,” and has proposed an extension of policies set by outgoing Commissioner Ricardo Lara, combined with efforts to reduce fire risk in communities that he says will attract insurers.

LOS ANGELES

FIFA FLOP — Los Angeles hotels experienced lower-than-expected room demand during this summer’s World Cup tournament, according to a new industry report.

The analysis and new survey conducted by the Hospitality Education and Research Organization (HERO), a project of the Hotel Association of Los Angeles (HALA), found that 72% of the hotels surveyed said the World Cup hurt their 2026 financial picture, and nearly three-quarters of hotels held staffing flat or cut it outright.

“Projections for World Cup visitors were based on optimistic assumptions that international spectators would be eager to travel and stay in cities like Los Angeles. When those projections fell flat, hotels and their staff ultimately paid the price,” said Jackie Fillia, HALA President & CEO.

TOP TALKERS

RON’S REGRETS — Democratic megadonor and venture capitalist Ron Conway regrets funding an influential Big Tech political group that has fought strict AI rules, according to former Obama press secretary Jay Carney, who leads communications for Conway’s new AI policy initiative.

“Ron, if he had to do that over again, he wouldn’t make that donation [to Leading the Future],” Carney said in an exclusive interview with POLITICO’s Decoded newsletter, referring to the pro-AI industry super PAC that was also funded by a16z’s Marc Andreessen and OpenAI’s Greg Brockman. Conway donated $500,000 to Think Big, the Democratic arm of LTF, last year.

It’s the first time Conway and Carney have publicly broken with LTF and deals another blow to the ambitious group, which has invested millions in causes like defeating AI safety advocate Alex Bores in a New York Congressional primary. Brockman also recently pulled out of a planned additional $25 million donation to the PAC, amid reported pressure from AI safety advocates within his company.

It also highlights how prominent tech donors are rethinking their allegiances ahead of the midterms amid public scrutiny of the sector, including its ties to bipartisan groups like LTF that have focused on common industry priorities: Billionaire donor Chris Larsen just split from pro-crypto PAC Fairshake following its decision to spend $30 million against Sherrod Brown in the Ohio Senate race. (Conway also notably cut ties with Fairshake over Brown in 2024.)

FIRE SPOTTERS — A network of nearly 1,300 remote, high-tech cameras are filling in blind spots in monitoring California’s most fire-prone areas — and artificial intelligence is helping respond to them, The New York Times reports.

Since 2023, artificial intelligence has instantly scoured Alert California camera footage, spotting wildfires before a 911 call is received in most cases. Officials then use the camera feeds to monitor the fires, coordinating teams at the scene. Real-time views are available to anyone on the Alert California website, and screen grabs and time-lapse footage help fuel fire incident pages and popular apps like Watch Duty.

One fire official says that the network has “changed the world of response.”
