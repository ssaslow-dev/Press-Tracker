Newsom just put new limits on ICE. Here’s what’s changing in LA

Martín Macías, Jr.

A tax on immigration detention center operators. Bans on shock-gloves used by immigration agents and the use of state property for detentions or staging during ICE operations.

Those regulations are at the center of California bills signed into law Tuesday by Gov. Gavin Newsom that aim to put restraints on federal immigration enforcement activity. 

California lawmakers don’t have authority to shape federal immigration policy. But state Democrats authored a set of bills this year aiming to hold ICE accountable. Facing a Wednesday deadline, Newsom signed 21 bills that advanced to his desk, though some legislation was weakened as it made its way through the Senate and Assembly.
The governor also vetoed a handful of ICE-related bills, including legislation that would’ve prevented local and state law enforcement from picking up second jobs with ICE.

“We will continue protecting our people, upholding the rule of law, and making clear that if the federal government operates in California we will hold them accountable,” Newsom said in a public statement on Tuesday. 

Angelica Salas, executive director of the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights, or CHIRLA, said in an email to LA Public Press the newly signed laws signal to California’s immigrants that they are valued.

“At a time when cruel federal immigration policies are creating fear, separation, and suffering, California is choosing a different path—one rooted in dignity, humanity, and belonging,” Salas said.

Newsom signed the legislative package after Department of Homeland Security agents arrested about 100,000 immigrants in July and August combined, compared to roughly 31,000 arrests in April and 32,000 in May. The federal agency is also working to hire 5,000 new agents.

Asked to comment on the legislative package signed by Newsom, a DHS spokesperson said in an email to LA Public Press that California has a constitutional obligation to enforce immigration law. 

“While California sanctuary politicians continue to release pedophiles, rapists, gang members, and murderers onto their streets, our brave law enforcement will continue to risk their lives to arrest these heinous criminals and make California safe again,” the spokesperson said. DHS didn’t respond to a request to name their spokesperson.

People with no criminal record make up the overwhelming majority of immigrants targeted by ICE nationwide, according to federal data. 

Below are some of the key immigration-related bills that Newsom signed and vetoed. A list of all the signed bills is available here.

Approved: AB 1627

This bill prevents DHS agents with histories of misconduct from joining law enforcement agencies in the Golden State.

In a public statement Tuesday, AB 1627 author Assemblymember Anamarie Ávila Farías, a Democrat representing the city of Martinez in California’s East Bay, thanked Newsom for his signature.

“Our kids deserve role models in uniform, not individuals tied to fear and intimidation,” Ávila Farías said. “Federal agents who have disregarded constitutional rights are not to be entrusted with peace enforcement roles in our state.”

AB 1627 is set to take effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Approved: AB 1633

This bill establishes a 25% tax on the gross income for operators of the 8 private detention centers in California. As it made its way through the Senate, implementation of the bill was delayed from 2027 to 2028. 

The tax, which was decreased from 50% to 25% by state Senators, is expected to generate $177 million annually and fund legal services for immigrants. 

A DHS spokesperson said in an email that the tax won’t deter the Trump administration’s mass deportation campaign.

“No tax will stop ICE from deporting criminal illegal aliens to make California safe again,” the spokesperson said. “We need California to cooperate with our officers and stop releasing criminals from their jails into California’s neighborhoods.”

AB 1633 is set to take effect July 1, 2028.

Approved: AB 1650

The Slam the Brakes on ICE Act will require federal agents renting vehicles in the state to publicly display the name and logo for their agency on the front door panels of the vehicles.

While the bill’s author, LA Democratic Assemblymember Jessica Caloza, told LA Public Press the legislation will make it easier to spot rental vehicles used during DHS operations, immigrant defense groups said the bill contains loopholes for agents.

Exempt from the law are officers engaged in “surveillance, active undercover operations or investigative activities,” along with vehicles used for the “transport of a person who is already in lawful custodial status.”

The law also wouldn’t apply if an officer “reasonably determines” that displaying the agency name and logo would compromise their safety or the security of the person being transported.

In a public statement Tuesday, Caloza thanked Newsom for his signature and said her legislation requires “commonsense transparency.”

“No family should have to live in fear every time an unmarked vehicle drives through their neighborhood, wondering who is inside or why they are there,” Caloza said. “Public safety requires transparency, accountability, and respect for human dignity.”

AB 1650 is set to take effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Approved: SB 1367

Newsom also signed Riverside Democratic Sen. Sabrina Cervantes’ bill prohibiting the expansion of ICE detention centers.

Senate Bill 1367 bars local governments from issuing new land-use permits for construction of  detention facilities operated on behalf of a government agency. The law also blocks any permitting changes that would allow DHS to convert an existing building, such as a warehouse, into a detention center.

DHS spent more than $3 billion in the last year purchasing warehouses to convert into immigration detention centers and signed a $10 billion contract last month to build new detention facilities, none of which are currently slated to be built in California.

ICE detention centers in California are plagued by overcrowding and unsanitary conditions, according to state inspectors. Immigrants detained at the facilities have also reported a lack of proper medical care, violence by guards, and dirty food.

At least 58 people have died in ICE custody so far in President Donald Trump’s second presidency.

Cervantes said in a public statement Tuesday that SB 1367 blocks DHS from using property in California to build facilities where “human rights violations” occur.

“California will not stand by and allow the establishment of more detention facilities to serve as sites for government-sanctioned acts of inhumanity,” Cervantes said. “These facilities are operated by private entities to purposefully evade legal standards and avoid public accountability.” 

The legislation is sponsored by Southern California-based Inland Coalition for Immigrant Justice, or IC4IJ.

Hector Pereyra, political manager for IC4IJ said in a public statement that SB 1367 prevents the expansion of facilities that put profit ahead of the wellbeing of immigrants.

“SB 1367 is a victory for communities across California and makes clear that our communities are not marketplaces for mass incarceration,” Pereyra said.

SB 1367 is set to take effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Approved: AB 1806

This bill requires the Attorney General to conduct an independent investigation into fatal shootings of unarmed civilians by a immigration enforcement officer. 

Encino Democratic Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who authored the bill, said in a public statement in August that he wrote the legislation following federal prosecutors’ unwillingness to investigate DHS agents who fatally shot Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis.

In LA, Keith Porter was fatally shot by an off-duty ICE agent on New Year’s Eve. The LA County District Attorney hasn’t announced whether he’ll criminally charge the agent.

Of the 11 people shot by ICE agents nationwide this year, only one was shot in California. In April, Carlos Ivan Mendoza Hernandez was shot and wounded by immigration agents during an arrest in Patterson in the Central Valley.

AB 1806 is set to take effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Approved: AB 1807

This bill bars state-owned structures, parks or other property from being used for ICE staging, processing or detention.

Asm. Gabriel, who authored the bill, said in a public statement both AB 1806 and 1807 will “strengthen the rule of law” in California.

“California must continue to push back against the Trump Administration to protect our immigrant communities and our constitutional rights,” Gabriel said.

AB 1807 takes effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Approved: SB 873

This bill, authored by San Bernardino Democratic Sen. Eloise Gómez Reyes, requires immigration agents to present identification and a valid judicial warrant before making arrests within 1,000 feet of a California courthouse.

In LA and across the country, DHS agents have continuously arrested immigrants at courthouses, including during check-ins with immigration officials and during court hearings. In June, a federal judge banned DHS from arresting people inside immigration courts.

Reyes said in a public statement her legislation aims to ensure ICE operations don’t prevent people from participating in the judicial process.

“Justice cannot work when people are afraid to walk through courthouse doors,” Reyes said. “No victim, witness, or family member should have to choose between seeking justice and risking civil arrest.”

Tina Rosales-Torres, policy advocate for Western Center on Law & Poverty, said in a statement that ICE arrests at courthouses are a threat to democracy. 

“When immigrants are afraid to enter our courthouses because they fear being arrested by ICE, it undermines more than their individual ability to seek justice, it threatens the principles that protect all of us,” Rosales-Torres said. 

SB 873 takes effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Approved: AB 2760

This bill bars local and federal law enforcement from using “wearable electroshock” devices in California and prohibits the use of state funds to purchase them.

ICE is spending $16.7 million to purchase 6,000 pairs of electroshock gloves for its agents over the next five months.

The law also requires the California Justice Department to study the devices’ safety and reporting findings to lawmakers by 2029. State legislators could extend or revise the law based on those findings before the law sunsets on Jan. 1, 2030.

Kevin Johnson, a professor emeritus at the University of California, Davis School of Law told the Associated Press on Tuesday that courts will likely rule that California can’t bar federal agents from wearing the gloves because the U.S. Constitution generally privileges federal law over any conflicting state law.

Spokespeople for the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department and Los Angeles Police Department told LA Public Press the agencies don’t own or utilize any electroshock gloves.

AB 2760 takes effect Jan. 1, 2027.

Vetoed: AB 1537

The No Side Jobs for ICE Act, authored by LA Democratic Assemblymember Isaac Bryan, would have prohibited members of local enforcement from having a second job detaining, arresting or deporting people while enforcing federal immigration law.

The bill would’ve required officers to report secondary job offers from DHS or other agencies to their employers and failure to comply with the law could’ve resulted in an officer being decertified, according to the bill’s text.

The bill was opposed by the Peace Officers Research Association of California.

In March, the LA City Council approved an ordinance barring city workers from working second jobs with DHS or any entity involved in immigration enforcement.

Following Newsom’s veto, he issued a statement Wednesday calling the bill’s prohibition on second jobs for law enforcement officers “a step too far.” 

The statement said officers in California already operate under personal conduct standards and could have their peace officer status revoked for violating them.

In a text message to LA Public Press, Bryan said he was disappointed with the veto.

“We have to do everything we can to protect our communities from ICE terror, including cutting off their recruitment efforts to local law enforcement,” Bryan said.

In a statement emailed Wednesday to LA Public Press, the ICE Out of California coalition said the veto leaves the public in the dark about secondary work local law enforcement is engaged in.

“At the end of the day, we are forced to ask: what are they hiding,” the statement said. “How many sheriffs and police are secretly moonlighting for ICE and abducting our neighbors?”

Vetoed: AB 2465

The bill would’ve banned businesses from receiving tax credits or state-approved loans and grants if they own, operate, manage or are invested in private immigration detention centers.

The first-of-its-kind bill, authored by San Leandro Democratic Assemblymember Liz Ortega, would’ve also applied to businesses who contract with agencies engaged in immigration enforcement.

“I was three years old when my parents brought me here. I’m not spending one dime of California money on helping ICE lock up kids like me,” Ortega said in a public statement in August.

Health care providers contracting with private immigration detention centers would’ve been exempt from the bill.

In his veto message Wednesday, Newsom said that while he agreed with the goal of the legislation, he was concerned it could harm legitimate state economic development programs or hurt businesses providing goods to a range of agencies.

Vetoed: SB 1105

The Protect California Rights Act would’ve prevented law enforcement agencies in the state from joining interagency task forces with the federal government unless agreements are in writing and expressly state that agencies won’t engage in racial or identity profiling. 

In his veto message Wednesday, Newsom said state law already prohibits racial profiling and that the bill could harm existing and future interagency agreements that facilitate critical information sharing and public safety initiatives, such as preparing for the 2028 Olympics in LA.
