I have been coaching people on food for years, and the single most common question I get has nothing to do with macros or meal timing. It's "how do I actually know what's good for me at the store?" Because the labels are designed to confuse. "Heart healthy." "Low fat." "Made with real ingredients." These phrases mean almost nothing, and food companies know it.

When I saw that California is moving forward with Assembly Bill 2244, a bill that would create the first state certified "non-ultra-processed" food seal in the country, my first reaction was relief. My second was skepticism. I think both reactions are worth sharing.

What California Is Actually Doing

Assembly member Jesse Gabriel introduced AB 2244 to create a "California Certified" seal for food products that are not ultra processed. Products get disqualified if they contain industrial additives, artificial flavors, artificial colors, emulsifiers, thickeners, or high levels of added sugar and sodium. Basically anything that turns real food into a shelf-stable science experiment.

The structure mirrors organic certification: voluntary for brands, independent third party verification, recertification every three years, overseen by the California Department of Public Health. Over 300 brands are reportedly on the waitlist already. Early certified products include Spindrift sparkling water, Chomps meat sticks, and Amy's frozen pizza.

That last one I will come back to.

The retail piece is where this gets genuinely interesting. Large retailers selling more than 25 certified products and doing over $10 million annually would be required to display those products prominently at checkout and store entrances. Right now the healthiest options in most stores are buried in the back while engineered junk sits at eye level at the register. That one shift in shelf placement changes behavior more than any label ever could on its own.

Why This Could Actually Be a Big Deal

California loves being the food police, and it usually works. In the 1990s, the state created its own organic certification that eventually pressured Congress into establishing federal organic standards. That market is now worth $60 billion annually.

Ultra processed foods make up over 60 percent of what Americans eat daily. The FDA and USDA are both scrambling to define UPFs at the federal level. California is moving faster, and if history is any guide, what California does in its grocery stores tends to show up in yours within a decade. The state's 40 million consumers mean food manufacturers would rather reformulate once for the whole country than manage two separate supply chains. If a major brand wants the seal, they clean up the formula. And once they clean it up for California, that cleaner version usually becomes the national one.

My Problem With the Certification

I want to be clear. I think this bill is worth passing. But any system is only as good as the people defining the terms, and I have watched "organic" get stretched beyond recognition over the last 20 years. Big food learned to check every box while keeping the spirit of it out. You can buy organic cookies loaded with sugar. Organic chips fried in seed oils. The word on the front of the package does not tell you whether the food inside is actually good for you. I expect the same thing to happen with this certification eventually. Not because the people behind it are bad, but because that is how these things always go.

Amy's frozen pizza being one of the first certified products is the clearest example of my concern. I do not know every ingredient in their line, but frozen pizza is still frozen pizza. The real question is how California ultimately defines "ultra processed," and if that definition has enough gray area for frozen meals to qualify, brands will find every inch of it and set up shop there.

There is also a political dimension. The MAHA movement has been loudly critical of ultra processed foods, but has been notably absent from supporting this bill. My read is that it is either political positioning, a preference for federal action on their own terms, or a genuine belief that the California approach is not strict enough. Probably some combination of all three. That does not make the bill wrong. It just means the conversation at the top is more complicated than the label on the shelf.

How I Actually Shop

I appreciate the certification and I will use it as a useful signal. But I do not rely on any label as the final word, and neither should you.

Here is what I actually do. I try to buy as close to the farmer or the source as possible. Farmers markets first when I can get there. When I am at a grocery store, I shop by single ingredients. A chicken breast. A sweet potato. An avocado. Not products. Ingredients. I look for organic and non-GMO when those labels apply to something that actually needs them, and I flip the package over. If there are more than five or six things listed on the back, I think hard about whether I need that item at all.

A client of mine came to me dealing with PCOS. Hormones, weight, energy, all of it disrupted. We did not use a single supplement or shake. We just ate real food. Meat, eggs, vegetables, fruit, rice, potatoes. Things that came from the earth, not from a factory. She lost weight. She got her period back. Her hormone levels improved significantly. None of what she ate needed a certification label, because none of it was processed in the first place.

What to Do With This

In the meantime, use the California Certified seal as one data point when you see it. Better than nothing. But do not let it replace your own judgment. The same way "organic" became a costume big food learned to wear, "non-ultra-processed" will too. It is only a matter of time.

Go to your farmers market this weekend. Buy something with one ingredient. Cook it. That is still the clearest path to eating well, and no certification changes that.
