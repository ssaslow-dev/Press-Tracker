California creates a non-ultraprocessed food seal for the state

Douglas Brown

The Non-GMO Project, which runs Non-UPF Verified, opposed the voluntary seal, which won't go into effect until at least 2029. Get the details of the new legislation.

At a Glance: California officials must accredit non-UPF certifiers by June 1, 2029. The new standard borrows California's 2025 school UPF definition. With the new law, state and local prosecutors can sue brands that misuse the certification.

Critics of ultra-processed food enjoyed a big boost Monday, when California Gov. Gavin Newsom signed a bill creating a voluntary, state-backed Non-Ultra-processed Certified seal that could require some large grocers to spotlight certified products. The law could help natural and organic brands stand out on conventional grocery shelves, and the seal enters a marketplace that already supports at least two private non-UPF certifications.

Now, the work heads to the California Department of Public Health, which is tasked with creating the seal and rolling it out. Manufacturers seeking the seal will apply to third-party certifiers, a model that mirrors how brands gain USDA Organic certification.

“Like the USDA Organic label, this new seal will provide consumers with clear, trustworthy information and make it easier for them to locate healthier foods that are free from harmful additives,” Assembly member Jesse Gabriel, D-Encino, the bill's author, says in an Environmental Working Group news release. “Parents shouldn't need a Ph.D. in chemistry to understand what they're feeding their kids.” The group co-sponsored the bill.

The bill passed both chambers unanimously, 32-0 in the Senate and 72-0 in the Assembly.

Experts raise concerns about new law

California’s standard, which won’t reach store shelves until the middle of 2029 at the earliest, will enter a marketplace with at least two non-UPF certification regimes: Non-UPF Verified and WiseCode. Megan Westgate, CEO and founder of the Non-GMO Project, which runs Non-UPF Verified, praised California for helping to move forward the national conversation about ultra-processed foods. But Non-UPF Verified opposed the legislation and asked Newsom to veto the bill. Non-UPF Verified’s problem with California’s standard hinged on certification integrity, Westgate says.

“Any rigorous non-UPF certification must examine the processing methods used for ingredients and finished products,” rather than just focusing on the lists of ingredients on labels, Westgate says. “Non-UPF Verified asks, ‘How were the ingredients and finished product actually made?’ Any products bearing our verification mark must meet rigorous requirements for ingredient integrity and formulation, and processing limits and methods.”

The new standard draws from an earlier law that defined ultra-processed food and set a timeline for phasing the worst of it out of schools. That definition classifies a food as ultra-processed if it contains an additive with certain technical effects, such as an emulsifier, stabilizer, color or flavoring agent, and is also high in saturated fat, sodium or added sugar or contains a nonnutritive sweetener. The same definition now also determines which grocery products can qualify for the seal. Products that fall within the state’s definition of ultra-processed cannot emblazon packaging with the seal. The department must finish writing its rules on which foods are disqualified by June 1, 2028, and accredit third-party certifiers by June 1, 2029.

Once it’s up and running, the department, the attorney general and local prosecutors can go to court to stop brands from misusing the seal. That enforcement matters. Many natural and organic products industry stakeholders will point toward the legal teeth as one reason for USDA Organic’s success.

Anna Rosales, vice president of science and policy at the Institute of Food Technologists, wrote in a blog post Monday that even as California crafts ultra-processed food policy, the FDA and USDA together are working on a uniform federal definition. If other states follow California’s lead on ultra-processed foods before a federal definition is finalized, she writes, manufacturers, retailers and schools could face conflicting rules about what counts as ultra-processed. She also argues that nutritional quality, rather than processing alone, should serve as the foundation of any standards. The Institute’s position contrasts with Non-UPF Verified’s emphasis on the importance of assessing processing. California’s definition falls somewhere between the two, identifying a list of additives along with thresholds for saturated fat, sodium and added sugar.

While the law doesn’t require brands to apply for the seal, it does compel grocery stores with more han $10 million in annual sales that carry more than 25 state-certified products to highlight at least three of them. Grocers can use dedicated displays, signage or other shelf cues to showcase certified products.

Alyssa Moran, deputy director of the Center for Food and Nutrition Policy at the University of Pennsylvania, says in the Environmental Working Group news release that “because companies are not required to disclose an ingredient’s purpose, it can be really difficult even for experts, even for people like me who have a doctoral degree in nutrition, to look at a food package and determine whether a food is ultra-processed or not.

“If we want to meaningfully curb disease, we need a suite of complementary policies that limit the availability of ultra-processed foods while promoting non-ultra-processed alternatives.”
