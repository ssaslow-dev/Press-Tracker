LA County Superior Court Judge, Office 131

David Ross and Donna Tryfman are the candidates for LA County Superior Court Judge, Office 131 on the November 3, 2026 ballot.

Superior court judges run the trial courts in California, where a judge, and sometimes a jury, hears testimony and other evidence and decides cases by applying the law to the facts. Each county has one superior court that handles all types of cases. Office No. 131 is one seat on the Los Angeles County Superior Court.

What it means for the City of LA

Superior court judges are elected by voters across the whole county, so every City of Los Angeles voter gets this contest. The Los Angeles County Superior Court is the trial court for the entire county, including the City.

The candidates

David Ross

Ross was born and raised in Los Angeles, graduated from Beverly Hills High School and UC Davis, and earned his law degree at the University of West Los Angeles (CityWatch; State Bar). Before law he was a legislative aide to an Indiana congressman and a TV reporter and documentary producer (LA Times). He was a Riverside County deputy public defender for five years and has been a Los Angeles County deputy alternate public defender since 2000, in the Torrance office since 2018 (LA Times). He has not held public office.

Donna Tryfman

Tryfman is the daughter of Holocaust survivors (Daily News). She graduated from Brandeis University in 1989 and the University of La Verne College of Law in 1994, and worked at the Law Offices of Mark J. Werksman (campaign bio; State Bar). She has been a Los Angeles County deputy public defender since 1998 and now works in the Major Crimes Unit on special-circumstances homicide cases (LA Times). She served on the Beverly Hills Rent Stabilization Commission from 2019 to 2024, chairing it in 2022 (MetNews), and ran unsuccessfully for the Beverly Hills school board in 2020 (Daily News).

The job

What a judge does. Superior courts have trial jurisdiction over all criminal and civil cases in the county. Judges serve six-year terms and are elected on a nonpartisan ballot. When a seat opens between elections, the Governor fills it by appointment (Judicial Council fact sheet). LAist lists the kinds of cases the LA court hears: child custody and divorce, landlord and tenant disputes, contract fights, theft and murder trials, probate and small claims. Judges have discretion over things like sentence length and damage awards (LAist).

Size. Hoodline, citing the court, calls it the nation’s largest trial court system and reports 36 courthouses in 12 judicial districts, more than 580 judicial officers and about 5,000 full-time employees serving 9.5 million residents (Hoodline). LAist counts more than 400 judges (LAist). Superior courts also have commissioners and referees, according to the Judicial Council fact sheet. The court says it receives about 1.3 million filings a year (ABC7).

Budget. The court’s funding comes through the state budget. In September 2026 the court said it is underfunded by $233.7 million, that the current state budget covers 76.77% of its calculated need (the lowest level since 2021), and that it would offer $35,000 to employees who agree to leave. It warned of longer lines and slower processing (ABC7).

Pay. The LA Times reports a superior court judge’s salary is more than $244,000 a year (LA Times).

Rules for candidates. The Code of Judicial Ethics bars judicial candidates from making statements “that commit the candidate … with respect to cases, controversies, or issues that are likely to come before the courts” (Canon 5B(1)(a), Code of Judicial Ethics). As a result, candidates talk about how they would do the job rather than where they stand on issues.

Discipline. The Commission on Judicial Performance handles complaints against judges. In 2025 it received complaints about 1,116 judges and justices, opened 99 preliminary investigations and took 15 public actions, the Metropolitan News-Enterprise reported from its annual report (MetNews).

Bar ratings. The Los Angeles County Bar Association’s Judicial Elections Evaluation Committee rates candidates Exceptionally Well Qualified, Well Qualified, Qualified or Not Qualified. It issued its 2026 ratings on April 22, 2026, for the primary (LACBA report). LAist notes the LACBA is a private, volunteer membership group separate from the State Bar (LAist). No separate general-election report was found.

This seat. Office No. 131 is an open seat. Judge Joseph Brandolino, who held it, announced he would retire effective March 31, 2026, the Metropolitan News-Enterprise reported (MetNews, Jan 30, 2026).

Records

David Ross

Legal career. Ross was admitted to the State Bar on Dec. 12, 1994 (bar number 174591). His profile lists the University of West Los Angeles law school and the Alternate Public Defender’s office in Torrance, and shows no public discipline (State Bar profile).

  • He was a Riverside County deputy public defender for five years. In 2000 he joined the Los Angeles County Alternate Public Defender’s office, working in branches including Long Beach and Compton, and has been in Torrance since 2018 (LA Times).
  • His campaign says he has represented more than 10,000 indigent defendants, tried 113 jury trials and handled more than 2,000 preliminary hearings (Beverly Hills Standard, citing the campaign). The LA Times reports “more than 110 jury trials” from his website (LA Times).
  • His title is Grade IV deputy alternate public defender (Beverly Hills Standard). The campaign also says he taught as an adjunct professor at Western State University College of Law from 2002 to 2006 and served as a judge pro tem in small claims and limited civil cases (Beverly Hills Standard).

Before law. He was a legislative aide to Rep. Floyd J. Fithian of Indiana, produced segments of the PBS series “Congress: We The People,” and worked as a TV reporter in Tennessee and Kansas (CityWatch; Daily News questionnaire).

Ballot designation. His designation on the ballot is “Deputy Alternate Public Defender, County of Los Angeles” (final candidate list).

Prior campaigns. None found. He was the first candidate to file for this seat, in January 2026 (MetNews, Jan 30, 2026).

Ethics and court records. His State Bar history shows only his 1994 admission, with no discipline or administrative actions (State Bar profile). No public enforcement actions were found in FPPC or FEC records that refer to him (FPPC case search; FEC legal search). He has sat as a judge pro tem; the Commission on Judicial Performance’s list of public decisions by judge’s name includes no David Ross (CJP). Searched Sept. 30, 2026.

Donna Tryfman

Legal career. Tryfman was admitted to the State Bar on June 12, 1995 (bar number 176744). Her profile lists the University of La Verne College of Law, criminal law as her practice area, and Hebrew and Spanish as languages, and shows no public discipline (State Bar profile).

  • She has been a Los Angeles County deputy public defender since 1998. From 2016 to 2019 she was deputy in charge at Eastlake Juvenile Court, supervising 25 lawyers. She has worked in collaborative drug court. She is now in the Major Crimes Unit, handling only special-circumstances homicides (LA Times). She says she has tried more than 100 jury trials (Daily News questionnaire).
  • The Metropolitan News-Enterprise reports her office performance evaluations give her an overall rating of “competent,” with comments that she “asks for the most serious cases and never backs down from a challenge” (MetNews editorial, Apr 1, 2026).
  • She was president of the statewide Public Defenders Association from 2015 to 2020 (MetNews editorial). Her campaign bio says she received the Gideon Award as felony attorney of the year in 2025 (LA Times).
  • She has served as a judge pro tem in traffic and small claims cases and on the court’s Mediation Volunteer Panel (Daily News editorial). She is vice chair of the LACBA delegation to the California Conference of Bar Associations and has chaired its Criminal Justice Section (MetNews editorial).

Public roles. She served on the Beverly Hills Rent Stabilization Commission from 2019 to 2024 and chaired it in 2022 (MetNews editorial).

Prior campaign. She ran for the Beverly Hills Unified School District board in 2020 and lost (Daily News editorial).

Ethics and court records. Her State Bar history shows only her 1995 admission, with no discipline or administrative actions (State Bar profile). No public enforcement actions were found in FPPC or FEC records, including for her 2020 school board campaign (FPPC case search; FEC legal search). She is not a judge, so the Commission on Judicial Performance has no record on her. Searched Sept. 30, 2026.
