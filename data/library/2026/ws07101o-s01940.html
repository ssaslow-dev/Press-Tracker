Xavier Becerra's dispassionate supporters

UNENTHUSIASTIC YESES — Xavier Becerra is poised to win the governorship by a wide margin. But he isn’t exactly generating enthusiasm: Just 44% of likely voters view him favorably, according to a new poll from POLITICO and its partners.

Becerra leads Republican rival Steve Hilton by 18 points with 54% support among likely voters. And he is more popular among Democrats and registered voters who lean toward the party. But even 18% of respondents in that group view him unfavorably, the UC Berkeley Citrin Center for Public Opinion Research-POLITICO poll found.

“This is rather tepid, even among Democrats,” said Jon Cohen, founder and CEO of TrueDot, whose firm fielded the poll.

The findings reflect the public opinion challenge facing the former health secretary after Tom Steyer and other, shallower-pocketed opponents spent millions of dollars seeking to tarnish his image in the primary.

But his struggles with California voters are not unique. Fifty-three percent of likely voters disapprove of the way Gov. Gavin Newsom is handling the governorship, and 68% disapprove of President Donald Trump’s job performance. Hilton’s 55% unfavorability among likely voters is higher than Becerra’s by a full 8 points, even if the Trump-endorsed candidate fares better with his own party than Becerra does with Democrats.

To voters right now, “there’s no heroes out there,” said Jack Citrin, a UC Berkeley political science professor and co-director of the poll. “That tepid outlook sort of is consistent with this more generalized mistrust of politicians and cynicism.”

Becerra did better in a separate poll from the Berkeley Institute of Governmental Studies, which found Becerra’s favorability among likely voters higher, at 48%, and his unfavorability lower, at 40%.

And in the Citrin results, Becerra registered higher marks, too, in a concurrent survey of POLITICO’s audience of key political and policy influencers in the state, including political staffers, lobbyists, policy advisers and others deeply versed in California’s political landscape. Among this group of influencers — the kind of people Becerra would need to work with as governor — 64% view him favorably, compared to only 29% unfavorably. These data come from parallel surveys of California voters and policy influencers, fielded by TrueDot, the AI-accelerated research platform, in collaboration with the Citrin Center at UC Berkeley and POLITICO. Interviews for the voter survey were conducted online in English and Spanish from Sept. 8-14, among a sample of 2,418 registered voters, including 1,983 likely voters, selected at random through Political Data Inc. and Verasight, with supplemental online panel recruitment. Voter results were weighted to California registered-voter benchmarks for age, educational attainment, race and ethnicity, gender, and geography, with additional calibration to 2024 presidential vote by region.

From Sept. 11-16, a parallel study was conducted in partnership with POLITICO among its audience of key political and policy influencers in California. The audience was defined based on job title and organizational affiliation and included state and local government employees, political staffers, lobbyists, policy advisers, consultants, business decision-makers and subject matter experts.

The margin of error is plus or minus 2.7% for the registered voter survey, plus or minus 3% for likely voters and plus or minus 4.5% for the influencers.

IN THE COURTS

THE NEXT CAMPAIGN — The 2025 election was at the heart of a dispute resolved by the California Supreme Court yesterday. But the 2026 vote loomed unmistakably over the standoff.

A vindicated Bonta repeatedly linked Bianco’s unlawful seizure of some 650,000 ballots to the Trump administration trying to assert control over voting, including by subpoenaing information about election officials in Georgia and seeking to limit mail ballots. Bonta said his office has run extensive exercises gaming out scenarios like Trump deploying the National Guard or the military to polling places. (Gen. Dan Caine, the chair of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, has said there are no plans to send troops to polling places.)

“We never should have been in this position but, of course, with Trump we’re seeing unprecedented attacks on our election integrity,” Bonta, who sued to halt Bianco’s probe, told reporters. “Come November, we’re ready for whatever comes our way.”

Attorney Norm Eisen called the ruling “a key precedent as we prepare to defend the 2026 election.” Gov. Gavin Newsom responded by touting newly signed laws “preventing a similar future attack on democracy” by deeming various forms of election interference felonies.

And in a bracing concurring opinion, Justice Goodwin Liu emphasized “the seriousness of the violation,” warning that “for our democracy to flourish, our elections must be conducted in accordance with the law.” That depends, he wrote, on officials following the rules — an implicit warning about a bad actor’s power to disrupt the process.

“It is a lot to ask of our election officials that they not only do their jobs competently but also stand ready to sue anyone who unlawfully interferes with their duties. Yet here we are,” Liu wrote, adding: “It should not have come to this.”

LOS ANGELES

WRITE ON — Nithya Raman scooped up the endorsement of the Writer’s Guild of America West on Thursday. The screenwriters union said the City Council member and mayoral hopeful was “the true pro-labor candidate who walks the walk.” The vast majority of Los Angeles’ labor unions have aligned with incumbent Mayor Karen Bass, but Raman has racked up a number of heavyweight union endorsements, including the carpenters and hospitality workers unions.

Raman and the WGAW were on the same side of the biggest topic dominating Hollywood lately — the merger of Paramount and Warner Bros., which both opposed. But Raman’s ties to the union go way back; her husband, TV writer Vali Chandrasekaran, is a longtime member.

STAR GAZING — The Bass campaign is jumping on a report from the Westside Current that Raman missed a vote on eviction protections and financial assistance for victims of the Palisades fire to attend the unveiling of Mindy Kaling’s star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame.

“Nithya Raman never showed up for the Palisades until she started running for mayor. Here’s more proof: Instead of voting to help fire survivors, she was hanging out with Hollywood celebrities,” said Bass campaign spokesperson Alex Stack.

But the story’s description of the “key” vote came under scrutiny when City Hall watchers noted the missed vote was procedural in nature and that the council member who represents the area, Traci Park, was also not present.

“This motion passed unanimously, and votes not cast from the three Councilmembers who were absent did not affect the outcome,” said Liz Garcia, spokesperson for Raman’s campaign. “Nithya has introduced and moved multiple Council measures addressing fire response and recovery and released a detailed Palisades Recovery Plan. As mayor, she would work closely with Councilmember Park and the community to help residents rebuild and return home as quickly as possible.”

The hit on Raman’s missed vote was panned by Spencer Pratt, who said the storyline just reminded the public of Bass’ “most infamous failure” in the Palisades.

HOLLYWOOD

NOT A DONE DEAL — The judge overseeing a case brought by 12 states seeking to block Paramount’s acquisition of Warner Bros. questioned the parties during a 50-minute hearing Thursday but did not approve a proposed settlement that would end the litigation.

Federal Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín said she was not a “rubber stamp” for the deal and that a ruling would come “in due course.”

The judge began the hearing in Oakland with a series of detailed questions about the settlement, and ended with a “homework” assignment for both sides: She asked them to address, by noon Monday, a letter the court received from Sen. Cory Booker that questioned whether the agreement’s temporary commitments would adequately restore competition.

The prospective settlement includes several five-year commitments, including a requirement that Paramount release at least 30 theatrical films a year for the first two years and 32 a year for the next three. It also calls for an editorial-independence board for CNN and CBS News, among other provisions. But it requires no immediate structural remedies — which typically involve asset sales — and allows Paramount to proceed with the $111 billion merger without any upfront divestiture.

Martínez-Olguín questioned whether the settlement meets the standards of federal antitrust law and asked about the process leading up to the agreement, including whether the two sides were effectively coordinating rather than engaging in a truly adversarial negotiation.

An attorney representing the California attorney general’s office said that Paramount was vigorously represented, and that both sides engaged in “hours and hours” of substantial negotiations over the terms.

The judge also singled out a number of provisions of the proposed settlement, including one that allows Paramount, after two years, to ask the court to modify the agreement if the company can show that the deal harms its ability to operate and compete.

“You all know that isn’t the standard under” the federal antitrust statutes, which permit modification of relief ordered or agreed to only under certain specific and extraordinary circumstances, Martinez-Olguin said. “I’m trying to understand what you all are trying to do with that. It reads to me like a modification of what’s required under federal law, and that gives me some pause.”

An attorney for Paramount responded that the provision is aimed at a highly unlikely hypothetical circumstance in which the company is somehow prevented from complying with the agreement. — Daniel Miller and Hassan Kanu

FOR GOOD MEASURE

FIRST IN POLITICO: A MILLION ‘YESES’ — Airbnb is backing Proposition 1, donating $1 million dollars to the campaign for a ballot measure aiming to build more affordable housing and infrastructure, Chase DiFeliciantonio reports.

The $11.25 billion general obligation bond has Newsom’s backing, along with the state Democratic Party and labor interests including the California AFL-CIO.

The money comes from the company as part of Airbnb’s Housing Accelerator, a quarter-billion dollar initiative announced earlier this month to finance housing and support zoning and other reforms to make housing easier to build.

The donation would put Airbnb among the top donors to the campaign, which include the billionaire-backed PAC Building a Better California and union advocacy group the Western States Regional Council of Carpenters Issues Committee.

FIRST IN POLITICO: YOU LIE — Proponents of an initiative to scale back California’s landmark environmental law are pushing back on a recent ad rolled out by opponents of the measure, Proposition 45. At issue is the ad’s contention the initiative would expedite unpopular projects such as data centers. Proponents say that’s not so, citing Rachelle Chong, former commissioner of both the Federal Communications Commission and the California Public Utilities Commission, who concluded the measure does not apply to conventional data centers.

“Prop 45 opponents are lying to voters, plain and simple,” said Amelia Matier of the Yes on 45 campaign in a statement. “They know Californians are fed up with an unaffordable status quo that makes it too slow and too expensive to build the basics, driving up our cost of living. Instead of defending years of costly delays, they’re resorting to scare tactics about projects that aren’t even included in Prop 45.”

ON THE HILL

FIRST IN POLITICO: BIPARTISAN SPACE ACADEMY PITCH — California is the latest state to jump into the sweepstakes for Trump’s proposed Space Academy, with senior House lawmakers from both parties making a pitch to host it, POLITICO’s Morning Defense reports.

GOP Rep. Ken Calvert, who chairs the Defense Appropriations panel, and Rep. Ted Lieu, the vice chair of the House Democratic caucus, urged NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, who is leading a commission tasked with standing up the institution, to consider California in a letter sent Thursday. The pair are the co-chairs of the California Aerospace Caucus.

California joins at least a half-dozen states whose leaders have expressed interest in hosting the Space Academy. But it is the lone solidly Democratic state so far to enter the fray. Much of the campaigning so far has come from Republicans in largely GOP-led states, some of whom are drawing on close ties to the Trump administration.

Top Talkers

HIT PAUSE — A group of 10 Democrats, including Rep. Ro Khanna, has signed on to Texas Rep. Greg Casar’s bill that would ban superintelligence and pause advanced AI development until a vetting regime could be stood up.

Khanna and New York Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez are co-sponsoring the bill, named the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, Owen Dahlkamp reports.

In a statement, Khanna noted that he convened a hearing this week on AI safety cooperation between the U.S. and China as ranking member of the House China Select Committee with AI experts who underscored the need for a verifiable pause on superintelligence.

SHARE OR HOARD — The White House has asked OpenAI and Anthropic not to share their new artificial intelligence models with the U.K. government’s testing agency until the models have gone through testing with the U.S. government, a person familiar with the matter and a senior U.S. administration official told Sophia Cai and Joseph Bambridge.

The request puts companies in the difficult position of having to choose between withholding their models from the U.K.’s AI Security Institute or risk upsetting the Trump White House. The official said the White House wants the U.S. to review the models and take steps to make sure U.S. systems are secure before the models are shared with U.S. partners.
