California’s ‘crazy’ ballot measure economy and Newsom’s world tour

By JEREMY B. WHITE, BLAKE JONES and DUSTIN GARDINER

SIGNATURE ISSUE — California’s overheated ballot measure economy was spared one temperature spike — but it’s still about to boil over.

Proponents of more than a dozen statewide measures have been gathering signatures to get on the November ballot, with each new entrant threatening to drive up what signature gathering firms can charge. For some measures, prices had already climbed as deep-pocketed players like Uber, which recently dropped another $20 million into its ballot committee, pushed to qualify.

“It’s already crazy,” said longtime petition pro Fred Kimball. “This is the strangest year I’ve ever seen.”

And that was before the anti-wealth-tax windfall.

An expected acceleration … Consultants have been anxiously waiting for campaigns with enormous war chests to hit the street, causing prices to spike — and lately, they’ve been obsessing over a mysterious cluster of measures designed to undercut a billionaire’s tax. Because latecomers have a scant two months to qualify, a sprint to the finish inflates costs even further.

“With so many that are late, the price is bid up,” said former Sen. Bob Hertzberg, who said his housing bond has enough signatures that it can come off the street soon. “There’s a real fear that a lot of the people aren’t going to qualify.”

Everyone is watching for the fallout. Signature inflation has become one of the hottest topics in California politics, with people trading data about who’s paying what and speculating about which rival campaigns will lose out.

“You have really well-resourced groups that are willing to pretty much light a match to money,” said a person working on an initiative already in circulation, who was not authorized to speak publicly on its proponent’s behalf. “‘If you think it’s expensive now, just wait,’ is the framing I keep getting.”

We’re starting to find out. Two of the five anti-wealth-tax measures hit the street at $12 a signature as of Friday, according to campaign professionals tracking the market. The going rate rose to $10 for a union-backed measure extending a high income tax on top earners and a lawyer-championed measure counteracting Uber’s initiative governing legal representation.

A reprieve: Sighs of relief echoed through Sacramento last week as OpenAI announced it would pull its measure to regulate artificial intelligence, which had just received title and summary, as it hunts for a legislative deal. The initiative could still return in 2028.

So for now, OpenAI’s cash won’t be a factor. But the wealth tax’s opponents are both determined and rich, and campaigns are closely monitoring to see if they flood the market with enough money to undermine SEIU-UHW’s proposed wealth tax.

Kimball, who has the levy as a client, said he believes it is still on track to qualify, although the cost per signature had already jumped from $5 to $8 in a couple of weeks before the counter-measures started circulating.

The people behind the five anti-wealth-tax measures have been resolutely tight-lipped about their supporters and their strategy (per usual their designated proponent did not comment for this story). But it’s not hard to envision a world in which they put their best-polling measure on the street at the highest price they can manage.

Whether that’s intended to qualify their own measures as quickly as possible or freeze out their rivals, the hit to rival campaigns’ bank accounts would be the same.

“It’s the battle,” said Kimball, “between the billionaires and the people trying to pass legislation to fight the billionaires.”

Speaking of wealth tax foes … another committee to fight the initiative surfaced last week, as the Anti-Tax Cinematic Universe continues to add characters.

The new Golden State Promise PAC is staffed by consultants who worked for the crypto industry Super PAC Fairshake. Others on board include pollster Ben Tulchin and voter outreach firm JPM&M. Spokesperson Roger Salazar didn’t provide detail about who will fund the committee but said in a statement that it would bankroll an eight-figure statewide campaign arguing the wealth tax would damage California’s economy and budget.

WHERE’S GAVIN? Back in California after attending the Munich Security Conference.

GAVIN’S WORLD TOUR — Newsom had a packed long weekend at the Munich Security Conference and in London, signing a clean energy pact with the U.K. on Monday after reassuring world leaders that Donald Trump is “temporary.” He further poked the president by saying during a panel that Europe had united — against Trump.

Newsom’s second appearance at an international gathering in Europe in as many months drew the attention of the president, who tore into the governor and said his agreement with the U.K. was “inappropriate.”

“The U.K.’s got enough trouble without getting involved with Gavin Newscum,” Trump said in a brief interview with Sophia Cai. “Gavin is a loser. Everything he’s touched turns to garbage. His state has gone to hell, and his environmental work is a disaster.”

Advisers to the governor privately claimed victory … after Trump drew further attention to Newsom, Melanie Mason and Camille von Kaenel report.

“The more Trump takes the bait, the more attention you get in return,” said a Newsom adviser granted anonymity to speak about the political considerations of the trip. “But it’s much bigger than that. It’s about bringing attention to the fact that what Trump is doing is out of step and it’s not normal and it’s not the American view. It certainly does not reflect what the American people want.”

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: BIG THREE — Yet another internal poll shows Eric Swalwell, Katie Porter and Tom Steyer as the top Democrats in the governor’s race. The latest from Xavier Becerra’s campaign has them tightly bunched, with Swalwell at 10 percent and Porter and Steyer at 9 percent. To the extent any candidate’s private poll is to be believed, this survey is good news for Steyer, who has lagged Porter and Swalwell in recent surveys.

Becerra was behind them at 6 percent, and his campaign found his name recognition among respondents planning to vote for a Democrat was comparatively low — trailing all three Democratic polling leaders and Antonio Villaraigosa. That could be viewed as a vulnerability less than four months out from the June primary, but his campaign manager in a memo cast it as a strength, arguing his unfamiliarity with voters, cash on hand and his opponents’ negatives give him a higher ceiling than his competitors.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: FIREFIGHTER FINANCE — The California Professional Firefighters have endorsed Lt. Gov. Eleni Kounalakis in the state treasurer’s race, with President Brian Rice saying she will “invest responsibly in infrastructure and wildfire resilience.”

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: LEGISLATORS LINE UP — Assemblymembers Jesse Gabriel and Chris Rogers are endorsing California chief service officer Josh Fryday in the lieutenant governor’s race.

DEAL OR NO DEAL — The clock is running out on the dwindling Colorado River — and this time, the West may be headed for court. The rules dividing the West’s most important waterway between seven states, including California, expire at year’s end, and negotiators remain far apart. “They seem inclined to roll the dice a little bit and trust other outcomes rather than negotiating a deal,” said Jeff Kightlinger, the former general manager of Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, which delivers Colorado River supplies to 19 million people.

Kightlinger, who was a key part of the landmark 2007 Colorado River agreement, is advising the Imperial Irrigation District, California’s other main user of Colorado River water, in the current negotiations. Read his interview with Camille in California Climate.

JESSE JACKSON DIES AT 84 — The Rev. Jesse Jackson, a civil rights activist known for his rousing oratory who became the first African American candidate to have a plausible path to winning the presidency, has died, David Cohen writes. His success in the 1984 and 1988 Democratic primaries ushered in a new era in Democratic Party politics.

CHANGING TUNE — Karen Bass told CNN that LA28 head Casey Wasserman should step down from his role leading the Olympic planning organization after the Los Angeles mayor previously avoided doing so. The Olympic committee backed Wasserman last week after several Los Angeles officials called on him to resign over his ties to Jeffrey Epstein.

PELOSI WADING IN? — Los Angeles Times columnist George Skelton writes that he was told Nancy Pelosi will soon endorse Swalwell in the governor’s race. Swalwell launched his campaign with a video featuring Pelosi, and he was recently endorsed by Sen. Adam Schiff, a close ally of the retiring former House speaker.

HIGH-SPEED RAIL ARREST — California High-Speed Rail Authority CEO Ian Choudri was one of two people arrested this month at his Folsom home following an alleged domestic disturbance between his fiancee and his daughter, The Sacramento Bee reports. An attorney for Choudri told The Bee the Sacramento County District Attorney’s Office “immediately” declined to file charges.

AUDIT AXE — California lawmakers killed dozens of proposals that the nonpartisan state auditor had recommended passing, according to a CBS News California analysis. Newsom vetoed at least a dozen more auditor-backed bills.
