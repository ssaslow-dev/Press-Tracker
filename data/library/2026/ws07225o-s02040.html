Now that California legislators are back from their summer recess, a host of LGBTQ bills await their fates in the Legislature. A deadline looms next week for bills with fiscal costs to survive votes in the appropriations committees, while the two legislative chambers have until August 31 to send legislation to Governor Gavin Newsom to sign into law.

One bill establishing a permanent online youth suicide prevention training program was enacted last month by Newsom, a Democrat in his final year as governor, with a second bill addressing suicide ideation among LGBTQ young people still pending before lawmakers. (See related story.) They are among 21 bills dealing with LGBTQ concerns that the Bay Area Reporter has been tracking this year.

Similar to past legislative sessions, this year’s LGBTQ bills package in Sacramento is again skewing toward addressing the needs of queer and transgender young people. Senate Bill 934 authored by gay Senator Scott Wiener (D-San Francisco) would allow people who were subjected to the debunked practice of conversion therapy in which they try to change their sexual orientation or gender identity to have more time to bring a civil suit against the practitioner.

It has taken on more relevance following the U.S. Supreme Court this year ruling 8-1 to strike Colorado’s ban of the practice based on free speech grounds. It also ordered lower federal courts to review if similar bans like the one adopted by California are also unconstitutional. The Golden State in 2012 was the first to ban the use of conversion therapy in minors for the purpose of changing their sexual orientation, with two dozen states following suit.

No matter what the court decides, Wiener’s bill would clarify that plaintiffs could cite scientific principles and consensus around proper medical care to demonstrate conversion therapy’s harm in bringing forward a malpractice claim. It would also extend the deadline for doing so, with someone under 18 years old at the time of being subjected to the therapy allowed to file a claim before age 40.

For people older than 18 when they underwent conversion therapy, they’d be able to file a claim within 10 years of their last session. A person would also be able to bring a claim within five years of their discovering an injury or illness due to the efforts to change their being LGBTQ.

“We can’t allow this awful Supreme Court decision to jeopardize the life of even a single child,” stated Wiener as his bill moved through the review process this spring. “Conversion therapy is psychological torture, and every major medical association has declared it to be quackery. At a time when many powerful people are trying to erase the LGBTQ community, I’m grateful to my colleagues in the Legislature for having our backs.”

SB 1328 introduced by lesbian Senator Sabrina Cervantes (D-Riverside) would require California’s colleges and universities to designate a confidential employee at satellite campuses, branch campuses, and outreach centers to support the needs of LGBTQ+ students, faculty, and staff. It follows on a bill passed in 2024 that required such a person on the main campuses of higher education institutions.

SB 998 by Senators Lena Gonzalez (D-Long Beach) and Akilah Weber Pierson (D-San Diego) would among its provisions require the state’s Office of Civil Rights to appoint an LGBTQ+ discrimination prevention coordinator to develop educational materials for school teachers, staff, governing board and body members, administrators, and other local educational agency personnel so they can identify and proactively prevent LGBTQ+ discrimination at their school sites.

Intersex youth and adults would be provided protection under Assembly Bill 1876 authored by Assemblymember Dawn Addis (D-Morro Bay). It would clarify that intersex traits, as well as gender identity and other characteristics, are included under state law prohibiting sex discrimination by health plans and insurers.

All of the bills are before the appropriations committee in either the state Assembly or Senate. Several bills have been sent to the committees’ suspense files over the last month, raising doubts about their being voted out on August 13, as often such bills are blocked from moving forward even if they previously had gained support.

One is AB 1578 authored by gay Assemblymember Corey A. Jackson, Ph.D., (D-Moreno Valley) that would require elected school board members and other public office holders across California to complete at least one hour of anti-hate speech training and education within six months of taking office and subsequently every four years thereafter. The requirement would begin on January 1, 2028, if his bill becomes law.

SB 1149 authored by Senator María Elena Durazo (D-Los Angeles) aims to allow workers to take bereavement leave for chosen and extended family members, similar to previous legislation Durazo saw become law that extended paid family leave provisions to workers in need of caring for chosen or extended family members, not merely their immediate family. It was sent to the Assembly’s suspense file in July.

Also sent the chamber’s suspense file was SB 1023 by gay state Senator John Laird (D-Santa Cruz) aimed at addressing hiccups with insurance policies covering the cost of an injectable form of the HIV prevention medication PrEP. Some health plans restrict injectable PrEP coverage to their medical benefit for policyholders, which causes problems for nonprofit service providers that provide it via their own pharmacies in recouping their costs for doing so.

Joining the suspense file Monday were two bills authored by gay Assemblymember Rick Chavez Zbur (D–Hollywood). Under AB 1967 county social workers would need to immediately review petitions filed by LGBTQ or straight youth seeking to enter the state’s foster care system due to being abused or neglected at home and determine whether to open a juvenile dependency case within 21 days. It also would ensure young adults ages 18 to 21 are not blocked from extended foster care when they are no longer receiving support and are at risk of homelessness.

“For many youth in foster care, extended foster care can be the difference between stability and homelessness,” stated Zbur upon the bill being sent to the Senate. “These young people are often navigating homelessness, unsafe living situations, unemployment, mental health challenges, and disruptions to education or healthcare all at once. California has a responsibility to make sure that youth in foster care can access the support systems designed to help them transition safely into adulthood.”

Zbur’s AB 1930 aims to strengthen state privacy protections for confidential medical information. It comes amid politically motivated investigations being initiated by the Trump administration and Republican officials in other states who are targeting providers of abortion and gender-affirming care in California and other states.

AB 1930 would require business entities in California to notify the California attorney general before responding to a subpoena or inquiry regarding health care they provide that is legally protected in the Golden State. The attorney general would also be allowed to intervene in any civil action brought by a state business entity that faces a legal inquiry or summons related to the care they provide.

“Patients seeking reproductive or gender-affirming health care deserve privacy, dignity, and the freedom to make personal medical decisions without fear of political intimidation,” stated Zbur upon the bill’s passage out of the Assembly earlier this year. “As extremist politicians across the country attempt to criminalize legally protected health care, California must continue standing firmly on the side of patients, families, providers, and the rule of law. AB 1930 strengthens our ability to protect people seeking lawful medical care in our state from politically motivated attacks and invasions of privacy.”

Placed on suspense this week was AB 1775 by gay Assemblymember Chris Ward (D-San Diego) that would allow access to state programs by transgender veterans being forced out of the military under an executive order issued by President Donald Trump last year. It would make sure such service members discharged under the policy would still qualify for housing assistance and professional licensing support afforded to them by the state, as well as be able to utilize discharge upgrade services as they transition back to civilian life.

Also sent to the suspense file this week was AB 1836 co-authored by Ward and Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino). It would allow nonprofits that host community events such as Pride celebrations and other LGBTQ community events to seek funding upward of $500,000 for security measures via the state’s Nonprofit Security Grant Program. It currently covers nonprofit facilities but not off-site events put on by such entities.

Other health bills under consideration

Several other pieces of legislation related to health issues await votes by the appropriations committees. Lesbian state Senator Caroline Menjivar (D-San Fernando Valley) is trying to pass a condom access in schools bill after seeing former versions of her legislation be vetoed by Newsom. Her SB 608 would ensure students in grades 7-12 can get the prophylactics from school-based health centers. It also would prohibit a retail establishment from furnishing nonprescription contraception to a person solely on the basis of age.

SB 1114 from gay Senator Christopher Cabaldon (D-Yolo) would strengthen privacy protections for sexual orientation, gender identity, and intersex data by limiting when state agencies can share the SOGI information. It would ensure the LGBTQ data cannot be disclosed outside of California state government, except under limited circumstances.

AB 2164 authored by Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan (D-Orinda) would prohibit California’s governor from recognizing a request for extradition of a person related to their providing a legally protected health care activity in the Golden State such as gender-affirming care except under certain circumstances.

Bills change focus, stall

Two bisexual legislators gut and amended their bills from their initial focus. Senator Sasha Renée Pérez (D-Pasadena) changed her SB 957 from mandating safety policies be in place for California users of online dating apps to now require social media companies to notify users when they have received an administrative subpoena.

Set to be heard Wednesday by the Assembly’s appropriations committee, SB 957 has been dubbed the Stopping Harmful Information Exploitation and Lawless Data Sharing (SHIELD) Act. It would place restrictions on how such companies respond to subpoenas and allow the state’s attorney general to intervene in such matters.

“People should be able to use social media to stay informed and keep one another safe without worrying that their activity could result in retaliation from the federal government,” noted Pérez. “No one in this state should be intimidated into silence out of fear that their personal information will be secretly shared with federal authorities.”

In noting its support for the bill, the California Legislative LGBTQ Caucus pointed out that “recent reporting and research have highlighted how social media platforms collect and infer deeply sensitive information about users, including data related to gender identity, sexual orientation, and personal beliefs. In the wrong hands, this information can be used to target, harass, or even endanger LGBTQ+ individuals.”

Assemblymember Sade Elhawary (D-South Los Angeles) altered the focus of her AB 2551 from being about LGBTQ health data to instead be about protests on college campuses and was placed on the suspense file Monday. Meanwhile, her AB 2014 dealing with gender-based bias in court proceedings was held in committee in May, as was Jackson’s AB 2119 aimed at addressing gender bias in sexual assault and domestic violence investigations.

Gay Assemblymember José Luis Solache Jr. (D-Lynwood) had hoped to move forward his AB 908 aimed at monitoring compliance among school districts with a state law requiring the adoption of instructional materials inclusive of LGBTQ history. It stalled last summer and didn’t move forward this year.
