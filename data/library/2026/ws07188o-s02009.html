California bill to classify Jews as an ethnic group heads to Newsom

Asaf Elia-Shalev

California is on track to become the first state to classify “Jewish” as an ethnic identity for the purpose of collecting demographic data, after lawmakers gave final approval to SB 1387 in the closing hours of the 2026 legislative session on Aug. 31.

The votes on the bill weren’t close. Lawmakers in both houses overwhelmingly supported the legislation, which would require state agencies to list “Jewish” as an option on government forms that give Californians the choice of selecting their ancestry or ethnicity.

The bill now heads to the desk of Gov. Gavin Newsom, who has until Sept. 30 to sign it into law or veto it. If he does nothing, the bill becomes law without his signature.

With an estimated 1.2 million Jews, California is home to the second-largest Jewish population of any state, trailing only New York. 

Existing law recognizes Jewish only as a religious identity, which supporters of the bill call a misclassification. They say the bill is needed to ensure Jewish Californians are accurately counted in state data, helping track everything from antisemitic hate crimes to disparities in public health and education.

“Good data makes good policy, and this bill means hate crime reports, health surveys, and education data will finally reflect who we actually are,” David Bocarsly, CEO of Jewish California, an advocacy coalition of more than 40 groups, said in a statement. 

The bill drew support from many Bay Area Jewish organizations including the Jewish Federation Bay Area, Jewish Family and Children’s Services, Jewish Silicon Valley and the Bay Area Center to Counter Antisemitism.

Not everyone in the Jewish community backs the bill. Opponents, including two California chapters of Jewish Voice for Peace, Berkeley’s Beyt Tikkun Synagogue and the Workers Circle of Northern California, argue the measure is unnecessary and potentially risky, pointing to the historic use of lists of Jews to target them.

If the bill becomes law, the new Jewish category won’t take effect until Jan. 1, 2029, giving agencies such as public schools, the state employment service and prisons time to update their forms and data systems. Agencies can seek an exemption only if compliance would cost them federal funding. The timeline mirrors a 2025 law, AB 91, which added similar categories for Middle Eastern and North African ancestries, including Israel, and is on the same 2029 schedule.

Five other bills backed by Jewish California also made it to the governor’s desk, marking an overwhelming success rate for the coalition this session.

Lawmakers approved the Safe Worship Zone Act, AB 2664, which creates a 100-foot buffer around house-of-worship entrances in which no one may approach a congregant within 8 feet without consent. Modeled on a Colorado law protecting reproductive-health clinic patients, the bill drew backing from a wide interfaith coalition and came as a reaction to protests outside Los Angeles synagogues in 2024 that left congregants feeling harassed and intimidated. It defeated critics concerned that it would restrict free-speech and protest rights near religious sites that also host political events.

“The rise of antisemitism is undeniable, and our communities must be protected,” Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan, the bill’s author and East Bay representative, said in a statement. “AB 2664 gives communities an important tool to push back against harassment and intimidation and protect the sanctity of our houses of worship.”

Lawmakers also passed AB 1853, which bars threatening, false and misleading information from California voter guides. It was introduced after a candidate statement containing antisemitic conspiracy theories and white nationalist rhetoric was mailed to 23 million households ahead of the June primary. 

Also approved was AB 1836, expanding the state’s Nonprofit Security Grant Program to cover off-site events like Pride parades and outdoor religious gatherings such as menorah lightings.

Lawmakers passed AB 395, requiring state agencies and public colleges to make a “good faith, reasonable effort” to avoid scheduling major events on religious and cultural holidays. It marks the first time that Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur and Passover have been written into California statute. 

“For too long and in too many communities, religious minorities and other people of faith have been asked to make difficult choices on the days that matter most to them,” Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, the bill’s author, said in a statement. Gabriel, who represents San Fernando Valley, chairs the California Legislative Jewish Caucus.

Lawmakers also approved AB 1763, guaranteeing K-12 students an excused absence for religious holidays, removing a requirement that parents get prior written approval.
