From allowing survivors of conversion therapy to sue practitioners to improving suicide prevention services for youth struggling with their sexuality, this year’s LGBTQ bills package in Sacramento is again skewing toward addressing the needs of queer and transgender young people. The legislation is among the nearly two dozen bills being heard in 2026 that deal with the concerns of LGBTQ Californians.

HIV prevention will receive renewed attention in the coming months from state legislators, as gay Senator John Laird (D-Santa Cruz) is carrying a bill to ensure insurance companies aren’t putting up roadblocks for people who want an injectable form of the HIV preventative medication known as PrEP. It is one of several LGBTQ health bills moving forward this year.

Employment time off policies for LGBTQ families will also be given another look via Senate Bill 1149 authored by Senator María Elena Durazo (D-Los Angeles). It would allow workers to take bereavement leave for chosen and extended family members, similar to previous legislation Durazo saw become law that extended paid family leave provisions to workers in need of caring for chosen or extended family members, not merely their immediate family.

And transgender veterans being forced out of the military under an executive order issued by President Donald Trump last year would have access to supportive services offered by the state should Assembly Bill 1775 by gay Assemblymember Chris Ward (D-San Diego) become law. It would make sure such service members discharged under the policy would still qualify for housing assistance and professional licensing support afforded to them by the state, as well as be able to utilize discharge upgrade services as they transition back to civilian life.

“California has a responsibility to step in when the federal government turns its back on people who served honorably,” stated Ward. “These service members are being forced out of service not because of misconduct or performance, but because of who they are.”

Under SB 934 authored by gay Senator Scott Wiener (D-San Francisco), people who were subjected to the debunked practice of conversion therapy in which they try to change their sexual orientation or gender identity would have more time to bring a civil suit against the practitioner. It comes amid expectations that the conservative-packed U.S. Supreme Court will strike down state bans on the disputed practice being used with minors.

“Make no mistake: conversion therapy is psychological torture, and California will always stand strong with LGBTQ youth to protect them from the extremist movement to harm them,” stated Wiener. “With conversion therapy bans facing legal threats from right-wing extremists and a hostile Supreme Court, we must create new strategies to protect LGBTQ youth.”

In 2012, California was the first state to ban the use of conversion therapy with minors for the purpose of changing their sexual orientation. Two dozen states have since followed, though those bans are likely to be overturned when the justices this spring issue their ruling in Chiles v. Salazar.

No matter what the court decides, Wiener’s bill would clarify that plaintiffs could cite scientific principles and consensus around proper medical care to demonstrate conversion therapy’s harm in bringing forward a malpractice claim. It would also extend the deadline for doing so, with someone under 18 years old at the time of being subjected to the therapy allowed to file a claim before age 40.

For people older than 18 when they underwent conversion therapy, they’d be able to file a claim within 10 years of their last session. A person would also be able to bring a claim within five years of their discovering an injury or illness due to the efforts to change their being LGBTQ.

“Survivors struggle with deep scars from this treatment for years and years, and they deserve the right to justice for what has been done to them. SB 934 gives them the right to hold charlatans accountable for this debunked torture,” stated Wiener.

With the Trump administration last year ending the specialized services LGBTQ youth could access when calling the federal suicide prevention hotline 988, gay Assemblymember Mark González (D-Los Angeles) has authored AB 1540 to restore the dedicated counselors under California’s 988 hotline. Known as the “Press 3” option, it was used by 73,000 California callers between July 2024 and last June before it was deactivated.

"I am not waiting for this federal administration to realize what they've done," stated González. "Lives are on the line right now, and every day we wait, more LGBTQ+ youth are pushed into crisis. If we stand by, thousands could be lost to suicide.”

Should AB 2003 by Assemblymember Marc Berman (D-Menlo Park) become law, then an online suicide prevention training program would be permanently provided to K-12 students, teachers, administrators, and parents. Berman had secured funding in the 2018 state budget for such a training offered to middle and high school students and staff from June 2020 to December 2024.

“Too many communities across California have been devastated by youth suicides, including my hometown of Palo Alto. We must do everything we possibly can to prevent these tragedies,” stated Berman.

Under AB 1967 authored by gay Assemblymember Rick Chavez Zbur (D–Hollywood), county social workers would need to immediately review petitions filed by LGBTQ or straight youth seeking to enter the state’s foster care system due to being abused or neglected at home and determine whether to open a juvenile dependency case within 21 days. It also would ensure young adults ages 18 to 21 are not blocked from extended foster care when they are no longer receiving support and are at risk of homelessness.

“Young people who reach out for help are often doing so at a moment of crisis,” stated Zbur. “This bill ensures that youth in foster care – who are disproportionately LGBTQ+, Black, and Native American – receive timely support and crucial interventions required to care for youth and keep them safe.”

SB 1328 introduced by lesbian Senator Sabrina Cervantes would require California’s colleges and universities to designate a confidential employee at satellite campuses, branch campuses, and outreach centers to support the needs of LGBTQ+ students, faculty, and staff. It follows on a bill passed in 2024 that required such a person on the main campuses of higher education institutions.

After it stalled last year, gay Assemblymember José Luis Solache Jr. (D-Lynwood) has revived his AB 908, dubbed the LGBTQ+ Inclusion and Fair Treatment in Schools (LIFT) Act. It would require that the state’s Department of Education monitor compliance among school districts with a state law that requires they adopt instructional materials inclusive of LGBTQ history.

“This measure aims to address the disproportionate challenges our LGBTQ+ students face and safeguard supportive, inclusive environments in our schools,” noted Solache.

Elected school board members and other public officeholders across California would need to complete at least one hour of anti-hate speech training and education within six months of taking office and subsequently every four years thereafter under AB 1578 authored by gay Assemblymember Corey A. Jackson, Ph.D., (D-Moreno Valley). The requirement would begin on January 1, 2028, if his bill becomes law.

“Our communities continue to face rising hate and hostility, and the language used by public officials can have real-world consequences,” stated Jackson. “According to the California Commission on the State of Hate, there is direct correlation between harmful rhetoric and increases in hate crimes. AB 1578 will require elected officials across California to receive training on hate speech and its impacts, ensuring we understand the power of our words and the responsibility that comes with public leadership.”

Intersex youth and adults would be provided protection under AB 1876 authored by Assemblymember Dawn Addis (D-Morro Bay). It would clarify that intersex traits, as well as gender identity and other characteristics, are included under state law prohibiting sex discrimination by health plans and insurers.

As advocacy group interACT noted in a letter supporting the bill, it would “protect coverage for gender-affirming care and other care that may be considered not to align with the sex category recorded by the patient’s insurance (like cervical cancer screenings for someone assigned male at birth) – a frequent reason that intersex patients experience denials of coverage.”

Other health bills introduced
SB 114 from gay Senator Christopher Cabaldon (D-Yolo) would strengthen privacy protections for sexual orientation, gender identity, and intersex data by limiting when state agencies can share the SOGI information. It would ensure the LGBTQ data cannot be disclosed outside of California state government, except under limited circumstances.

“Federal agencies, including but not limited to ICE, are demanding information from states like ours so that they can target, deport, kidnap, and otherwise assault Americans of all types, immigrants and LGBTQ Americans including transgender Americans especially,” noted Cabaldon, referring to Immigration and Customs Enforcement. “That is why I am introducing Senate Bill 1114 which will protect that data that is so critical to serving Californians, protect it from being used by the federal government to target us. Privacy is fundamental to dignity, safety, and equality, and SB 1114 helps ensure those protections remain strong for LGBTQ+ communities.”

Zbur’s AB 1930 aims to strengthen state privacy protections for confidential medical information. It comes amid politically motivated investigations being initiated by Republican officials in other states who are targeting providers of abortion and gender-affirming care in California and other states.

It would require business entities in California to notify the California attorney general before responding to a subpoena or inquiry regarding health care they provide that is legally protected in the Golden State. The attorney general would also be allowed to intervene in any civil action brought by a state business entity that faces a legal inquiry or summons related to the care they provide.

“AB 1930 protects transgender patients and patients who receive reproductive health care services and their health care providers,” stated Zbur.

Correlated to such legal prosecutions initiated by other state officials, AB 2164 authored by Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan (D-Orinda) would prohibit California’s governor from recognizing a request for extradition of a person related to their providing a legally protected health care activity in the Golden State such as gender-affirming care except under certain circumstances.

Bisexual Assemblymember Sade Elhawary (D-South Los Angeles) with her AB 2551 seeks to have health care service plans conduct annual surveys of their enrollees that track expenditures paid out-of-pocket by enrollees for out-of-network behavioral health care services and in-network behavioral health care services. The surveys would need to ask SOGI questions and be in place by October 1, 2027, if her bill becomes law.

Bills address safety matters
Nonprofits that host community events such as Pride celebrations and other LGBTQ community gatherings would be allowed to seek funding upward of $500,000 for security measures via a state grant program under AB 1836 co-authored by Ward and Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino). The state’s Nonprofit Security Grant Program currently covers nonprofit facilities but not off-site events put on by such entities.

Elhawary aims to require judges of criminal proceedings when determining the admissibility of evidence at trial “to consider that undue prejudice includes, among other things, the possibility that the trier of fact will rely on gender-based stereotypes related to sexuality, parenting, gender expression, romantic relationships, appearance, or assumptions about a person’s emotional or intuitive nature.” It requires a two-thirds vote to be passed out of the Legislature due to the California Constitution’s Right to Truth-in-Evidence provision.

“Too often, our communities have faced bias in courtrooms where arguments focus on how someone dresses, who they love, or how they express themselves rather than the facts of the case,” noted Elhawary. “AB 2014 ensures judges take a closer look before allowing these kinds of assertions into the court so that bias does not influence the outcome of a trial. Our courts should focus on the facts and evidence of what actually happened, not on assumptions about someone’s identity.”

Bisexual Senator Sasha Renée Pérez (D-Pasadena) introduced SB 957 to require online dating apps with users who live in California have safety policies in place to protect them on or before January 1, 2028. They would also need to submit annual reports thereafter to the state attorney general about their safety policies.

“Online dating is firmly established as a popular way for people to meet, but the safety standards behind these platforms have clearly not kept pace,” stated Pérez. “Californians deserve transparency, accountability, and basic protections when they use apps or websites that facilitate intimate, personal interactions. This legislation would ensure companies take responsibility for user safety instead of leaving people to fend for themselves.”

And AB 2119 by Jackson aims to address gender bias in sexual assault and domestic violence investigations. It specifies that “every victim of sexual assault or domestic violence has the right to a fair, unbiased, and complete investigation by law enforcement and the collection of all relevant evidence by law enforcement regardless of the victim’s gender, gender identity or expression, sexual orientation, age, race or ethnicity, disability, immigration status, or relationship to the alleged perpetrator.”

Under the bill, victims of such crimes would be able to request a reinvestigation of their reported incident by the district attorney if they are unhappy with how a law enforcement agency handled the matter. The bill would create a civil cause of action if a law enforcement agency violated these rights and would make that cause of action retroactive to investigations made before January 1, 2027.
