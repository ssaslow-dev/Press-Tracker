Prison Is Not a Field of Dreams

As I write this from inside the San Quentin Rehabilitation Center, I do so with a great deal of respect for what it means to pass time and a memory that stretches long beyond the razor wire. When a new legislative cycle begins, there’s always a bill that makes me suspicious, a new proposed policy that is dressed like hope. Assembly Bill 2204, authored by California State Assemblymembers Isaac Bryan and Jesse Gabriel, is a perfect example. It promises expanded access to sports programming for incarcerated people, wrapped in the language of opportunity, wellness and second chances. On paper, it sounds progressive. But from the steel bed where I sit, it’s far more complicated than that.

For three years, from 2017 to 2019, I trained for and ran marathons with San Quentin’s 1000 Mile Running Club. Instead of running on open roads, we ran 104 and a half laps around the prison yard. I know first hand the discipline that requires. Waking up early, pushing through the physical pain, staying committed to something long after the motivation starts to fade. My running habits didn’t just shape my body, they reshaped my mind. Training for marathons taught me to focus. It demanded that I hold myself accountable. It gave me a structure that mirrored the life of a responsible human being in society. Every time I finished a marathon, I grew prouder. My best time was 3:45.

Sports can do that. They help build community in a place of isolation. They teach teamwork and help develop emotional intelligence. They teach us how to manage frustration, support others, and how to lose without giving up and losing yourself. The running club gave me all of that.

But it doesn’t help us get out of prison. The California Board of Parole (BPH) doesn’t care about me finishing the marathon or playing baseball. Prison is not a field of dreams. They call it a “rehabilitation center” now, but it’s a place of nightmares kept at bay by high walls.

AB 2204 would establish the Second Chance Fund Act, authorizing the California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation (CDCR) to solicit proposals and enter into contracts to expand sports programming in prisons across the state. The title alone suggests the hope of redemption, a movement or path forward. But inside prison walls, I’ve learned to analyze these policy proposals beyond their titles.

BPH has enormous control over the incarcerated population’s futures. It has the power to grant our parole or deny it for a period of 3, 5, 10 or even 15 years. The BPH doesn’t measure rehabilitation by our jump shots or number of touchdowns. They don’t care how many laps we run around the prison yard track or how well we play Pickleball. They are looking for something completely different when we stand before them, hoping to be released: insight into our own criminal behavior, accountability for the harm we’ve caused and participation in programs that directly address the factors that led to our crimes. Sports, with all their value, carry zero weight.

CDCR’s stated vision, mission and goals center rehabilitation and restorative justice. Yet real rehabilitation has always been treated like a limited resource and a privilege, not a fundamental right. A small fraction of CDCRs $14.2 billion budget – they claim five percent, although haven’t publicly released details about how that money is spent – goes toward the programs that help people change. Many of us are serving time for serious offenses, including murder, robbery, burglary and rape. We need high-level support, resources and tools that address our trauma and teach us to manage anger, overcome substance abuse and end cycles of domestic violence and sexual abuse. Those of us who want to do that work to heal ourselves and take accountability for our actions often can’t access the resources we need. The programs that address serious crimes are limited, oversubscribed – waiting lists for cognitive behavior therapy and other proven programs can stretch for months, sometimes years – or nonexistent.

If we’re serious about public safety, reducing harm and preventing future violence, access to rehabilitation and treatment programs that actually reduce people’s risk of reoffending should be a guaranteed right. Allocating public dollars so we can play sports makes people feel good, but it does nothing to reduce the prison population or prepare people for reentry.

And there are other problems with programs like the ones the Second Chance Fund would support. They can have insidious side effects that outside people have little to no visibility into. Sports can become another lever for the prison to further control the population, a privilege that can be granted or withheld. They can also open the door to exploitation. Bringing in professional sports clubs under the banner of rehabilitative programming risks turning incarcerated people into assets. Our participation creates good optics for the prison system and often benefits the outside organizations running these programs. Both CDCR and the outside groups benefit from our presence more than our progress.

When a bill like AB 2204 appears, inviting new sponsors and outside contractors to compete for funding, it raises a concerning question: What is our priority? Is it to use the prison system to make society safer by preparing incarcerated people to reenter their communities? Or is it a further investment in the status quo of the prison system itself?

I’m not against sports. They always have and should continue to exist here. I personally know their benefits and how they can shape a man. But they cannot, on their own, transform the system. If California is serious about second chances and making communities safer, it has to invest in the hard work of real rehabilitation. A five percent investment is not enough to help the tens of thousands of long-term offenders who remain warehoused in California prisons look inward, take responsibility and change.

AB 2204 plays games with incarcerated peoples’ futures. Policies that fund feel-good, one-off programs like this look good on paper, but allow the state to avoid fulfilling its true public safety obligation. California should first pass a bill that transforms rehabilitation from a privilege to a right. Maybe then, I’d be more willing to play ball.
