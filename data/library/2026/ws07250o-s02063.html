Federal lawsuit challenges California Glock‑style handgun ban

Noe Padilla Brian Day James Ward

USA TODAY NETWORK

July 1, 2026, 6:12 p.m. ET

California’s latest effort to restrict the sale of Glock-style handguns is facing a new legal challenge after the Trump administration’s Department of Justice sued the state on July 1, arguing the law violates the Second Amendment. The lawsuit escalates a growing battle over firearms regulation in a state long known for having some of the nation’s strictest gun laws.

At the center of the dispute is Assembly Bill 1127, a first-in-the-nation law that took effect July 1 and prohibits licensed firearms dealers from selling certain semiautomatic pistols that state lawmakers say can be easily converted into fully automatic weapons using illegal devices commonly known as “Glock switches.”

The federal lawsuit seeks to block enforcement of both AB 1127 and California’s handgun roster, which limits which handguns can be sold through licensed dealers in the state. Acting U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche said California lawmakers cannot ban what he described as “the most popular type of handgun in America.”

Gov. Gavin Newsom and state officials, however, argue the measure is a common-sense public safety law designed to curb the growing use of illegally modified firearms.

The federal lawsuit is likely to become the latest major test of California’s gun laws in the post-Bruen era, when courts increasingly scrutinize firearm restrictions under the Second Amendment.

With the DOJ, the NRA, the Firearms Policy Coalition and other gun-rights organizations all challenging aspects of California’s handgun regulations, the fight over AB 1127 appears headed for a lengthy court battle that could ultimately help define how far states can go in regulating firearms that remain legal elsewhere in the country.

For now, the law remains in effect, and licensed California firearms dealers are prohibited from selling affected Glock-style pistols while the legal challenges move through federal court

What is California's Glock ban?

AB 1127 does not specifically name Glock, but it targets semiautomatic handguns that use a cruciform trigger bar design and can be readily converted into fully automatic weapons through the installation of a conversion device.

Those devices, often called Glock switches, are already illegal under federal law and can transform a handgun into a machine gun capable of firing multiple rounds with a single pull of the trigger. Lawmakers behind the bill argued that manufacturers have known about the vulnerability for years but failed to redesign affected firearms.

Beginning July 1, licensed firearms dealers can no longer sell newly acquired Glock-style handguns that fall under the law. Existing owners may keep their weapons, and private-party transfers remain legal. Sales to law enforcement agencies are also exempt.

Why the Department of Justice is suing California

The Trump administration contends California's law bans commonly owned firearms based on the possibility that someone could illegally modify them.

In court filings and public statements, DOJ officials argue the state is prohibiting legal firearms because they could potentially be converted into illegal ones through the addition of a separate, already-banned device. They also challenge California’s handgun roster, saying it restricts access to firearms that are widely used for lawful purposes.

The lawsuit follows a June warning letter from Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon, who accused California of violating the constitutional rights of residents seeking to purchase lawful firearms.

Gun-rights groups have also launched separate legal challenges, including the federal case Jaymes v. Bonta, arguing that the Supreme Court has repeatedly ruled that firearms in common use cannot be banned.

California says the law targets machine-gun conversions, not lawful gun owners

Supporters of AB 1127 say the measure targets a specific public safety threat rather than ordinary firearm ownership.

Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, a Democrat from Encino and one of the bill’s authors, has argued that California is closing a loophole that allows firearms to remain on the market despite their susceptibility to conversion into fully automatic weapons. Lawmakers say the law is intended to encourage manufacturers to redesign affected firearms rather than prohibit handgun ownership outright.

There are still hundreds of handguns available for purchase in California through the state’s handgun roster, and AB 1127 does not affect people who already own Glock pistols. Dealers who violate the law face escalating penalties, ranging from fines to license revocation and potential misdemeanor charges

Newsom points to falling crime and gun death rates

Newsom quickly pushed back on the federal lawsuit, calling it another attempt to dismantle California’s gun safety framework.

The governor said California is experiencing historically low crime and gun death rates and pledged to continue defending laws designed to keep dangerous weapons off the streets.

Those comments came as California Attorney General Rob Bonta released new criminal justice data showing the state’s homicide rate declined between 2024 and 2025. Even with that decrease, firearms remained the most commonly used weapon in California homicides, accounting for roughly two-thirds of killings statewide.

Supporters of AB 1127 argue those numbers underscore the need for stricter firearm safeguards, while opponents say constitutional rights should not be limited based on potential misuse by criminals.

High-profile crimes linked to Glock firearms in California

The debate over AB 1127 has been fueled in part by increasing concern among law enforcement agencies about Glock switches and other machine-gun conversion devices.

Supporters of AB 1127 argue that limiting future sales of easily convertible firearms could reduce the availability of weapons that can be unlawfully transformed into machine guns. Gun-rights advocates counter that existing laws already make conversion devices illegal and that banning firearm sales punishes lawful owners rather than criminals.

Federal officials have warned that small conversion devices can transform compatible semiautomatic pistols into fully automatic weapons capable of firing dozens of rounds in seconds. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives has reported recovering tens of thousands of machine-gun conversion devices nationwide in recent years.

California law enforcement agencies have repeatedly cited Glock-style handguns in investigations involving gang violence, organized crime and mass-casualty shootings. Public safety officials say the growing availability of conversion switches has increased concern about the firepower available to criminals.

In 2024 alone, ATF’s Los Angeles Field Division reported recovering over 31,000 machine gun conversion devices nationwide, with a significant portion linked to Glock models in California. These seizures often occur during raids targeting organized crime networks and drug trafficking operations, where Glock pistols are frequently discovered alongside narcotics and other illegal firearms.

Several headline-making cases underscore the role of Glock firearms in violent crimes. For example, in San Francisco, a Glock modified with a conversion switch was used in a drive-by shooting that left multiple victims injured. Similarly, in Sacramento, a Glock handgun was central to a mass shooting outside a nightclub, prompting renewed calls for stricter firearm regulations. Prosecutors have highlighted that the ability to convert these pistols into machine guns easily has made them a weapon of choice for criminals seeking maximum firepower, reinforcing the rationale behind California’s legislative crackdown.
