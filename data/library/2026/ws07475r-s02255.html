(FOX40.COM) — The California Legislature needs a "sincere partnership" with Gov. Gavin Newsom as it works to address the deficit, a top budget leader said Tuesday.

Asm. Jesse Gabriel, chair of the Assembly Budget Committee, said "deep engagement" between lawmakers and the governor is necessary, adding that he expects proactive communication in order to avoid surprises in Newsom's May budget revision.

Gabriel, D-San Fernando Valley, spoke at Tuesday's budget committee meeting. It was the first such meeting after Newsom announced his initial 2026-2027 state budget.

The state cannot fully backfill funding lost as a result of H.R. 1, President Donald Trump's "Big Beautiful Bill," Gabriel said, but he promised that lawmakers will look for ways to help vulnerable communities.

"We cannot ignore the real challenges facing middle class and working families," he said. "But we also refuse to ignore the real fiscal challenges facing our state."

In particular, Gabriel said Newsom's plan doesn't do enough to address issues of housing and homelessness.

"The lack of resources for housing in the governor's budget is impossible to ignore, and it does not reflect our prior commitments or our values," he said. "We can and must do better, regardless of our budget situation."

The committee's new vice chair, Asm. David Tangipa, R-Clovis, said he's deeply concerned about the structural deficits identified by the Legislative Analyst's Office.

"That should give everyone pause," he said.

The LAO's analysis identifies a $18 billion deficit while the Department of Finance's report puts that figure at $3 billion. Legislative Analyst Gabe Petek told lawmakers that's because the governor's projections include $30 billion more in revenue next fiscal year — revenue Petek worries may never materialize.

Much of California's revenue comes from the stock market, which Petek said is benefiting from optimism around AI. But he believes there's "elevated risk" for a market downturn.

Petek said stock prices have gotten so high that the risk-adjusted expected return has shrunk to 1.5% — a level only reached in the summer of 2007 and late 1998, before the Great Recession and the dotcom bust.

"In good conscience, we cannot advise the Legislature to adopt a budget" that doesn't account for risk of a stock market downturn, Petek said.

It's important for the Legislature to adopt plans now to reduce multi-year deficits that could grow as high as $30 billion annually over the next few years, Petek said. He agreed with Gabriel that it's important to act fast and not wait until Newsom's May revision.

Erika Li, chief deputy director of budgets for the Department of Finance, said the Newsom administration views his plan as a "workload budget" — with neither significant new investments nor significant cuts.

Gabriel said what's important to lawmakers this year is the timing of collaboration between the Legislature and the administration.

"If the governor is intending to propose significant solutions to balance your shortfalls, I think it's really important that we have the opportunity to have conversations about those prior to the May revise," Gabriel said.

"The sooner the better," Li replied.
