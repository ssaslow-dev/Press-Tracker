City of Los Angeles

Mayor: Nithya Raman

When the history books are written, what will they say about Karen Bass? How did someone who came into political life as a radical, traveling to Cuba with the Venceremos Brigade and protesting police brutality, find herself here, writing billion dollar checks to LAPD and smiling with Trump as ICE terrorizes her city? How does someone who co-founds Community Coalition go on to lead police crackdowns on the homeless and end needle exchange programs? How does an anti-apartheid activist live to become a genocide denier?

If we knew how, could we innoculate against it in our leaders and ourselves? All we know is that the world of people Bass fought for, and her willingness to stand up to power, shrank further and further. And that brings us here, to the end of her career — a champion for wealthy NIMBYs and the landlord lobby, trading favors with Airbnb, hoping LAPD’s fearmongering bullshit will let her hold on to a mayorship she doesn’t seem to want of a city that she has long since ceased to understand.

We refused to give her our recommendation in 2022. At first the announcement of her campaign was met with relief by Democrats hoping for an alternative to conservative mall-magnate Rick Caruso. It quickly became apparent, however, that she was completely uninformed about the struggles that had come to define LA politics: the rights of the unhoused, the role of LAPD, development and land use. She campaigned on feeding more and more of our budget into punishment and policing while regurgitating right-wing propaganda about an imaginary crime wave. And she prioritized NIMBYism over desperately needed affordable housing.

As mayor, she found new and unexpected ways to be disappointing. Her signature homelessness program, “Inside Safe,” has spent hundreds of millions of dollars while only a small fraction of its participants ever see permanent housing. Mostly, it serves as an excuse for encampment sweeps. Bass’ entire homelessness program has been focused on reducing visible homelessness rather than actually keeping people housed in the long term. The result has been an enormously expensive and inefficient program that only serves to make Angelenos cynical about the search for solutions.

Under Trump 2.0 Bass has attempted to cast herself as some sort of resistance figure. This is laughable. Days after Trump defeated Kamala Harris in 2024 Bass appointed Jim McDonnell as chief of police — the very same Jim McDonnell whose coziness with ICE led to him becoming the first Los Angeles sheriff to lose reelection in 100 years. This utter lack of concern for immigrant Angelenos is an example of how rudderless her office is.

Meanwhile Bass sat on her hands as LAPD began cracking the heads of anti-ICE and anti-Trump protesters. She took no action to protect her own constituents, who she claimed to support, from shocking violence at the hands of her police. Instead, she fought tooth and nail to give LAPD raises and new positions at the expense of city services — literally, the police defunding the rest of us. 

We haven’t even mentioned her failures surrounding the Palisades Fire, such as choosing to leave town in the face of what was already predicted to be an unprecedented and damaging windstorm. Time and time again she has frozen in the face of emergency — the Palisades fire, the ICE invasion, the Lineage warehouse fire — leaving a void where there should have been leadership. Afterwards there are always platitudes to mouth and scapegoats to find, and a city to pick up the pieces for itself.

Karen Bass has failed as mayor. It is time for her, and Los Angeles, to move on.

We urge you to vote for Councilmember Nithya Raman, whose run offers the possibility of a better Los Angeles. In many ways she was the prototype for all of the left’s electoral victories since 2020, winning a dramatic upset over incumbent David Ryu and putting the torch to the old assumptions of LA politics. She was the first to come into office seeking answers to the housing crisis, and she showed that there was a huge constituency for compassionate solutions to homelessness rather than cops and cages.

Nithya also has helped achieve major victories during her time in office, such as passing and then, with an expanding progressive bloc, strengthening the Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance. Her team has also been excellent on treating the unhoused with dignity and working to get people into housing and care rather than relying on violent sweeps, and she has been a consistent advocate for the right to counsel in eviction cases. Without her we certainly wouldn’t have the strongest package of renter protections this city has seen in 40 years. Her team set the model for staffing progressive offices and her housing and homelessness staff have kept her ruthlessly prepared and are lockstep with her in her strategy on building power as a minority progressive voice on council.

As the election has played itself out she seems to have found a voice that many (us!) had been worried she had lost, calling for the expansion of unarmed crisis response and the firing of LAPD Chief McDonnell. She has been quick to call out waste and abuse in the police budget, voting against Bass’ blank checks for LAPD. She is also partnering fruitfully with Controller Kenneth Mejia, promising him the resources and freedom to audit many of the city’s most important projects — authority denied by Mayor Bass. 

Nithya has also clashed with the left in the past, most recently over the implementation of the mansion tax Measure ULA, and understanding those clashes tells us a lot about the frustrations and pitfalls we might see in a Raman administration. During the campaign she has often been compared to Mayor Zohran Mamdani of New York, particularly as her social media game has developed. Beyond the DSA endorsements and Indian heritage, however, the two are arriving at the work from very different places. Zohran comes from an organizing background, often citing the taxi drivers’ hunger strike as a pivotal moment in his political consciousness. He understands the inevitability of failing and falling short as he moved to the mayorship, and the role of the movement in keeping the pressure on him to fulfill his promises. That conflict, between the movement and the institution, is part of how he sees his work as developing.

Nithya comes from a policy and academic background, not from organizing. She was brought into politics not just from compassion for the unhoused, but from running the numbers, doing the analysis, and coming to an intellectual understanding of how the city was failing. She’s in many ways a technocrat, looking to subject experts to figure out what needs to be done. In that framework the continued demands of the movement aren’t seen as creating room for her to work. Instead they seem like an annoyance — Why are these people yelling? Don’t they know what the experts say? Don’t they know we’re doing as much as we can? This is a self-destructive approach to conflict, and one which pushes away allies and creates blind spots. Reports and academics, after all, are not the sole sources of knowledge, particularly when it comes to implementation.

What will a Raman administration look like? Will she be able to navigate the crises facing Los Angeles in the next four years: El Niño and climate disaster, another ICE gestapo invasion, the looming chaos of the Olympics, Hollywood consolidating under right-wing oligarchs? Time will tell. But we know our best chance to build a better Los Angeles, here in 2026, is with Raman in the mayor’s office.

Council District 3: Barri Worth Girvan

As we wrote in the Primary Voter Guide, the candidates running in CD 3 offer the same flavor of nothing to residents of the San Fernando Valley — just more cops and policing and a crackdown on homelessness. However, with Celona knocked out of the race, we’ll take a closer look at Barri Worth Girvan and Tim Gaspar.

Tim Gaspar is working hard to position himself as the “public safety candidate,” and with endorsements from just about every police union in LA County, it does seem he’ll win that distinction. His public safety plan is straightforward: more money for more cops and more surveillance technology. He does talk about “stopping crime before it starts,” but don’t be fooled: he’s not talking about affordable housing and higher wages, things that actually stop crime. Nope, he’s talking about programs like the LAPD Cadets — brainwash them young!

Barri Worth Girvan’s public safety platform is vague, but has the same underlying current of more money for cops. She wants to “modernize” the city charter to press for accountability, which sounds promising — but without details, it isn’t something we can put our support behind. 

On homelessness, Girvan has more to say, mostly about cutting red tape and pushing for more accountability for homeless service providers. But, again, she doesn’t talk about how she plans to do that. Gaspar, on the other hand, clearly wants to throw every homeless person in jail with a “zero tolerance” policy on encampments, saying “If help is refused, we’ll use every legal tool to clear the encampment.”

Neither Girvan nor Gaspar are candidates that the Valley truly deserves, but they are, unfortunately, the current available options. We recommend a vote for Girvan, as Gaspar’s absolute adoration of LAPD is something we need much less of in City Council.

Council District 9: Estuardo Mazariegos

Curren Price is lucky to be terming out of office, considering he’s currently on trial for 12 felony counts of corruption. While Price has been councilmember since 2013, CD 9 has been the victim of disinvestment for decades. The district contains the second-largest unhoused population in LA and is home to many Black Angelenos and recent immigrants, and needs a working-class champion to bring resources back into these neighborhoods.

Estuardo Mazariegos is the candidate for the job. An immigrant from Guatemala who grew up in South Central, Estuardo has devoted his life to his community. Over two decades of organizing, he has risen to co-director of Alliance of Californians for Community Empowerment (ACCE), one of the most effective working-class groups in the state and a key part of the coalition behind victories such as the creation of the Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance. Mazariegos has spent his career fighting for the often-neglected constituencies of low-income renters to have a voice at City Hall and in Sacramento. 

Mazariegos’ messaging has focused on the wage and rent exploitation by the billionaire class that extracts wealth from CD 9. His decades of both organizing experience and community-led policy advocacy means that his platform is deeply informed by conversations with tenants, workers, and marginalized people in the district. His primary goals are to ensure safe, accessible streets; build affordable housing and protect renters; and create green space and climate improvements in CD 9.

Jose Ugarte, on the other hand, is cut from the same cloth as predecessor Curren Price, whose office he is currently employed by. Ugarte failed to disclose years of income from the lobbying and political consulting firm that he ran at the same time he worked for Price, leading to a $25,000 fine from the Ethics Commission. Ugarte’s limited policy platform includes more homelessness sweeps and 41.18 zones, more bans on sleeping in vehicles, and more LAPD patrols — in short, more criminalization of poverty. He also wants to hire more women and people of color as police officers so the people brutalizing you look like your neighbors.

The choice cannot be clearer: vote for Estuardo Mazariegos.

City Attorney: Marissa Roy

Marissa Roy, deputy attorney general, is the clear and necessary choice. Previously a lawyer with the city attorney and now with the state attorney general, she has extensive litigation experience and the vision to transform the office into a leading force for progressive change. Roy aspires to be a city attorney for the people, using the power of the office to target polluters, slumlords, wage theft, and the Trump administration — which she does not hesitate to call fascist.

City attorney is one of the most important but least understood elected positions in Los Angeles. On the one hand, the office acts as the prosecutor for misdemeanors and has a direct policy impact on the criminalization of poverty and homelessness. On the other, the office is the counsel for the city as both defendant and plaintiff in civil lawsuits. The city attorney also advises City Council on the law and puts proposed ordinances into legal language. It does not take much imagination to see how much potential the office holds… or how badly it could go off the rails. 

The incumbent city attorney, Hydee Feldstein Soto, is one of the most unqualified and incompetent officials in the entire state of California. Her term in office has been marked by abuses of power, humiliating scandals, and ballooning liability payouts that bleed the city budget. This led to her utter defeat in June, knocked out of the general election with less than 20% of the vote. 

Now Roy is in a runoff with District Attorney John McKinney, a latecomer who previously made a failed run for district attorney in 2024 and who was there to absorb the police union’s money when a data breach led to LAPD abandoning the incumbent. McKinney boasts three decades of experience at the DA’s office, which he falsely claims makes him the more qualified candidate. On the contrary, his tough-on-crime posturing — absurd in an office that can only prosecute misdemeanor offenses — reflects a man who had given no thought to what the office entailed before he filed to run. 

In May, he offered the Los Angeles Times this inanity: “McKinney, 58, said he wants more aggressive prosecutions for misdemeanor gun crimes, and believes the city attorney has the power to ‘leverage’ homeless people into mental health or addiction treatment after they’ve been arrested.”

McKinney is, in fact, unqualified for the office — not only has he clearly never considered the specific role of the city attorney, he has not one single day of civil law experience, in an office that is the primary civil litigation firm for the second largest city in America. 

His attempts to patch together a platform read like a game of charades — piecing together “police liability settlement” and “environmental lawsuit” from a consultant’s frantically waving hands. Let’s not fool ourselves. We’ve seen the upshot of an unqualified city attorney — under Feldstein Soto, liability payouts have increased 35% every year, to a crushing $287 million.

What this vitally important office needs is someone who understands the issues and the stakes. Only Marissa Roy has a clear plan to reduce our liabilities but also change the way the office operates. As a former city attorney and someone who has been frankly obsessed with the office for the past six years, Roy understands the power of the office to shape policy, to protect the budget, to advocate for the resident, and to find the windfalls in corporate law enforcement. She has the ability to speak convincingly to any audience without hedging her principles, and knows the value of education and politics that are needed to make progressive policy last.

What we do not need is another “tough prosecutor” who, like Hydee, thinks that glaring angrily at shoplifters can substitute for the management of the legal needs of the entire city of Los Angeles. That sort of leadership has blown a hole in our city budget and left us defenseless against the illegality of the Trump administration.

Marissa Roy is the only candidate in this race who understands the office, who treats it with the seriousness it deserves, and who has a clear vision of how to manage it to the benefit of the city. In many ways, her campaign is one for basic fairness and justice under the law. What if the theft of money from a worker were treated as seriously as the theft of money from a corporation? What if laws around pollution, or predatory lending, or tenant harassment were treated as seriously and pursued as aggressively as sleeping on the street? The choice to treat laws that punish the poor as sacrosanct and laws that restrict the rich as optional is a political one. 

We get to make a different choice. Vote for Marissa Roy.

LA County

Sheriff: Robert Luna

Sheriff Luna has maintained a subdued status quo over his first term, keeping himself out of the headlines and focusing on rebuilding the many bridges burned by his predecessor, neckless fascist Alex Villanueva. This quiet approach has paid off as a management strategy, but it hasn’t addressed the department’s deep-seated problems; in many ways it seems that the goal of his tenure has been to cut down on scandalous headlines rather than fix underlying issues. And, during last summer’s protests against the Trump regime’s mass deportation policy, LASD dealt out horrific violence to peaceful demonstrators, gassing and beating without restraint, and Sheriff Luna explicitly lied to the media to justify it.

Lucky for Luna, his opponent in this runoff is neckless fascist Alex Villanueva. The former sheriff’s disgraceful record hardly bears repeating: baselessly investigating political opponents, defying subpoenas, protecting deputy gangs, hiring and promoting abusers, building an unpermitted helipad at his house, and on and on and on. LA’s dollar-store Trump spent the last four years fuming fruitlessly in front of his webcam to an audience of dozens, and has crawled out of his man cave for a campaign that amounts to nothing more than a resentment tour.

Judge of the Superior Court

In Los Angeles, judges are elected to six-year terms in countywide races, which in a county of 10 million people ensures that no candidate can reach more than the tiniest fraction of the electorate. This means that incumbents are impossible to beat unless they’re up to their necks in scandal, and even then a judicial PAC protects even the most loathsome among them. Judicial hopefuls go to war over the open seats — futures are often decided by a game of musical chairs at the County Registrar, leaving a lucky few running unopposed. This is obviously a great system and a credit to democracy.

Finding meaningful information about the candidates also poses an enormous challenge. Your average campaign website has a list of endorsements, some vague language about fairness and the law, and a smiling photo of a person in a suit. That’s why we’re here. Our recommendations are based on dozens of conversations with progressive lawyers and organizers.

Part of the purpose of this guide is to push back against the biases built into what little information is available. The Bar Association, for example, rates candidates on their “qualifications” in a manner that privileges prosecutorial experience over defense or civil work. In the race for Seat 64, for example, they rated Public Defender Haymon as only “qualified” with 26 years of experience, and District Attorney Ghobadi as “well qualified” with 17.

Many other publications also fall for the fiction that “nonpartisan” judicial races are politically neutral. The police unions certainly don’t believe that: they endorse the judges they think will be most friendly to an agenda of criminalization and imprisonment. We also see the intervention of groups like the Crime Survivors PAC, a Trumpist Republican group that prospective judges list proudly on their websites. Even without those extremes, there is a meaningful difference between a judiciary made up of white male ex-DAs and one with a diversity of cultural, class, and professional backgrounds.

Seat 64: DPD Rhonda Haymon

Rhonda Haymon is an experienced trial attorney who boasts more than 25 years as a public defender. In our research we heard both pros and cons: praise for her grit and determination as an advocate, criticism of her ability to work collaboratively. She has the endorsements of Judges Holly Hancock, George Turner, and Ericka Wiley, who won election as part of a movement to expand the diversity of background and experience in the judiciary; she also is endorsed by La Defensa, who helped power slates of progressive judicial candidates in previous cycles. She is highly skeptical of automated “risk assessment” tools, and emphasizes the need to uphold and protect constitutional rights even when they go against the preferences of an individual judge. We recommend her for this seat.

Seat 65: Anna Reitano or Justin Clayton

This is a rarity: a race with no bad choices. 

In the four-way primary we recommended a vote for Reitano, who as part of the original Defenders of Justice slate had the campaign experience to break through and ensure a good candidate in the runoff. The electorate did all of us a favor — no matter what happens, we’ll get a decent result in Seat 65. 

Anna Reitano is an attorney with Los Angeles County Counsel and a former public defender. She emphasizes the breadth of her experience in law as preparing her for an office that might take her to civil trials, family court, or any other branch of the legal arena. During her time as a public defender she emphasized the importance of mental health diversion and treatment.

Justin Clayton is a career public defender and deputy in charge at the Inglewood office. He touts his leadership and management history that will help him control a courtroom, and highlights the need to protect the rights of litigants across language and cultural barriers, vital in a city of over a hundred spoken languages.

Both Reitano and Clayton boast more than a decade of heavy courtroom and trial experience with the public defender’s office. Both have a wide range of endorsements. It’s a shame that two qualified candidates are stuck in competition for a single office.

Seat 87: DPD Anthony Bayne

Anthony Bayne boasts a quarter century of experience at the public defender’s office, including 19 years at the Compton courthouse in roles ranging from defending the most severe felony offenses to the supervision of new misdemeanor attorneys. His broad experience with the courts at every level puts him head and shoulders above his competition for the seat.

Seat 131: DAPD David Ross

For those who don’t know, the county has two public defender offices. The alternate public defender, where Ross has worked for more than 25 years, handles cases where there are multiple defendants or conflicts of interest between them that would disqualify the public defender — the same work, but under a different banner. Before becoming an attorney, he spent a decade as a journalist. He has an excellent resume and a commitment to the people left behind by our system. In interviews he impressed with his thoughtfulness and temperament. He’s both the progressive choice and the most qualified candidate.

Public Defender Donna Tryfman is also in the runoff. She faced allegations of verbal abuse and misconduct during her previous campaign for Beverly Hills School Board, where she ran on a platform of forcing teachers back into the classroom against COVID-19 guidelines. She has also been accused of harassing coworkers who expressed opposition to the genocide in Gaza. We do not believe that she has the character or impartiality to serve on the bench.

Neighboring LA Cities

Burbank

Burbank has a solid progressive bloc of electeds to maintain in 2026, to the benefit of everyone in LA County who can look to these leaders as a blueprint. As a center of the entertainment industry – and home to Warner Brothers, recently bought up by Skydance – Burbank is sure to be a center of political action in the next cycle. Three progressive candidates are running for the three seats available on Burbank City Council.

Mike Van Gorder

We are back again with another recommendation for Mike Van Gorder, who ran in the previous election in a slate with Konstantine Anthony. His platform is refreshingly straightforward on the policies he will be advancing to protect tenants (makes sense since he is a founder of the Glendale Tenants Union and formerly a housing policy analyst for SEIU Local 1000), immigrants, bike and street safety, and small businesses and jobs. Van Gorder’s 2026 campaign has kept his slate of progressive endorsers from his first campaign and added institutional supporters. He has been a strong advocate for entertainment workers and unions – given the major anti-labor changes the film and TV industry has been entertaining, Burbank would do well to bring a good advocate into office and elect Mike Van Gorder.

Nicole Jane Perez

Former Burbank Mayor and councilmember Nicole “Nikki” Perez was Burbank’s first Indigenous and openly LGBTQIA+ councilmember. As a Burbank native with a social work background, her tenure in office has been marked by an impressive array of pro-worker/pro-tenant policies and programs aimed at keeping Burbank affordable, inclusive, and safe. She introduced a ban on Automatic License Plate Readers (including Flock) and has mobilized resources to Burbank residents impacted by ICE. Perez’s continued experienced leadership will be crucial under these federal circumstances.

Nolan Southerland

Nolan Southerland has positioned his campaign to be a promising addition to the Burbank squad. His platform commits to continuing his advocacy as a member of the Burbank Tenants Union and 15 year member of IATSE local 700 through the powers of the seat. Southerland has also garnered the endorsement of City Controller Kenneth Mejia, Burbank elected Kontantine Anthony, and notably the LA Labor Fed, a very rare get for first time candidates. Southerland will be another experienced voice in the pro-entertainment worker bloc on council and his priorities include protecting immigrants, auditing and reviewing military-grade law enforcement equipment, and bus and bike friendly infrastructure.

Santa Monica

Santa Monica City Council (3 Seats): Mayor Caroline Torosis, Danny Ivanov, and Brett Morrow

Santa Monica chooses three new councilmembers for at-large seats this November. There are three solidly pro-tenant options up for election.

Mayor Caroline Torosis has been a champion for renters, establishing Right to Counsel for tenants facing eviction, supporting long-term solution building policies like tenant opportunity-to-purchase which gives tenants a first bite at the apple to buy their building when it goes on the market. She was formerly a Rent Control Board member and has defended Santa Monica’s rent stabilization. 

Santa Monica’s pro-renter slate also includes Chief Communications Officer for the Los Angeles County Department of Public Health Brett Morrow who articulated nuanced housing and development positions which center non-displacement of existing tenants and current Rent Board chair Danny Ivanov who while strong on tenants issues could improve on wealth taxation, immigration, and policing. For their united pro-tenant agenda, and progressive endorsers, they should all have your vote.

Santa Monica Rent Stabilization Board (3 Seats): Sue Himmelrich, Heather Thomason, and Kurt Gonska

Similarly to city council, Santa Monica’s Rent Stabilization Board also has three open seats with a good selection of candidates.

Sue Himmelrich was a volunteer attorney for the Legal Aid Foundation of Los Angeles, major supporter of Measure GS and GSH and is currently Special Counsel at the Western Center on Law & Poverty. Himmelrich also has a strong pro-tenant record in public office for Santa Monica. Her incredible resume fighting for tenants makes her an easy recommendation.

Heather Thomason’s campaign for Rent Stabilization board comes from the experience of being displaced multiple times as a renter. Her story and commitment to renters is well laid out in her Santa Monicans for Renters Right’s questionnaire. She is a WGA member, renter, strong advocate for rent control, and non-displacement. 

Kurt Gonska is running for re-election on the Rent Stabilization Board. Also a strong candidate but not as clear on ULA style Measure GS “taxes prevent development” as we would like. 

Soledad Marcial is also running and it’s worth noting that her website is in English and Spanish. She is from Pico and it is great to see Pico represented through a candidate in this race.

She has also moved quickly to bolster West Hollywood’s sanctuary city ordinance and vote down Flock contracts. An easy, strong recommendation for Chelsea Byers, so tell your WeHo friends.

Krieger does invoke active partnership with law enforcement but her approach to Flock cameras shines some light into her perspective on public safety and privacy. She proposes that WeHo might develop its own digital privacy policy but it falls short of being a stalwart opponent to the intrusion of corporate and surveillance architecture in our lives. For a city with historic queer and immigrant communities, we would prefer to see a stronger position on this. 

Walkability and bikeability are major concerns for her as well as development that suits the need for more affordable housing units while tailoring the approach to the neighborhood. Her experience living in New Orleans through Hurricane Katrina and advocating for the development of affordable housing in the aftermath is part of what set Krieger on this path.

Stephen Post

With a strong backing of progressive west side and city wide organizations, Stephen Post has demonstrated that he is paying close attention to the advocates of LA and is serious about effective solutions that put people first. His housing justice approach includes models of social housing, like public housing, and expanding the affordable housing that is so deeply needed in LA. His work with cannabis decriminalization and clear positions on ICE and Flock shows real concern over the needs of targeted and marginalized communities, as opposed to criminalization-for-the-sake-of-public-safety theater. All this in addition to his considerable advocacy lobbying and experience on governmental bodies is a promising sign for people hoping to see a thriving, progressive, West Hollywood.

Ballot Measures

Statewide

State Measure 1: Yes

The approval of Proposition 1 would allow the state to sell $11.25 billion in bonds in order to fund affordable housing programs. The majority — $10 billion — would go towards homeownership programs, farmworker housing, affordable student housing, tribal housing, and other initiatives. If voters approve it, the bond sale is expected to finance up to 40,000 multifamily rental units and assist another 40,000 households with buying a home. The remaining $1.25 billion would specifically be allocated to veteran housing.

It is no secret that California’s housing crisis is beyond concerning. Creating financial support for affordable housing, rent-burdened households, and vulnerable populations is a step towards remedying the absolute inhumanity of more than 187,000 people being unhoused in the state of California.

State Measure 2: Yes

If approved, this proposition would amend the California State charter to move 20% of general fund tax revenue into reserves — also known as the Budget Stabilization Account (BSA) — increasing the ceiling from the previous 10%. Approval would also authorize spending tax revenue to pay down the state’s federal unemployment insurance debt, totaling $20 billion, through a change in how “excess” capital gains revenues are calculated. The proposition would change the relationship between deposits into the BSA and the state spending cap, commonly called the Gann Limit. Put in place by voter-approved ballot initiative in 1979, the current spending cap law limits growth in state and local government spending funded by tax revenue and requires that the government will remit funds to taxpayers when the limit is exceeded. Under current law, deposits into state reserves are counted as dollars spent and dollars withdrawn are not counted as spending. The approval of Proposition 2 would codify the inverse: deposits into reserves would not count as spending, while withdrawals would. 

More money in California’s savings account is a good thing. Period. This proposition is opposed by right-wing groups like Reform California, an ultra-conservative PAC that is chaired by conservative activist and sentient frown line Carl DeMaio. So take from that what you will.

State Measure 3: Yes

This ballot measure extends a tax on the top 2% of income earners, first enacted in 2012. The new measure, titled The California Children’s Education and Health Care Protection Act, will make permanent a range of income taxes for high-earning individuals, beginning at 10.3% for people making $360,000 per year and capping at 12.3% for those making $721,000 or more per year. The overwhelming majority of revenue collected from this tax — which totals up to $15 billion annually — funds California’s public schools. The remaining 11% goes to community colleges and any extra is allocated to public healthcare. 

Allowing this tax to expire would cause up to a 10% budget cut for California public schools. One in six public school teachers would face termination as a result. Essential programs, like free lunch initiatives, would be cut, just to fund a handout to the wealthiest Californians. Our recommendation needs no further explanation: vote yes.

State Measure 4: Yes

Prop 4 would end a ban on public financing for campaigns and allow local governments to build programs that provide political candidates with public funds. State law allows charter cities to distribute public funds for campaigns, but not state and county governments. If passed, Prop 4 would require the creation of expenditure limits and other criteria for candidates seeking public funds. Campaigns would be prohibited from using the money for legal defense fees, fines, or repaying personal loans. 

This proposition doesn’t create a public financing plan, but makes it possible for us to fight for the creation of an equitable system. In a world where money still sways elections, public financing has been a powerful tool for working people to participate in politics. If you follow Los Angeles politics — which you probably do, you’re reading Knock LA — you’ve seen matching funds make it possible for progressive challengers to compete with the big money of the Los Angeles Police Protective League (LAPPL) or the landlord lobby. At the same time, many of our favorites in other cities or running for state legislature have been swamped by wealthy interests.

Prop 4 would help us open our elections to candidates who do not have personal fortunes or a network of rich donors. Vote yes.

State Measure 5: Yes

Under current California law, a special election to select a successor occurs at the same time as a successful recall removes someone from office. This bizarre system means that a much smaller plurality of voters can drive a recall and replace a majority-elected official. Prop 5 would require a public office to remain vacant after an official is recalled rather than the position being filled by a simultaneous election. It would also allow said recalled officials to run again for the office they were recalled from if there is a special election, and the measure would authorize the lieutenant governor to step into a gubernatorial vacancy if the governor is recalled. 

Recalls are a tool that conservatives and right-wing activists love to haul out when they’re upset about the policies set by Democratic lawmakers, or even the simple fact of their existence. Unsurprisingly, the California Republican Party came out in opposition to Prop 5, because the current anti-majoritarian recall system is essentially their only chance to capture a statewide race. Anti-progressive, cave-dwelling Reform California is also among this proposition’s opponents. 

This is a basic measure to make our elections more rational and more small-d democratic.

State Measure 37: Yes

Election season is not complete without at least one mysteriously worded ballot measure about homeownership. In that way, Prop 37 provides. 

If passed, Prop 37 would create a second mortgage program for qualified homebuyers on qualified homes, with $25 billion in bonds to fund the program. “Qualified homes” are defined as newly constructed homes, townhomes, row houses, condominiums, and residences in nonresidential buildings — key words: “newly constructed.” Proponents of Prop 37 include, unsurprisingly, realtors and the construction trades. The mortgage under this program would cover up to 17% of the purchase cost, essentially pushing the down payment to 3%. The most likely beneficiary, then, would be a household with stable middle-income work but without the savings for a down payment. 

While this can provide some real benefits for homebuyers without ready capital, it’s not a real solution for the housing crisis. Think of it as an industry subsidy, providing demand-side funding to encourage new construction; realtors like it because it increases home sales, unions like it because it increases construction work.

We’re not opposed to this proposition, but it is hardly a cure for the housing crisis.

Prop 43 is about limiting the ability for new taxes to be proposed and enacted. State Measure 44: No

A tough one in terms of parsing the language in its text. If passed, the measure would require federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) to spend at least 90% of their annual revenue on advancing the center’s mission. “Require community health clinics spend 90% of revenue on program services” seems great. This measure will likely result in clinic closures and the loss of services in vulnerable areas.

State Measure 45: No

The sales pitch on 45 is reform of the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA), a 1970 state law that requires public agencies to analyze, disclose, and minimize the significant environmental impacts of proposed discretionary projects before approving them. If passed, the proposition would shorten the review process for development projects deemed “essential,” which includes construction of housing, power infrastructure, and highways, among others.

While CEQA has in some cases been abused to prevent housing and transit projects, the cure can’t be worse than the disease. Prop 45 is a mix of arbitrary timelines and carve-outs that are designed to serve the most destructive developers rather than improve our state’s built infrastructure. The goal of radically shortening the amount of time for public comment and agency review is meant not to improve the review process but to evade it entirely; the time limits that would be imposed on the courts by Prop 45 have been described as “arbitrary and unrealistically short” by the Judicial Council.

Ultimately Prop 45 is a wolf in sheep’s clothing, using the need for housing and transit to fast-track highway construction and AI data centers.

Measure E: Yes

This one is vitally important. Los Angles City

Measure TE: No

Measure TE is an attempt to create tax exemptions for people whose homes burned in the Palisades Fire should they wish to sell their property. If passed, it would allow these certain residential property owners to be exempt from Measure ULA, which taxes real estate sales that surpass $5.4 million. This exemption would run for five years from the date of the fire, expiring in 2030. 

We want to be clear about something: the loss of a home during a fire is a traumatic and terrible occurrence. The Palisades Fire was a devastating event, and the rebuilding process will be long and difficult. 

We also believe that if passed, Measure TE would set a precedent for wealthy homeowners to opt out of taxes, an option that is not available to people in Altadena who lost their homes in the Eaton Fire. If an individual’s property sells for more than $5.4 million, even as a bare lot, it’s not right to exempt that sale from a tax. $5.4 million is a lot of money for a piece of land without a house on it! To protect the integrity of Measure ULA, we do not support creating carve outs to the mansion tax. The recommendation is no.

Measure LA: Yes

We are fans of this one. If passed, this measure would change the LA City Charter to create capital infrastructure plans and a two-year budget cycle, along with a host of other shifts. Capital infrastructure plans are needed to restore and repair the city’s existing infrastructure. The two-year budget cycle would create more long-term planning, build more financial stability, allow evaluation to occur during off years, and improve oversight. This is a common-sense update that makes the basic functions of the city easier to carry out. We like this. The city needs this.

Measure PL: Yes

This measure is quite technical and of limited scope. If PL passes it would consolidate the Area Planning Commissions and let certain additional decisions be made by the City Planning Department. It would also prevent City Council from killing projects by refusing to put them on agenda, a favorite bribe-inducement strategy of former councilmember and current federal inmate José Huizar. Essentially, the measure streamlines certain planning elements and removes some opportunities for corruption or tomfoolery. 

PL is supported by the Engineers & Architects Association, the union that represents city planners. Vote yes.

Measure EE: Yes

EE is a grab bag of charter reforms. A “yes” vote grants more authority to LAPD’s Inspector General and LAFD’s Independent Assessor, civilian oversight bodies designed to audit the departments and investigate claims of misconduct. It would also raise the penalties for campaign finance violations, something that grassroots progressives could benefit from.

Measure EE also brings down the number of required weekly City Council meetings from three to one. Some feel that would reduce accountability, but we don’t agree; if anything, the current system makes it easier to miss meetings or hide motions in the shuffle.

Another part of EE would raise the threshold for referendums to overturn a City Council action to 15%, similar to voter initiatives. This is not a desirable change but its impact is small enough that it is outweighed by the other parts of the measure.

Measure PRK: Yes!

We love this one. This measure, if passed, would amend the LA City Charter to change the minimum budget allocated to the Department of Recreation and Parks. To put it simply, more money for parks! Los Angeles desperately needs this. Our parks department has struggled to staff and maintain our existing parks, to say nothing of expanding the amount of green space in the city. With our weather we deserve to be a city of world-class parks. Let’s have more playgrounds, more bathrooms, more concerts and programming, and more beautiful spaces to enjoy.

PRK is opposed by the LAPPL, because “How dare you want something nice? What about 300 new cop cars instead?”

Measure PRT: Yes

Not a very consequential measure unless you care a lot about airport commissions. If passed, it would allocate funds for waterfront public access projects and would also require people serving on Airport Commissions to live near the airport (though it doesn’t particularly define “near”). This is a fairly minor change. Don’t lose sleep over it.

Measure FD: Yes

This one is simple. Using a 0.5% sales tax (raising the sales tax from 10.25% to 10.75%), Measure FD would raise funds for the LA Fire Department. No surprises here. As the climate crisis grows and dangerous fire conditions continue to increase, more funding for the fire department is a positive. Los Angeles faces horrific wildfire risks across a huge territory, and we need to grow our fire department staff and facilities and take proactive steps to prevent fires and harden our infrastructure.

Measure FD is primarily opposed by the LA Police Protective League, which is investing millions against it for reasons that seem unclear; as best we can tell it’s a mixture of spite and anti-tax sentiment. They might want to think twice — if the fire department misses out on this revenue, folks might start looking at the LAPD budget sooner rather than later. 

We do need to offer our recommendation with some caveats. First, a sales tax is a regressive tax. Lower income Angelenos will pay more as a percentage of their income than the rich. Second, we simply cannot fund everything forever by carving out fractional cents of sales tax and earmarking it for different departments; this is not a way to manage a budget, and will eventually hit the limit of its usefulness. 

In the long run, we need to talk seriously about the budget, particularly the huge proportion of it eaten up by LAPD and the hundreds of millions in liability claims they face every year. We need to talk about Prop 13 and the hole it has blown in municipal governance in California. And we need to talk about billionaires and concentrations of massive wealth.

In the meantime, we will offer a reserved “yes” on FD.

Measure SC: Yes

This one is quite specific. If passed, it would do what it says on the tin: clarify campaign finance rules for Board of Education elections by making them match the same rules as the Mayor. That’s fine. It could be interesting in the future if voters also pass Prop 4 — maybe matching funds could be extended to the School Board race as well.
