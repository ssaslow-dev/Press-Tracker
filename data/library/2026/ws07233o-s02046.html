DRIVING THE DAY — California Democrats on Thursday evening forcefully rejected the Department of Homeland Security’s claim that 190,832 noncitizens are registered to vote in the state, arguing the administration offered no evidence to back up the explosive allegation.

Trump’s “latest election conspiracy is nothing but a political stunt built on sham numbers that no one should trust,” Alex Padilla, California’s senior senator and former secretary of state, said in a statement. “DHS hasn’t explained how it arrived at these figures, how it obtained the underlying data, or whether any of these individuals are actually noncitizens who actually cast a ballot.”

DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said his agency found thousands of non-citizens on voter rolls in California and three other states during a preliminary review and drafted a press release making the claim, John Sakellariadis and Maggie Miller reported.

The press release, which did not say whether there was any evidence of voting by non-citizens or explain how DHS reached its finding, was circulated inside the agency ahead of Trump’s planned prime time speech in which he had promised to reveal “really big news” on election security.

The announcement immediately raised questions about what records DHS relied on. California has so far succeeded in stopping the Trump administration from accessing its voter rolls. A federal judge in January dismissed a lawsuit the Department of Justice filed against Secretary of State Shirley Weber after her agency wouldn’t turn over the records the administration requested.

The DOJ has appealed the decision, and oral arguments took place before the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals in May.

“The California Secretary of State has not sent the federal government our statewide voter registration list in any format,” said DeDee Verdin, a spokesperson for the Secretary of State’s Office, in an email. “We have successfully fought the U.S. DOJ’s unlawful demand for a copy of our entire [unredacted] statewide voter registration database.”

“Given that no court has ruled in favor of U.S. DOJ’s efforts to access states’ unredacted voter rolls out of 15 cases that have reached a decision, we are optimistic that the Ninth Circuit will uphold the State’s ability to protect the sensitive information of California’s 23 million registered voters,” Verdin added.

Trump alluded to that challenge in an address Thursday evening, claiming his administration’s estimate was likely an undercount because states had refused to hand over their voter rolls.

Padilla said DHS’ “lack of transparency also raises serious questions about whether it violated state law to obtain California’s voter registration records.”

A spokesperson for the Los Angeles County Registrar said in a statement that “we have not received or provided information to the federal government regarding non-citizens on the rolls in Los Angeles County.”

Asked for comment, a spokesperson for Gavin Newsom pointed to a social media statement from the governor noting that only citizens are legally allowed to vote in state and federal elections in California.

“Voter fraud is EXTREMELY RARE — and almost always committed by U.S. citizens,” Newsom wrote. “Donald Trump’s Administration has repeatedly pushed false and misleading claims about elections. They have provided NO evidence to support these new ‘claims.”’

Marc Berman, a Democratic state lawmaker whose 2021 legislation made universal vote-by-mail permanent, said the Department of Homeland Security should instead focus on Trump’s baseless theories about his 2020 election loss.

“If Secretary Mullin wants to investigate voter fraud in America, I suggest he start with his boss,” Berman said in a statement. “The only person who has been caught demanding that elections officials make up thousands of votes is President Donald J. Trump, who begged the Georgia Secretary of State to ‘find 11,780 votes’ to steal the 2020 presidential election.”

RO-ING — In the span of a week, Ro Khanna went from recriminations over his support for Graham Platner to sparring with the Israeli government over his detention in the West Bank and back to endorsing a new progressive Senate candidate in Maine.

It was peak Khanna — so much so that, at one point, he referred to himself as a “long-shot presidential” candidate.

The Silicon Valley lawmaker, a likely presidential contender, has long been known on Capitol Hill for his progressive politics and outspoken style. But now Khanna is offering for broader viewership an early glimpse into how that 2028 campaign may play out: Take major, headline-grabbing swings, and always double down on the next news cycle.

Critics cast him as a publicity hound, and he’s become a regular target of criticism from President Donald Trump, trillionaire Elon Musk and Israel Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

But where they see showboating, Khanna argues he’s offering a playbook for a more aggressive kind of Democratic politics that progressive voters long for — embracing tough fights that challenge the status quo around wealth inequality and foreign policy.

“I’m willing to take on the elite power structures of this country,” Khanna told Playbook. “And I’m willing to do it at a risk. That’s a lesson for Democrats in the attention economy: Have guts, take bold, clear stands that aren’t a word salad.”

Khanna’s headline-snatching sprint started last week with the implosion of Platner’s campaign for the Senate in Maine after POLITICO reported an allegation of sexual assault against Platner, an allegation he denied. Khanna had been one of Platner’s most vocal defenders amid a swirl of earlier controversies.

Khanna, after withdrawing his support and saying he “should’ve listened to (his) wife” about Platner, swiftly waded back into the Maine Senate contest, endorsing former Maine state Senate President Troy Jackson, another progressive who’s also backed by Bernie Sanders.

Within days, Khanna was sparring with Netanyahu and top Israeli officials after he said he had feared for his life when he was detained by Israeli settlers and soldiers during a visit to the occupied West Bank.

The incident made international headlines and emboldened Khanna and other progressives who’ve pushed to stop military aid. Israeli officials disputed aspects of Khanna’s account, but he pushed back — and in spicy fashion.

“Free advice to the Israelis: It’s not a good idea to detain long-shot presidential candidates,” Khanna told The New York Times. “Not how you’re going to build good will with the next American president, whoever that is.”

U.S. Ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee called “the whole thing a stunt,” and various Israeli and Republican officials accused Khanna of staging a publicity gimmick to boost his presidential odds. A prominent Jewish group in Khanna’s backyard, the Jewish Community Relations Council of the San Francisco Bay Area, accused him of “using the backs of the Jewish community to gain the limelight.”

It’s the latest in what’s been a punchy year for Khanna. Last fall, he was a key player in Congress’ effort to force the release of the Epstein files and he sparred with Silicon Valley titans over his support for California’s proposed billionaires’ tax. There was also his tweet showdown with Musk earlier this summer.

Khanna said he has no plans to temper himself, adding that he’s still considering running for president despite being an “underdog” in early polling.

“Traditionally, people look for charismatic big personalities to charm them and win them over with magnetism and sizzle,” he said. “My view is these times call for something different.”

ISRAEL FALLOUT — Democratic House candidate Eric Jones is slamming his opponent, longtime Rep. WASHINGTON

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: ANTI-AID — Gregg Phillips, who attracted controversy as a top FEMA official and once claimed to have teleportation powers, fought against approving an extension for housing assistance for California fire victims shortly before he was pushed out of the agency, according to three people familiar with the matter.

“He kind of went off the reservation and was not keen on approving it,” said a current DHS official who had direct knowledge of Phillips’ qualms about granting the aid. A former DHS official said there was tension between Phillips and acting FEMA administrator Bob Fenton over the California extension. Phillips was pushing back on approving the California aid the day before he left his FEMA role in late June, according to one of the three people.

The people were granted anonymity to discuss internal deliberations they were not authorized to share.

Phillips pushed colleagues at FEMA for independent audits and reviews for the California assistance extension to be consistent with Trump’s executive order on combating fraud, the three people said. Phillips told colleagues that any grant that required an extension needed such anti-fraud protections, according to the people.

Some 35,000 people in California have received a total of $177 million in housing aid through the main FEMA program for such disasters, and an additional 1,000 people have used another FEMA program for getting such aid. In late June, the agency granted FEMA’s request for a yearlong extension for fire victims, which was due to expire July 9.

Phillips declined to comment. A FEMA spokesperson said that the agency was “grateful for his significant contributions” and said Phillips had stabilized the Office of Response and Recovery. “FEMA remains committed to transparency and integrity in all personnel and program decisions,” the spokesperson said. The spokesperson did not address a question on Phillips’ maneuvering against the aid.

Trump has clashed for years with Newsom over disaster aid. And in 2018, Trump initially refused to give California disaster aid for a massive wildfire but changed his mind after White House aides told the president that the fire-damaged area had supported him in the 2016 election, POLITICO reported. — Daniel Lippman and Thomas Frank

SILICON VALLEY

LOOKING FORWARD— In his two terms as governor, Gavin Newsom has threaded the needle with California’s booming tech sector, an approach that could be put to the test in his final months in office.

Neither an unabashed ally nor outright antagonist to Silicon Valley, Newsom has given the industry wide berth to innovate and grow, a posture that’s helped fuel billions of tech tax dollars into Golden State coffers. In some cases, he’s stepped in to put guardrails on the tech industry, as fears have risen around social media and artificial intelligence.

Now, with bills on chatbots, social media and AI workforce issues moving toward his desk, Newsom faces a choice: Try to stick to the middle path or take a more decisive stand for or against the industry.

“August into September could be the most consequential period of his tenure,” John Bennett, executive director of the nonprofit California Initiative for Technology and Democracy, said in an interview. “What he accomplishes in 2026 [could] define him in the next two years.”

WILL BRAKE FOR FIREWORKS — San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie is asking state autonomous vehicles regulators for changes after numerous Waymos blocked traffic lanes, leading to gridlock for cars, buses and thousands of fireworks viewers in the city on July 4th.

In a letter Lurie asked Toks Omishakin, who leads the California State Transportation Agency, to ensure that AVs won’t break down or run out of battery in traffic lanes — and that AV companies are able to respond quickly and coordinate with local agencies during events and emergencies.

State agencies regulate autonomous vehicle operations in California, with municipalities exercising little official control over their operations.

Lurie, in the letter, acknowledged that Waymo agreed to geofence some areas of the city ahead of July 4th and assigned staff to the city’s Emergency Operations Center. But the mayor said “the events that followed demonstrated that voluntary coordination and pre-event planning alone are insufficient.”

“Manufacturers must be able to assess evolving conditions and rapidly adjust fleet operations in real time,” he wrote. The San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency has apologized for the traffic delays, saying the transit system could not absorb the massive crowds that came out for the fireworks.

The state transportation agency referred questions to the Department of Motor Vehicles, which did not immediately respond to a request for comment. A spokesperson for the California Public Utilities Commission, which regulates commercial autonomous ride-hailing operations, said the agency was reviewing the letter.

Some of the changes Lurie is asking for are reflected in a bill from South Bay state Sen. Dave Cortese. His bill, SB 1246, would require stricter safety and oversight rules for AV companies statewide, while also requiring remote operators to be based in the U.S. with a valid driver’s license, establishing new rules for coordinating with first responders, and creating fines for blocking an emergency response.

In an interview with Decoded, Cortese called the Independence Day situation “predictable.”

“I hope his letter has some influence on the governor so my bill doesn’t get vetoed,” he said.
