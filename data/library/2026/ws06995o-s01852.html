California's new non-UPF label law draws skepticism, ire from industry

Scott Miller

California recently became the first state to establish a certification program for non-ultraprocessed food labeling, but inconsistencies between AB 2244 and existing legislation left food manufacturers questioning which definition of UPFs to use.

At a Glance

• California created the first state certification for non-ultraprocessed foods under AB 2244.
• The new law's UPF definition may conflict with the state’s 2025 Real Food, Healthy Kids Act (AB 1264).
• The voluntary program may have been modeled after USDA organic label.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom may be preparing for a food fight.

On Sept. 28, his office released an announcement about the signing of several important pieces of legislation, some of which will attempt to regulate the food industry. The biggest news was the creation of a labeling program — and the accompanying state certification process — for products that are not considered ultraprocessed foods (UPFs).

Donnelly McDowell, a partner at Kelley Drye specializing in consumer protection, highlighted a hint from the press release: The process might be modeled after USDA organic certification.

“Certification bodies would look at ingredient formulations for products and confirm that they don't meet California's definition for ultraprocessed foods,” McDowell explained. “Where it gets really complicated is thinking about the broader federal and state regulation of ultraprocessed foods because there's no consensus yet on the definition.”

Now, as a federal definition looms, Newsom passed the first state law to create a certification process for non-UPF labeling. The law itself, however, contains some concerning inconsistencies for members of the food industry.

Zero consensus on UPFs

Several programs already exist that offer a non-UPF certification, but consensus on a unified national definition for UPFs remains difficult to find.

California’s new law, AB 2244, was reportedly designed around the definition for “ultraprocessed foods of concern” established in 2025’s “Real Food, Healthy Kids Act,” or AB 1264. The most specific version of this definition can be found in Section 104662 of the California Health and Safety Code.

It starts by clarifying that a UPF means any food or beverage containing 1) one of the substances described within the definition, which includes stabilizers, thickeners and other functional ingredients defined deeper within the legislation; 2) high amounts of saturated fat, sodium or added sugar, or 3) a nonnutritive sweetener or other substance described elsewhere in the law.

Nate Rose, VP of communications and public affairs at the California Grocers Association (CGA), called this a “three-pronged” approach.

In AB 1264, “There’s a three-pronged test to decide if the food is ultraprocessed, and then there are a number of qualifiers that were worked out to try to cover foods that could land in this middle zone,” Rose said. “Healthy foods, right, like Greek yogurt, could contain pectin as a thickener, for example.

“For most of the legislative session, we were working with the author of AB 2244 [assemblymember Jesse Gabriel] under the assumption that they were going to use those exact definitions. But when AB 2244 was amended, those qualifiers were not brought over, just the three-prong test. Now, a number of foods commonly held to be healthy are on an island where they're not considered ultraprocessed foods under AB 1264 — but under AB 2244, they would not qualify for a non-ultraprocessed food label.”

How AB 2244 defines UPFs

The bill downplays its own association with the Real Food, Healthy Kids Act, referencing it as “existing law” that defines UPFs “for certain school-related purposes.” Then it declares that existing law also requires the State Department of Public Health to define, on or before June 1, 2028, “ultraprocessed foods of concern” … the same language used in the Real Food, Healthy Kids Act to reference its own detailed definition.

AB 2244 kicks the can down the road on various promises, setting milestones all the way out to 2040. With this announcement coming right before a contentious national midterm election, California’s last-minute swerve on setting a real consequence behind noncompliance with a strict statewide definition could indicate a political play.

Regardless of which UPF definition this law may use (or promise to define later), AB 2244 leaves critical questions unanswered about what constitutes a UPF — which unfortunately also leaves food developers well beyond the Golden State in the lurch.
