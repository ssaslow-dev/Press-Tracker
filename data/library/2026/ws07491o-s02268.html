Newsom unveils final California budget proposal, projecting smaller-than-anticipated deficit

The budget highlights the governor's priority as he takes on his final term

By Jenny Huh
Published 7:28 PM PST January 9, 2026

SACRAMENTO, Calif. — Gov. Gavin Newsom on Friday unveiled his proposed $348.9 billion budget for the 2026–27 fiscal year, offering a cautious spending plan in what will be his final budget as governor and projecting a significantly smaller deficit than previously forecast.

But what stood out just as much was who wasn't there. The governor did not attend the morning's presentation, and his office did not answer our questions on why.

The proposal anticipates a $2.9 billion shortfall, far less than the $18 billion deficit projected by the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst’s Office, a difference driven largely by stronger-than-expected revenue estimates, according to the Department of Finance.

“The budget doesn’t include new general fund initiatives or expenditures,” said Joe Stephenshaw, the state’s finance director. “It really is a workload budget and taking a very conservative approach.”

Democratic Sen. John Laird of Santa Cruz, chair of the Senate Budget Committee, said the difference stems from updated revenue data unavailable to the legislative analyst and differing assumptions about an economic downturn.

“The legislative analyst didn’t have the benefit of a few months of revenue numbers that the governor had,” Laird said. “And the legislative analysts built in an economic downturn, which the governor didn’t.”

Laird emphasized that the governor’s proposal marks the beginning of a months-long budget negotiation process, during which lawmakers will assess additional revenue data, including income tax receipts due in April.

More than half of California’s general fund revenue comes from personal income and sales taxes, making budget projections particularly sensitive to economic shifts.

“[The state's revenue comes from] personal income, which fluctuates, it's sales tax, which fluctuates, and so that's why there's such volatility in the state budget, and that's why the voters adopted the rainy day fund in 2014 to try to scrape off money in good times to be able to have it there in the bad times.”

Newsom's budget adds $3 billion to that reserve. 

In short, the proposal includes no new spending initiatives and no cuts, maintaining existing programs. 

Under the proposal, roughly 30% of spending would go toward health care, while 26% would be directed to K-12 education. Finance officials said stronger general fund revenues triggered higher required education spending under Proposition 98, which guarantees minimum funding levels for public schools and community colleges.

The budget also absorbs administrative costs tied to new federal eligibility requirements for health care and food assistance programs enacted under the so-called federal “Big Beautiful Bill.” 

However, critics note it does not include additional funding to offset potential benefit losses for Californians affected by federal cuts.

“That’s where the governor’s proposal doesn’t include any response,” said Chris Hoene, executive director of the California Budget and Policy Center, noting that federal changes are expected to reduce benefits for individuals.

When pressed about the matter during Friday's press conference, Stephenshaw countered that there is still lack of guidance by the federal government and that the state will closely monitor updates as they come.

Republicans criticized the proposal as overly optimistic. Sen. Roger Niello, a Republican from Fair Oaks and vice chair of the Senate Budget Committee, said California’s core issue is spending, not revenue.

“The governor’s proposal, I believe, is dangerously more optimistic than the LAO’s, and by a lot,” Niello said. “There’s still a deficit, in any case, that needs to be closed. [Newsom is] just not closing enough of it.”

Newsom’s administration projects the deficit could grow to $22 billion the following year, a figure closer to — but still below — the legislative analyst’s $35 billion projection.

“The place they agree is they agree that there is going to be a big problem after this next year's budget," Sen. Laird noted.

Top Democratic leaders said the budget reflects caution amid economic uncertainty. Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas and Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel said in a joint statement that while revenues are currently strong, the state must prepare for potential downturns by building reserves, strengthening schools and accelerating housing construction.

Senate President Pro Tem Monique Limón echoed that sentiment, saying the budget must prioritize “the safety and fiscal stability of California families.”

The proposal now heads into negotiations, with revisions expected in May and a constitutional deadline to pass a final budget by June 15.
