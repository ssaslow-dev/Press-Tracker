California becomes first state to list ‘Jewish’ as an ethnicity

Niva Ashkenazi

Gov. Gavin Newsom signed a bill Sept. 30 that makes California the first state to officially treat Jewish identity as an ethnicity. The bill, known as SB 1387, adds “Jewish” to a list of about 50 racial and ethnic identities recognized by the state for data collection. 

David Bocarsly, CEO of Jewish California, a statewide coalition of 46 Jewish groups that backed the bill, framed its passage as a major turning point. 

“This is a historic step for the Jewish community,” Bocarsly said in a statement. “Under the shadow of Nazi race theory, American Jewish leaders asked the government to count us only as a religion. That was an understandable attempt at self-preservation, and it didn’t work. Our community was mischaracterized and misunderstood, and we were excluded from interethnic coalitions and the data that shapes policy.”

State Sen. Henry Stern (D-Sherman Oaks), a member of the California Legislative Jewish Caucus, authored SB 1387, which will update state forms such as unemployment claims or hate-crime reports.

Supporters say the law will produce better data for tracking and combating antisemitism and for identifying disparities in health and education. They also say it will more accurately reflect secular Jews who see their identity as ancestral or cultural rather than religious.

Newsom signed the bill over the objections of some in the Jewish community, led by progressive groups including Jewish Voice for Peace and Bend the Arc, which said a single Jewish ethnicity box flattens the community’s diversity, singles out one religion for ethnic status and creates a government record of who is Jewish.

The bill passed in the Assembly’s final floor vote on Sep. 1, with 74 votes in favor and five abstentions. It passed the state Senate on Aug. 31 with 39 votes and one abstention. No one voted against it in either chamber.

SB 1387 was one of six bills sponsored by Jewish California in this legislative session. Newsom signed all six. The new laws strengthen security at houses of worship, limit hate speech in the state’s voter guides and expand religious accommodations at public forums and schools. 

On Sept. 29, Newsom signed Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan’s (D-Orinda) Safe Worship Zone Act, or AB 2664, which bars anyone from approaching a congregant within 8 feet without consent inside a 100-foot zone around house-of-worship entrances. 

AB 1853, authored by Assemblymembers Gail Pellerin (D-Santa Cruz) and Marc Berman (D-Menlo Park), lets election officials reject threatening, false or misleading candidate statements in voter guides. Legislators introduced the bill after an antisemitic screed by a gubernatorial candidate was published in the state’s voter guide for the June 2 primary election.

AB 1836 expands the State Nonprofit Security Grant Program to cover off-site events like Pride marches and menorah lightings. AB 395 asks state agencies and public colleges to avoid scheduling major events on religious and cultural holidays, adding Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur and Passover to state law for the first time. Both bills were authored by Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), who chairs the California Legislative Jewish Caucus. 

Jewish California also sponsored a bill from a non-caucus member. AB 1763, by Assemblymember Alex Lee (D-San Jose), guarantees K-12 students excused absences for religious holidays without prior approval.

All six laws take effect Jan. 1.

The Jewish ethnicity law gives state agencies two years to update their forms and data systems. The timeline mirrors AB 91, the 2025 law that added Middle Eastern and North African categories, including Israeli, to state forms on the same 2029 schedule.

Newsom also vetoed one bill that Jewish California initially opposed: AB 2551, which first sought to repeal SB 1287, a law that established “time, place and manner” restrictions for college protests. AB 2551 would have required the California State University system to report annually on student code-of-conduct violations tied to campus protests. 

Jewish California changed its position on the bill to neutral after working with the bill’s author, Assemblymember Sade Elhawary (D-South Los Angeles), to remove the language repealing SB 1287. 

“Given the numerous ways that the campus community can already provide perspective to policy leaders,” Newsom wrote in a Sept. 27 veto message, “it is unnecessary to require an additional forum outside these established processes.”
