California Labels "Real" Food. Will Grocers Make Shoppers Feel Something?

California Just Put a Seal on “Real” Food. Now Comes the Hard Part.

For the first time, a state government wants to show, right on the package, that a food isn’t ultra-processed.

When I first wrote about California’s move to become the first state in the nation to legally define ultra-processed food back in April, I said that “in my view, it is the most coherent food policy framework any U.S. government body has produced.” On September 28, Gov. Newsom signed AB 2244 into law which creates a standardized “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified” seal that manufacturers can put on qualifying products. It passed the Legislature 104 to 0, which tells you something about where the politics of this issue are right now. Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who wrote the bill, likes to say parents shouldn’t need a PhD in chemistry to understand what they’re feeding their kids.

It is important to note that this program is voluntary. A company that wants the seal pays an accredited certification agent who registered with the California Department of Public Health to review the product. The agent can’t certify anything that contains the additives listed in the state’s definition of ultra-processed food (think emulsifiers, stabilizers and thickeners, colors, flavor enhancers and non-nutritive sweeteners), or anything classified as an “ultraprocessed food of concern” or a “restricted school food.”

Grocers, those with more than $10 million in annual sales, will have to make certified products clearly identifiable on the shelf, this rule kicks in once a store carries more than 25 certified items and has to highlight at least three through signage, placement or separate displays. With the average supermarket selling about $37 million a year, that $10 million threshold covers nearly every full-size grocery store in the state. That turns a labeling law into a merchandising mandate, and that’s the part shoppers will actually notice.

Don’t expect to see the seal next week. The Department of Public Health still has to build the whole framework and accredit certifiers, and the first certified products are years away as the deadline is June 1, 2029 with certification renewed every three years.

The fine print

Claudia Lewis, co-chair of Venable’s FDA group, spotted something in her client alert that every brand manager should read carefully. The seal’s eligibility test borrows only the additive criteria from the Real Food, Healthy Kids Act, the 2025 law that gave California the nation’s first statutory definition of ultra-processed food. It doesn’t use that law’s complete definition. So a product that California itself wouldn’t call ultra-processed could still fail to earn the seal. Lewis warns companies not to “assume that a product is eligible for certification” just because they cleared the state’s broader definition.

Here’s the problem with that fine print: the state now has two tests for the same idea, and they don’t line up perfectly. If the lawyers need a client alert to sort it out, I’m not sure how a mom with two kids in the cart at 6 p.m. is supposed to.

What I like

This is a positive label. Most of what we’ve done in food policy for decades has been warning labels and “free-from” claims that tell you what’s missing. This seal points shoppers toward something, and that is a friendlier message at the shelf.

Today our aisles are filled with private “clean” and “real food” marks, each with its own rules. I’ve watched shoppers many times stand in an aisle in front of a kid’s cereal box trying to figure out which claim or badge means. A single state standard with enforcement behind it (misuse of the seal can be taken to court) is a real improvement over the CPG honor system.

And it will push reformulation the same way we have seen brands add protein and fiber to meet the needs of the GLP-1 shopper. As retailers have to spotlight certified products, the folks in R&D kitchens are already figuring out how to clean up their ingredient lists

What I don’t like

Food processing and nutrition are different issues. The California UPF seal is built around additives, so a full-sugar granola bar could earn it while a stevia-sweetened yogurt can’t. The additive list in California’s definition includes stevia and monk fruit, which means plenty of the reduced-sugar products that nutritionists have been urging brands to create won’t qualify. A full-sugar product made with a short ingredient list could.

Cost is another issue. Certification isn’t free, and it must be renewed. The state hasn’t said what it will cost, and that’s a question small brands will want answered before they apply for the seal. The big CPG companies can easily absorb that cost, but the small, regional brands that often make exactly the kind of simple food this law celebrates may find the paperwork and fees harder to justify.

The industry isn’t on board either. The Consumer Brands Association, the Calorie Control Council, the California Manufacturers & Technology Association and the Food Ingredient Safety Coalition all opposed the bill, and their objections deserve a hearing. According to the Senate Judiciary Committee’s analysis, their biggest complaint is that the seal borrows definitions written for school meals and applies them to the entire grocery store. They offered an example: California’s definition of ultra-processed food exempts alcoholic beverages. That means a spirit or a flavored malt beverage could qualify for the “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified” seal, while a low-fat yogurt sweetened with stevia, or a bagged salad whose dressing uses a common emulsifier, might not.

They also argued the timing is backward. Key terms like “ultraprocessed food of concern” and “restricted school food” still have to be defined by the Department of Public Health through a rulemaking that hadn’t started when the committee reviewed the bill. When the committee reviewed the bill, certifiers were supposed to be approved on the same date those definitions were due, leaving no time to settle the rules before products were tested against them. There’s also a legal concern; anyone can sue over misuse of the seal, and there’s already a private “Non-UPF” label on the market that looks similar, which opponents say invites lawsuits born out of consumer confusion. The opponents of the bill warned that if Washington writes its own definition of ultra-processed food, California’s could fall out of step, forcing brands to reformulate and re-certify all over again.

Will other states follow?

I have no doubt they will. Gabriel’s earlier additive bills have inspired similar legislation in more than 30 states, and a law that passes without a single “no” vote is catnip for legislators everywhere. I am watching Texas, New York and Pennsylvania, all of which have been active on food additives.

I’ve been covering the grocery business long enough to remember what organic looked like before 2002: a patchwork of state and private certifiers, each with its own definition, and shoppers who had no idea whether “organic” in one state meant the same thing in another. It took the National Organic Program to clean that up. We saw it again with GMO labeling, when Vermont’s law forced Washington to write a national disclosure standard because the industry couldn’t live with (or afford) 50 different packages.

Now imagine a Texas seal that allows stevia and a California seal that doesn’t. A national brand could need a California package and a Texas package, each with its own certification. Many will simply skip certification altogether, which defeats the purpose. My concern is that others will lobby hard for a federal standard, and depending on who writes it, that could end up weaker than what California just passed.

Will it confuse shoppers?

In the short term, yes. Consumers already believe “natural” means something it doesn’t. A “Non-Ultraprocessed” seal will be read by most people as “healthy,” and that isn’t what it certifies. Add a second state with different rules, plus the private seals that are popping up on packages, and we’ll have more labels competing for the shoppers’ attention.

My Bottom Line

I support what California is trying to do. Shoppers want help, and a clear, enforceable seal beats dozens of self-made marketing badges. But the state needs to line up its own definitions before this rolls out, and the industry, retailers and regulators should be talking now about a national standard that doesn’t water things down. If we get 20 states writing 20 versions of this seal, the people it was meant to help will end up more confused than they are today.

The Bell, the Aisle of Shame and Why Most Grocers Still Don’t Get It

Somewhere in a Trader Joe’s right now, a crew member in a flowered shirt is ringing a bell, and shoppers are smiling.

That bell came up more than once when I joined a panel at Groceryshop called “Store Innovations that Match Consumer Expectations.” Stuart Samuel of Retail Futures at IGD moderated, and I shared the stage with Rick LaBerge of Haribo America and Katie Blackburn of WHSmith North America. A candy company, a travel retailer and a grocery guy might sound like an odd mix. It turned out to be one of the more useful conversations I’ve had on a stage this year, because we kept landing in the same place. Shoppers want to feel something when they walk into a store, and too many retailers have forgotten how to give them that.

Candy, nostalgia and a factory in Wisconsin

Rick talked about how Haribo connects with people through nostalgia. Almost everyone has a gummy bear memory. What struck me was how they’re pairing that with a steady stream of new ideas: a Harry Potter licensing partnership, seasonal products, and the pick-and-mix counters in their U.S. stores. Rick described it as a live test kitchen, where they can try new flavors and textures and see what people actually reach for.

Their Wisconsin manufacturing plant makes a lot of that possible. Producing here lets them localize products and move quickly on trends, so they can keep the core items rolling in huge volumes while still experimenting with small runs. Plenty of grocers could take a lesson from that. Rick emphasized that their assortment pays the bills, and what’s new gets people to come back.

A billion travelers and one airport store

Katie made a point I hadn’t thought much about. A traveler sprinting to a gate needs water and a charger in 45 seconds. Someone with a delayed flight wants a treat and take their time looking around the store. WHSmith is designing for both. She described their programmatic retail media network, which reaches the roughly one billion travelers who move through U.S. airports each year, and their LaGuardia store as an example of balancing the essentials with something new.

The part of her story I found most interesting was the unglamorous work underneath it. WHSmith has been investing in new distribution centers and ranging tools so products are actually on the shelf and staff aren’t spending their shifts chasing inventory. The goal is to free those people up to talk with customers. I’ve walked more stores than I can count where the associates are too busy stocking to look up.

Then I got on my soapbox

I told the Groceryshop attendees that retail needs passion and emotion, and most traditional grocers have none.

Look at who’s winning. Trader Joe’s has its bell and a crew that seems to actually enjoy being there. Aldi fans have turned the center aisle of rotating specials into the “aisle of shame,” and they talk about it online like it’s a treasure hunt. Wegmans curates its stores so carefully that people plan road trips around them. Then there’s Erewhon, which sells some of the most expensive groceries in the country and has customers who treat a visit like an event. Nobody would call Erewhon a bargain, and people keep coming back again and again for a single Japanese strawberry priced at $20 or a celeb’s smoothie recipe for $21.

Compare that with the average supermarket, where the experience is fluorescent lights, a long checkout line and a loyalty app that mostly sends you coupons for things you’ve never bought. The moment something more convenient comes along, they’re gone.

I also talked about restaurant-style concepts inside grocery stores, which I believe is one of the biggest opportunities in this business. Hy-Vee’s Market Grills and Pullman Market show that when grocers do it right, food service becomes a reason to visit, food service becomes a reason to visit on its own. (It’s also why I’m launching Retail Plate with my co-founder and longtime friend Korry Stagnito later this month, but more on that another time.)

Amazon, Buc-ee’s and Lululemon

We spent some time on Amazon’s physical store strategy, including the Whole Foods Daily Shop format and the way the company is using benefits tied to Prime as a tool to hold on to shoppers and workers. Amazon keeps testing and adjusting, and I think grocers make a mistake when they write off each experiment as a failure. Amazon learns from every one of them.

The panel also shared a lot of admiration for Buc-ee’s. It’s a gas station, and people drive out of their way to go there. The restrooms are spotless. The smoked brisket sandwich is famous, but seriously for my taste buds the Beaver Nuggets are even better! The store is designed around the customer rather than for the convenience of the operator. Lululemon came up too, for turning its stores into community spaces with run clubs, events and classes. People go to a Lululemon store even when they don’t need leggings.

Not one of these is a traditional grocer, yet each has made its stores into a destination, and that should be the standard grocery is measured against.

My Bottom Line

Walking off that stage, I kept thinking about how often our industry treats emotion as a marketing problem. You can’t buy that with an ad campaign. It comes from staff who have time to engage and a few surprises every visit and a sense that someone actually cares about the experience. Haribo and WHSmith are building the operational foundation that makes that possible. Most grocers are still trying to win on price and promotions, and that’s not enough. If your store closed tomorrow, would your shoppers miss it or just drive to the next one?
