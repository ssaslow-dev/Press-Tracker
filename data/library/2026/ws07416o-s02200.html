Knowing Dave Regan, and a new push on ultraprocessed foods

By BLAKE JONES, MELANIE MASON and DUSTIN GARDINER

DRIVING THE DAY — It’s hard to find anyone who’s worked at a high level of California politics who doesn’t have a strong opinion about Dave Regan. And these days, the polarizing health care union leader is on a lot of people’s minds.

SEIU-UHW’s pursuit of a wealth tax ballot initiative — despite Gov. Gavin Newsom’s fervent opposition and reservations in some quarters of organized labor — has propelled Regan both to the forefront of California politics and to the center of a fiery national debate about Democrats’ economic agenda.

Jeremy B. White spoke with dozens of people who have worked with, for, or against Regan and sat down with Regan for his first extensive interview since launching the wealth tax.

To his proponents, the 60-year-old Regan is an unapologetic combatant for working people — a fiercely intelligent but blunt tactician who is willing to use any tool at his disposal to grow his union and win better working conditions for health care workers. His detractors describe him as a bruising operator who exaggerates his leverage and picks fights that have left him increasingly isolated even as he pursues a campaign with profound consequences for California.

FIRST IN POLITICO: BACK FOR SECONDS — No one likes a food fight more than Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, the San Fernando Valley Democrat who has become the face of California’s anti-additives crusade and who today is unveiling his latest effort to get the state — and the rest of the country — eating clean.

His new bill, shared first with POLITICO, would create the country’s first seal to indicate foods are not ultraprocessed, deeming them “California Certified.” And would require grocery stores to prominently display those products at the end of aisles and other visible locations.

The bill, AB 2244, follows legislation Newsom signed last year creating the nation’s first definition for “ultraprocessed” foods and phasing them out of school lunches.

The California Democrat’s effort is part of a larger war on unhealthy food being waged from Washington by Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — and gaining traction among Republicans and Democrats alike. Kennedy has rewritten the nation’s dietary guidelines to prioritize whole grains and vegetables over “highly processed” foods and launched his “Eat Real Food” campaign during the Super Bowl.

Getting ultraprocessed foods out of American diets is both popular and bipartisan: the rare public health measure that can tick both of those boxes. More than 60 percent of people said they support removing ultraprocessed foods from shelves in a poll conducted by POLITICO and Public First this month, including 73 percent of Donald Trump voters and 66 percent of Kamala Harris supporters.

Despite the popularity, Kennedy’s efforts have made him enemies in the food and grocery industry, who say his agenda on food additives is a business killer that will jack up the already high price of groceries.

Gabriel is bracing for similar pushback in California. He argued the regulations he is pushing represent a “light touch,” but that there are “certain folks in the industry who are just allergic to regulation, and they’re going to push back on anything they don’t want to be told what to do.”

Gabriel hopes that as California snacks, so snacks the nation. He’s anticipating that companies will reformulate their recipes to get the seal of approval in California and then sell those UPF-free foods everywhere.

“That’s an added benefit that we’ve been able to drive change more broadly across the country,” he said. “In a moment when, when Washington is not stepping up to the plate, and when they have failed to lead on this issue, California is going to continue to lead.” — Nicole Norman and Rachel Bluth

DEBATE DUD — Candidates for governor scattered to reach voters directly Tuesday night after USC canceled its controversy-laden debate. Tom Steyer invited candidates to participate in a substitute forum, and though Matt Mahan agreed to attend, the eleventh-hour effort never came together. Katie Porter spoke live to her Instagram followers, calling for a progressive tax on corporate profits. Tony Thurmond joined TikTok creator Mrs. Frazzled for a friendly conversation about his platform. Steve Hilton hopped on a live X chat with conservative social media influencers and blasted the Democrats for being “so incompetent they can’t even organize a debate.” And Betty Yee told reporters she was headed for a previously scheduled women’s event and a major Democratic club’s endorsement meeting.

The scramble came as the California Democratic Party released its first round of polling in the race following an effort to nudge non-viable candidates to drop out. Yee, the former state controller polling in the low single digits, again criticized the party’s efforts to winnow the field on her press call. She said she periodically assesses her campaign’s viability, and determined she had a path before this month’s filing deadline.

“We’re going to reassess again in mid-April to see whether we can, you know, be able to make it through into the primary,” she said.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: COMMUNITY CHEST — Attorney General Rob Bonta wants California to lead on antitrust cases as the Federal Trade Commission eases up enforcement under President Donald Trump.

But first, he’ll need some more cash from the Legislature, he told Playbook in an interview.

“These are big cases, they’re media cases. The other side is well-resouced,” Bonta said during a sit-down conversation at the Common Sense Summit in San Francisco on Tuesday. “Experts need to be brought in; they’re expensive.”

Bonta’s plea for more resources follows a recent string of antitrust-related actions from his office. In February, he vowed to review entertainment giant Paramount’s controversial Warner Bros. acquisition. Just this month, he sued to block broadcast giant Nexstar’s proposed purchase of Tegna (a deal Newsom also blasted in a statement) and took Live Nation back to trial on monopoly accusations alongside other state attorneys general, arguing that the federal government had failed to protect consumers in both cases.

The problem, Bonta told us, is that he needs more bodies. California’s Justice Department has the largest antitrust team of any state with about 50 staff, by his count, but it’s a drop in the bucket compared to the hundreds of antitrust specialists at the FTC. Bonta said he’s turned to hiring outside counsel to handle the workload in some cases, even though he prefers to keep operations in-house.

Adding 10 or 20 additional staff in California would make a difference, he said, especially if other states upgrade their antitrust operations. He estimated the expansion would cost “millions” but didn’t offer a specific figure when asked.

“If we ask for a little bit more, and then other states ask for a little bit more, and then we come together,” Bonta said, “we’d all have more to put on the table.”

About that Paramount-Warner Bros. review: Bonta said he’s still working through the review process and waiting to see how European regulators handle the megadeal — while also reiterating he sees “huge red flags” that may warrant a lawsuit.

“We don’t think this is imminent, like tomorrow imminent, but it’s probably around the corner,” he said. “And in the meantime, we’re doing our due diligence and finishing our investigation.” — Tyler Katzenberger

SUPERVISORS’ SCRUTINY — The Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors voted unanimously on Tuesday to direct county staff to conduct a comprehensive economic analysis of the prospective Warner Bros. sale.

The county’s effort, to be led by the Department of Economic Opportunity in collaboration with other county departments, will explore the effects of the deal on employment. The transaction, which requires regulatory approval, is widely expected to result in layoffs in a mainstay local industry that has already been roiled by major job losses in recent years. About 42,000 Hollywood jobs were lost between 2022 and 2024.

Supervisor Lindsey P. Horvath, who authored the motion calling for the study, said in a statement that it was needed to provide “a clear understanding” of the deal’s effects on “jobs, competition, and the future of storytelling.”

“Entertainment is more than what we watch on a screen — it’s part of who we are as Angelenos and a cornerstone of our economy,” she said. “Thousands of families rely on this industry for their livelihoods, and we must protect their jobs and our signature industry.”

As part of the motion, the Department of Economic Opportunity will develop job training and placement programs to support entertainment industry workers. It will deliver its final findings and recommendations to the board in 120 days.

Horvath’s motion also directs county attorneys to submit formal comments to the U.S. Department of Justice over potential antitrust concerns. The Trump administration has signaled a preference for the sale to close — with Paramount targeting the third quarter. Bonta told POLITICO last week that it appeared “the federal administration” was “retreating from its traditional role and its duty to do this work.” — Daniel Miller

MILLER TIME — Little-known tech entrepreneur and mayoral hopeful Adam Miller unveiled a new ad to introduce himself to Angelenos — and position himself as an alternative to Mayor Karen Bass. Miller, a Democrat, is paying seven figures to be the first candidate on air via cable, streaming TV and digital outlets. His team was coy about the exact spending, or how much Miller, who has considerable personal wealth, plans to spend to boost his campaign.

Miller has an uphill climb to build a profile in sprawling Los Angeles. His campaign released a polling memo that acknowledged his current low standing in the polls, trailing all the other major candidates. But Bill Burton, his senior strategist, said the mayor’s race is “wide open,” citing Bass’ dismal 32 percent approval rating.

“With sufficient resources, Adam has a very, very clear path to victory,” said campaign pollster Jef Pollock, though he declined to say how much money would be required to put him in contention.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: BULLY PULPIT — Newsom is today telling 15 cities and counties that they have one last chance to come into compliance with state housing requirements before they’re referred to the Department of Justice for possible legal action. His Department of Housing and Community Development notified the local governments that they’re violating laws requiring that their housing blueprints include plans to build residences for all income levels.

The final warnings — while attached to lawsuit threats — are an extension of Newsom’s attempts to prod cities and counties into promoting housing construction through what is essentially public shaming. He has repeatedly in letters, on social media and in legal fights pushed the issue as he attempts to address one of California’s most politically fraught problems ahead of a likely presidential campaign.

The municipalities notified were Atwater, Avenal, California City, Corcoran, Escalon, Half Moon Bay, Hanford, Kings County, Lemoore, Merced County, Montclair, Oakdale, Patterson, Ridgecrest and Turlock.

KNUCKLE UP — An offshore fracking proposal is emerging as a new flash point in the ongoing feud between the Trump and Newsom administrations over offshore oil production. California successfully got a judge to order a pause on fracking off the Golden State coast during the first Trump administration, but now, federal regulators are mulling a new proposal to do so. MAHAN’S PLAN — Mahan has proposed a five-point government reform plan that ties pay raises for elected officials to measurable performance outcomes like reduced homelessness, the Los Angeles Times reports.

The gubernatorial candidate’s plan, which advocates for implementing a private-sector model in government, also proposed implementing no tax increases until the state can prove “that we can deliver better outcomes with the dollars we already have.” Implementation of the plan would likely require a constitutional amendment and voter approval.

BIANCO’S BALLOTS — Attorney General Bonta is asking a California court to halt Riverside County Sheriff and candidate for governor Chad Bianco’s effort to recount ballots from the 2025 November special election, CalMatters reports.

In an unprecedented move, Bianco seized around 650,000 ballots to conduct a recount of votes. Bianco characterized the effort as a “fact-finding mission,” intended “just as much to prove the election is accurate as it is to show otherwise.”

IN THE NAVY — The Navy is “looking into” whether Democratic congressional candidate Ammar Campa-Najjar ran afoul of Pentagon restrictions on the use of military status and images in political campaigns, KPBS reports. A spokesperson for Campa-Najjar, who is running in the toss-up House seat currently held by GOP Rep. Darrell Issa, said claims of improper use of his military status are “unfounded.”
