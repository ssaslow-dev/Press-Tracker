Election 2026 · State of California

Proposition 2: Larger state rainy day fund

Proposition 2 would change the State Constitution so the state keeps saving in its main rainy day fund until the fund equals 20% of General Fund taxes, up from 10% today, and saves more in years when revenue from investment gains is very high. It would also extend required extra debt payments from 2030 to 2040 and stop counting rainy day fund deposits toward the state spending limit until the money is taken out.

Summary by CityHallPass, not the official text or legal advice. The official text controls: California Official Voter Information Guide: Proposition 2.

What does a yes vote on Proposition 2 mean?

Rules for building state budget reserves and paying down extra state debts would change. As a result, state budget reserves would be higher. The state also would be required to make extra debt payments for longer.

What does a no vote on Proposition 2 mean?

Rules for building state budget reserves and paying down extra state debts would not change.

What would Proposition 2 cost?

The Legislative Analyst says state budget reserves would be higher, which would make it somewhat easier to balance the budget when revenue falls. The state might also make more debt payments than it would otherwise. The analysis gives no dollar figure.

What it means for the City of LA

Proposition 2 changes only state accounts. It does not change the City of Los Angeles's own reserve rules or spending limit, and the Legislative Analyst does not estimate any local effect. Its effect on the City would come through the state budget, which pays for programs the City relies on, such as state homelessness grants. A larger state reserve is meant to make state cuts less likely in a downturn, but the measure does not guarantee any program's funding.

What would change

Today If Proposition 2 passes
Rainy day fund cap Deposits stop once the fund reaches 10% of General Fund taxes; money that would have gone in must go to infrastructure (LAO analysis) Deposits continue until the fund reaches 20% (LAO analysis)
Yearly set-aside 1.5% of General Fund tax revenue, half to the rainy day fund and half to debt payments, plus extra when capital gains revenue tops 8% of General Fund taxes (Budget Center) The 1.5% stays. Capital gains revenue between 8% and 10% of General Fund taxes still counts as extra; above 10%, 1.5 times the amount counts (Budget Center; text, Sec. 20)
Extra debt payments Required through 2030, and only for state pension and retiree health debts (LAO analysis) Required through 2040. Can also go to money owed to schools and community colleges, loans from other state funds, and federal unemployment insurance loans (LAO analysis)
State spending limit (Gann limit) Money put into the rainy day fund counts toward the limit when deposited (LAO analysis) Starting in 2027-28, deposits do not count; withdrawals count when taken out. The same applies to the Projected Surplus Temporary Holding Account, up to 10% of General Fund taxes a year (text, Sec. 20(i)-(j))
Declaring a budget emergency The Governor must issue a proclamation before deposits can be cut or money withdrawn (Budget Center) The Governor’s May budget revision counts as the proclamation if it proposes cutting deposits or withdrawing money and emergency conditions exist (text, Sec. 22(c))
School funding guarantee (Prop. 98) Unchanged Unchanged. The text says nothing in it “is intended to change the calculation or funding of the minimum guarantee” (text, First (b)(8))

What stays the same: the state can still cut deposits or withdraw money in a budget emergency, with the same definition of emergency. No more than half the fund can be withdrawn in the first year of an emergency. Required debt payments still cannot be suspended (Budget Center).

What it means for the City of Los Angeles

  • No direct change for the City. Proposition 2 amends only Sections 20 and 22 of Article XVI of the State Constitution, which govern state reserves (ACA 20 status). The Legislative Analyst’s analysis covers only the state budget (LAO analysis).
  • The City’s own reserves follow City policy. The City’s Reserve Fund must equal 5% of General Fund revenue, and the City’s goal is 10% across its Reserve Fund, Budget Stabilization Fund and unappropriated balance (City 2026-27 Proposed Budget, General Fund Reserves Policy). Proposition 2 does not change these.
  • Indirect effect through the state budget. The City budgets state money for some programs, such as the state’s Homeless Housing, Assistance and Prevention (HHAP) grants (City 2026-27 Proposed Budget). The Analyst says higher reserves “would make balancing the budget somewhat easier when revenues decline” (LAO analysis). The measure does not set aside money for any local program. The Budget Center says money set aside in boom years would not be available then for needs such as “support for local governments to address homelessness” (Budget Center).
  • The Los Angeles Area Chamber of Commerce is one of the signers of the argument in favor (arguments). We found no City Council position on Proposition 2 as of Sept. 30, 2026.

How it got on the ballot

ACA 20 needed a two-thirds vote of each house (ACA 20 status). It was filed with the Secretary of State without a Governor’s signature (ACA 20 history).

  • June 22, 2026: ACA 20 was introduced. Its lead authors are Assemblymembers Jesse Gabriel and Valencia and Senators Hurtado and John Laird. The Assembly voted 51-16 to suspend two of its rules the same day (ACA 20 history; ACA 20 status).
  • June 25, 2026: The Assembly tabled amendments offered by Assemblymember David Tangipa, 55-18, then adopted ACA 20, 59-8. The Senate withdrew it from committee and adopted it the same day, 29-2. It was filed as Resolution Chapter 130 (ACA 20 votes; ACA 20 history). The Assembly no votes were DeMaio, Dixon, Ellis, Hadwick, Macedo, Patterson, Sanchez and Tangipa. The Senate no votes were Choi and Grove (ACA 20 votes).
  • The Governor’s office listed the amendment as part of the 2026-27 budget agreement announced June 26 (Governor’s office). The Assembly Budget Committee’s floor report says the rainy day fund changes were “completed in conjunction with legislative approval of ACA 20” (Assembly floor report, p. 6).

Why the vote threshold is a simple majority. The State Constitution says a proposed amendment “shall be submitted to the electors and, if approved by a majority of votes cast thereon, takes effect” (Cal. Const. art. XVIII, sec. 4).

Related past measures.

  • Proposition 58 (2004) created the rainy day fund, the Budget Stabilization Account (Budget Center).
  • Proposition 2 (November 2014) set the current deposit, debt payment and 10% cap rules (LAO analysis). It passed with 69.1% statewide and 69.8% in Los Angeles County (Statement of Vote, 2014).
  • Proposition 4 (1979) created the state spending limit, known as the Gann limit (Budget Center).

What each side says

In the state voter guide. Arguments are the authors’ opinions and are not checked for accuracy by any official agency (arguments).

Argument in favor, signed by Darrell Roberts of California Professional Firefighters, Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel and Maria S. Salinas of the Los Angeles Area Chamber of Commerce (argument in favor):

  • The state budget swung “from a $100 billion surplus to a $50 billion deficit in just a few years.”
  • It doubles the reserve requirement “so California has a real cushion.”
  • It makes the state bank more of a revenue spike “instead of committing it to new ongoing spending.”
  • The Legislative Analyst “has recommended that the state should be saving significantly more than current rules require.”

Their rebuttal, signed by Linda Darling-Hammond of the State Board of Education, Carmela Coyle of the California Hospital Association and Francisco J. Silva of the California Primary Care Association, calls the taxpayer rebate “a 50-year-old accounting gimmick that rewards spending instead of saving” (rebuttal).

Argument against, signed by Assemblymember David Tangipa, Sen. Steven Choi and Carl DeMaio of Reform California (argument against):

  • It lets politicians “gut the voter-imposed spending limit” and creates a “SLUSH FUND.”
  • It “cancels your tax rebate,” which the Constitution requires when revenue exceeds the spending limit.
  • The state budget “has grown by 76% since 2018.”
  • “Within 24 hours” of putting it on the ballot, the same politicians “voted to suspend more than $5 billion in required deposits.”

Outside the guide.

  • The Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association recommends a no vote. Its guide says the measure’s reserve changes make sense “from the perspective of addressing revenue volatility” but calls it “little more than a Band-Aid on a severed limb” because it does not undo earlier changes to the Gann limit (HJTA 2026 voting guide).
  • Senate President pro Tempore Monique Limón said the budget deal addresses “our short and long-term financial future by building up our rainy day fund” (Governor’s office).

Independent analysis

  • Legislative Analyst. Besides the official analysis (LAO analysis), the Analyst says revenue declines over a bad recession “can equal 50 percent or more of annual General Fund revenues.” In a February presentation to the Senate Budget Committee, its simulations found current rules would cover about 30% of funding shortfalls over 50 years; raising the cap to 50% by 2055 with stronger deposit rules would cover about 75% (LAO handout, pp. 6-7). The handout does not model Proposition 2 as written.
  • Budget Center. The California Budget & Policy Center says that if Prop 2’s capital gains rules had applied from 2010-11 to 2023-24, the state would have set aside more in six of those 14 years, less than 1% to 2% of General Fund revenue each time. It says the change could take hundreds of millions of dollars, or more than $1 billion, off the table in some years. It also says a higher cap means less “spillover” money for infrastructure, and that using debt payments on federal unemployment insurance loans would shift a cost from businesses to the state (Budget Center).
  • Current reserves. The 2026-27 budget projects $28.8 billion in reserves: $15.1 billion in the rainy day fund, $9.2 billion in the school reserve and $4.5 billion in the operating reserve, plus $6.4 billion in the temporary holding account (Assembly floor report, p. 10).

Interaction with other measures on this ballot

  • The Legislative Analyst’s analysis names no conflict between Proposition 2 and other measures on this ballot (LAO analysis).
  • Proposition 41 also deals with the state spending limit, in a different way: it would stop new state taxes from being excluded from the limit (LAO analysis of Prop 41). Proposition 2 concerns reserve deposits, not taxes. See our Proposition 41 page.
  • Proposition 3 would keep higher income tax rates on high earners after 2031 (Prop 3 analysis). Rainy day fund deposits are set as shares of General Fund revenue, so more revenue means larger required deposits under either current rules or Proposition 2 (LAO analysis). See our Proposition 3 page.

If it passes

  • When it takes effect. A constitutional amendment takes effect on the fifth day after the Secretary of State files the statement of the vote (Cal. Const. art. XVIII, sec. 4). The spending limit changes apply starting in the 2027-28 fiscal year (text, Sec. 20(i)).
  • Budget emergencies. The Governor could use the May budget revision as the emergency proclamation (text, Sec. 22(c)).
  • Debt payments. The Assembly Budget Committee says the new debt payment rules would give the state another way to pay an estimated $3.9 billion owed to schools and community colleges for 2025-26 (Assembly floor report, p. 6).
  • Legal challenges. We found no reported legal challenge as of Sept. 30, 2026.
