Let’s start with a California Power Lesson and end with a campaign lesson with a few tidbits in between.

I Called It

I am going to give an “I told you so” moment to demonstrate how Power works in Sacramento.

Last fall, under the radar, there was a spat regarding anti-semitism, and it is a major flashpoint with the far left of the Democratic Party. In the case of California, there was a bill which was fought over in the legislature. The bottom line was small, obscure, Jewish Political Action Committee (JPAC), a small organization not aligned with AIPAC, was able to muscle through a watered down anti-semitism bill in the legislature against the wishes of the California Faculty Association (CFA) among other groups. It was a minor skirmish in the bigger issues going in the Democratic Party. It also brought out some of the most venial language you use in politics.

First there was the threat made by the CFA (and therefore others) they would not endorse any candidate who took money from JPAC, AIPAC or other “dirty sources.” The message was clear, Jewish money which fought them would be considered “dirty” for the purposes of politics. They “tainted the well” with a clear anti-semitic trope, but it was not presented as such.

Then, after blowback, they back off a couple days later, but the message was clear. The base was upset. It was more about Power. JPAC is a small player in Sacramento. Education labor unions/associations? They own Sacramento. How did this happen? Sure there is the politics, but it is the raw Power involved. We said at the time, it is good JPAC had friends with butts in seats, and it was a lesson to learn.

Then, in November, we published another piece which used the episode as an example of the future battles of the Party.

I am going to quote the three paragraphs because they illustrate the point clearly.

“People are worn out and those in Power get it. That is the point. The wearing you out is exactly what they want. They want to keep their seats. They will keep coming. You might get a temporary reprieve, but the long-term trends, unless changed, leave you exposed. I said a great example was the JPAC situation with the California Faculty Association (CFA). JPAC, a Jewish Political Action Committee focused on matters in California (not to be confused with AIPAC, which is the boogie man to the far left). JPAC won some anti-Semitism reforms in education. Not hard to understand, one would expect such a cause from JPAC.

Well, the CFA opposed the bill. When they lost to little old JPAC, they tried to exact retribution. Any political candidate seeking the CFA’s endorsement, the announced with great fanfare, had to indicate their receipt of money from JPAC (along with oil, tobacco, and AIPAC). While you say, “who cares about the CFA,” the answer is if you do not seek their endorsement but you do from others, well, are you hiding something, and then what is that something? Others can silently boycott candidates supporting Jewish causes through the CFA’s filter.

The situation was resolved when the CFA apologized the next day. One wonders why? Scott Weiner and Jesse Gabriel are two of the most Powerful people in the Legislature. What happens when they are gone? The CFA just goes back at it. JPAC might think they won, and they may have won a battle but the game is a lot longer, and unless the status quo is changed, the CFA will come back, mark my words. They did not forget little JPAC beat them on anti-semitism, watered down as much as it was. Publicly apologizing for their “spat” was not something they will just walk away from. [Emphasis added]”

What do they do? They “lean.” They came back. From today’s Politico Playbook.

“CLEANUP DRAMA — Two groups behind a proposal to undo key provisions of a law aiming to clamp down on antisemitism in schools escalated their clash with Jewish groups that have been highly critical of the effort.

The California Faculty Association and Council on American-Islamic Relations sent a letter to lawmakers referring to the criticism as a “targeted misinformation campaign” and urging them to give the bill, which is expected to be contained in AB 2159, a hearing.

In the letter, the groups disputed claims from Jewish California, which sponsored AB 715 last year, that they were trying to “gut” the bill. The measure was pushed by the Legislature’s Jewish Caucus amid concerns about a rise in antisemitic incidents on school campuses.

David Bocarsly, executive director of Jewish California, said last month that the groups “have decided that their priority is to try to weaken civil rights laws protecting Jewish children,” calling it a “moral failure, and a dangerous precedent.” Read the full story in POLITICO Pro. — Eric He”

As predicted, they came back. And, they will come back again until they get what they want. It is about Power, plain and simple. JPAC (which is new Jewish California) won and needs to be taught a lesson. The CFA, and whoever else is behind the effort, are “docked” in the Power Game until this “episode” is “resolved.” On and on it goes. The Power Game.

The Gubernatorial

Ok, back to the happenings of the campaign.

We said earlier this week Katie Porter was starved of oxygen in her campaign as it was stuck. It seems she is starting to find that oxygen.

Politico had a piece this morning with a lot of tidbits in its California Playbook. The first being Porter “fighting” to get attention via a “leftward shift.” Will it succeed?

Well, she is getting press now.

Interesting timing as Swalwell is getting his own (not positive) press, along with Steyer (the painful article in the Los Angeles Times where he invoked a “Road to Damascus Moment,” while saying that he “donated the proceeds on investments in Corrections Corporation of America to charity,” after he made his billions).

Good week for Ms. Porter so far, especially as Matt Mahan is reeling after the $35 million challenge (leading some to think his campaign is falling apart).

In fact, Playbook quoted Swalwell supporters as pointing the finger to Porter regarding the whole situation about the sexual harassment rumors.

What is also happening is the “second tier” candidates are hitting at the Big 3. Tony Thrumond has been an effective “foil” to Tom Steyer, and Antonio Villaraigosa is now hitting on Swalwell with regularity.

Today he kept the negative story regarding Swalwell’s rumors going with a hit on him not showing up to a candidate forum because of his concern of facing reporters because of the allegations. Another negative cycle for Swalwell after a good couple weeks.

The “undercard” is an effective tool to “hit” the front runners in the race, softening them up, while there is oxygen in the other campaigns. It is a “heads I win tails you lose” situation. You stay in and hope you can catch fire, but meanwhile, you score points with whoever you are aligned with by landing blows which look “part of the game.”

See how long this goes. 30 more days for those campaigns could be very effective to keep the “front runners” from getting “too dirty.” Ah politics.

Mahan’s Struggles

Mahan’s campaign has been reeling over the last few days. First, he hit the “reset” button and shifted his campaign staff last week. Then there was the New York Times piece about the $35 million “challenge.” Then the “rebranding” of the campaign slogan from “Back to Basics.”

Per Politico this morning:

“BACK TO BASICS — San Jose Mayor Matt Mahan pushed back on The New York Times reporting that he briefed donors on a plan to move away from his “Back to Basics” motto as he seeks to gain traction in the field.

“I think we absolutely need to get California back to basics, so I don’t really know where that came from,” Mahan told Playbook, adding that a donor or donor’s “opinion on the call is not really material to me.”

Mahan projected confidence that he’d gain support without a messaging pivot now that he has hired new consultants. “We need to deliver our message aggressively across the state and have best-in-class creative to do so, and so you’re going to hear a lot more from us in the coming weeks.””

The press he is getting looks like the whole campaign has “too many cooks in the kitchen,” and no direction, and he is out of control. Whoever is leaking to the New York Times (twice this week now), is trying to force the issue inside. Silicon Valley is all about pivots. They are trying to find the pivot, but it looks like they are out of control. He is seemingly trying to wrest control from a faction of donors who are not in synch with where his campaign is going.

Bill Gross, a local tech entrepreneur here in Pasadena, has a good TED talk on why businesses succeed (start ups in his case). He said a business succeeds when three things line up. First you need the idea. Then you need the team. You can control (usually for the first two). The last is timing. You cannot control for that.

Mahan’s campaign is struggling with the three elements.

They have the idea, but it is falling flat because it is not sharp enough or too generic and poll tested. It needs “life.”

They have the team, but there are a lot of smart people involved, and sometimes that team does not click. How many USC football fans can point to a huge recruiting class and failing team though a lot go on to play professionally. Something is not aligned because he is having to send messages to those leaking to the New York Times that he has control over “the basics” of his own campaign theme (see what I did there).

The timing seems right, but maybe it is not exactly the time. The campaign is not catching on with the electorate which may be a cycle away (see my Virginia post yesterday). We need to get the words to better capture the timing frankly, and we allow the easy words being used to ill-define the moment.

Each has to be in synch, just right, to make it happen.

The hard part about politics is the compressed window.

You do not get to fail small and fail often in a 120 day campaign. Many founders spend years in a start up to get where they need to. Campaigns need to be done faster and cleaner to succeed in that moment. Maybe the mindset is this is a long-term project, and this election is a “pilot” or “test,” though the millions being raised are a lot for a pilot.

In a campaign, the roll out “is hard and fast-” you are “going to market” in the Silicon Valley parlance.

It has very small room to deliberate.

A lot of investors can cause “fog of war.”

Either, the candidate “gets it, or he doesn't.” You cannot change the “product,” which is the candidate during the campaign.

The campaign is promising “big things,” but we heard those words two weeks ago when they “parted ways” with the chief strategist. They are doing it again. We are beyond “big things in the coming weeks,” it is time to “put up or shut up.” Enough salesmanship, enough “tease,” get to it.
