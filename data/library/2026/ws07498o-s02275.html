Key California Political Figures to Watch in 2026

Emily Hamann and John Mulholland · Jan 05, 2026

With a new Senate leader, a gubernatorial race overflowing with candidates, a fight to replace U.S. Rep. Nancy Pelosi and a new slate of redistricted/gerrymandered House seats, there is much at stake in 2026. This is a list, in no particular order, of some of the key lawmakers to look out for this year.

1. Matt Mahan – San Jose mayor; dark horse governor contender. The moderate, Silicon Valley–friendly mayor could prove a disruptor in the gubernatorial race if he chooses to enter. He has been busy hosting candidates over the last few months while keeping his options open for a run. He would represent a new moderate-technocratic face of California politics (in the mold of San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie), leaning into public safety, crime, business and the upsides of tech and AI.

2–3. Steve Hilton and Chad Bianco – Republican gubernatorial outsiders. As the list of Democratic gubernatorial candidates grows, so too does the (still small) chance of two Republicans topping the open primary in June as the Democratic vote splits between multiple candidates. (The two leading candidates advance to the November general election.) The latest poll found Riverside County Sheriff Bianco leading with 13%, followed by former Fox News host Hilton and Democrat Eric Swalwell (12%) and Katie Porter at 11%. But more than 30% of voters remained undecided. Hilton and Bianco have polled between 10% and 13% over the last few months. As attention turns toward the campaign, increased visibility could propel one or both into a position to leapfrog what is generally considered, at this stage, an underwhelming slate of Democrats.

4. Rob Bonta – attorney general; presumed gubernatorial frontrunner. This labor-backed, criminal justice reformer is one of the few potential candidates in the gubernatorial race with statewide name recognition. With Sen. Alex Padilla no longer considering a run and large numbers of voters undecided and seemingly uninspired by the current slate of candidates, could Bonta seize the moment? Expect an announcement in early January.

5. Lateefah Simon – emerging first-term congresswoman from the Bay Area. Simon replaced Barbara Lee in November 2024. She has strong progressive credentials in the Bay Area and is a leading voice on criminal justice, disability rights and civil rights. Her influence in, and stature within, California politics are likely to grow in 2026.

6. Scott Wiener – state senator; Pelosi’s likely successor, but still vulnerable. Weiner is a heavyweight on housing and artificial intelligence, with a strong record of delivering in the Senate. The San Francisco lawmaker is the front-runner to replace Pelosi for the state’s District 11 congressional seat, but two challengers —San Francisco Board of Supervisor Connie Chan and U.S. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s former chief of staff and Justice Democrats co-founder, Saikat Chakrabarti — means he could be caught in a generational and ideological crossfire.

7. Katie Porter – former congresswoman; running for governor. She stumbled in 2025 when old videos of Porter resurfaced showing her in "fighter" mode — not always a good look. But a fighter she is, and she still retains political capital among progressives. Her polling figures in the gubernatorial race may have stalled after "videogate" — but can she regain momentum in 2026?

8. Monique Limón – new California Senate leader. All eyes will be on the new pro tem as she steers the Senate through the end of the session. Under her leadership, will the Senate continue its momentum on housing deregulation or slow down? How will the ardent environmental advocate navigate the rising concern about gas prices? The first Latina to serve as pro tem, Limón has been a strong voice on climate, labor and health.

9. Caroline Menjivar – rising San Fernando Valley Senator. She’s the new chair of the Democratic Caucus. Earlier this year, she was removed from the budget subcommittee on health care for refusing to support Medi-Cal cuts for undocumented immigrants. Under Limón, not only has she been reinstated, she’s now chair of the committee.

10. Jesse Gabriel – assemblymember; likely attorney general contender. If Bonta runs for governor, Gabriel will be among the frontrunners to replace him. A Harvard Law School graduate, he has strong fundraising abilities and a track record in the Assembly that includes passing landmark legislation banning the sale of several ultra-processed food additives in California schools.

11. Rebecca Bauer-Kahan – assemblymember; returning with major AI/chatbot legislation. She leads on AI transparency and consumer data rights. With Wiener focusing on a congressional run, Bauer-Kahan is well-positioned to become the leading voice on tech and AI in the Legislature. She has already pledged to return with more legislation looking to bring in AI chatbot guardrails.

12. Kamala Harris – continues book tour in 2026, with eyes on ’28? The former vice president has extended the book tour for 107 Days, her memoir of running for president in 2024. The tour (from January to June) sweeps through California, Indiana, Ohio, the Carolinas, Mississippi, Tennessee, Virginia, Alabama, Georgia, Michigan, Arizona, Wisconsin and Michigan — with quite a few swing states on that list. Do these appearances double as groundwork for a 2028 presidential campaign?

13. Jasmeet Bains – assemblymember; physician turned lawmaker, eyes Congress. Something of an outlier within the California Democratic Assembly, she sometimes breaks with the party on key votes — all the better to distinguish herself in her Central Valley district. Now Bains has her sights set on Congress. She is in a highly competitive 2026 Democratic primary election, with the winner facing off against Republican Rep. David Valadao in California's 22nd Congressional District, a seat tilted toward the Democrats post–Prop. 50 redistricting. Bains is up against fellow Democrat Randy Villegas in a test of moderates vs. progressives in one of the most-watched House races this year.

14. Randy Villegas – educator, activist, college professor. Competing with Bains in the Democratic primary for the right to contest Republican Rep. David Valadao in California’s 22nd Congressional District, the 31-year-old is likely to run to the left of Bains. Already endorsed by U.S. Sen. Bernie Sanders, Villegas has engaged newly installed New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s lead media strategist to create an ad for his campaign.

15. Maggy Krell – assemblymember; anti-trafficking renegade. The freshman lawmaker showed she wasn’t afraid to cause problems for her own party by authoring a sex trafficking bill this spring that reignited a firestorm Democrats thought they had settled last session. It remains to be seen if she will light another powder keg of legislation in the new year.

16. Buffy Wicks – assemblymember, Housing Committee chair; pro-development torchbearer. She was the top Democratic legislator for California’s “Yes In My Backyard” pro-development movement. With Sen. Scott Wiener (longtime housing reformer) distracted by his run at Pelosi’s congressional seat, Wicks is poised to play a key role in ongoing housing fights as the state tries, finally, to get to grips with increased housing development.

17. Matt Haney – assemblymember; populist housing/labor voice. A credible left-populist foil to Wiener if he chooses to enter the race for the soon-to-be-vacant Pelosi congressional seat. And, if not, he is very likely to tilt at Wiener’s senate seat if the San Francisco senator wins the Pelosi congressional vacancy.

18. Suzanne Jimenez – SEIU leader; organizing 2026 health/billionaire tax campaign. Jimenez is the voice of labor’s wealth tax push, which proposes a 5% tax over five years (ie, 1% per year) on the state’s billionaires in order to backfill the health funding gap created by Congress's tax cut and spending bill of last year. Expect major campaign clashes with tech billionaires if the measure makes it to the November ballot.

19. Alex Lee – assemblymember, democratic socialist; leading anti-corporate voice. A leading figure in the Assembly’s Democratic progressive caucus, Lee leans into issues such as wealth taxes, social and affordable housing and climate. He’s been trying to raise taxes on billionaires since first being elected, and was one of the first to bring up the idea to address the budget shortfall during that year’s budget discussions.

20. Kevin Kiley – Republican congressman with nothing to lose. Unconstrained since his seat was redrawn into a blue-leaning district under Prop. 50, Kiley has become outspoken in attacking leadership in his own party over its failure to stop redistricting, the shutdown and health care subsidies. He has pledged to run for reelection but hasn’t announced which district he’s running in, leaving the possibility open for an intraparty challenge in 2026.

21. Mai Vang – Sacramento City councilmember challenges old guard. The 40-year-old Vang has set up a Democratic primary challenge to incumbent Rep. Doris Matsui, who has held California’s 7th Congressional District seat since 2005. Vang, a strong progressive voice on the City Council, is among a number of younger Democrats challenging older incumbents — Matsui is 80 and one of California’s longest-serving members of Congress.

22. Nancy Pelosi – what next? Pelosi will step down later this year after nearly 40 years in Congress. A highly competitive race is developing to replace the former speaker in the San Francisco seat. If Pelosi chooses to endorse one of the main candidates (Wiener, Chan, or Chakrabarti), it might tip the scale in their favor.

23. Gov. Gavin Newsom – eyes on California, or beyond, in 2026? The term-limited governor is facing a serious budget deficit. As he eyes a 2028 presidential run, will he focus on addressing the state’s budget to bolster his financial stewardship credentials, or avoid tough spending decisions and kick that particular issue down the road? To what degree, too, will Newsom's national ambitions impact his final year in office, particularly regarding crucial legislation around housing, gas prices and AI?

24. John Laird – new key role for state senator. Limón, the new Senate leader, just appointed Laird as the new budget chair, replacing Wiener. With the state facing a huge, ongoing structural deficit, Laird inherits a key role that will help shape how serious Democrats will be in limiting spending to address the $18 billion shortfall.

25. Sabrina Cervantes – rising Inland Empire senator. She was appointed last month as chair of the Senate Committee on Appropriations by Limón. Alongside Laird, she holds a key position as the committee can have a substantial impact on the state’s fiscal position.

26. Mia Bonta – assemblymember; leading voice on health care funding. As chair of the Assembly Health Committee, Bonta is a leading voice in debates on how, and whether, California can address the enormous funding gaps in its health care system as a result of last year’s congressional tax cut and spending bill.

Also worth watching: Aisha Wahab – state senator; first Afghan American legislator in California. Viewed as labor and tenant-friendly, she could move quickly into higher leadership or statewide roles. Isaac Bryan – former Assembly majority leader; key progressive in the Assembly. Bryan may play a significant role in budget fights, police reform and housing equity. Fiona Ma – state treasurer; term-limited in 2026. Ma is running for lieutenant governor to replace Eleni Kounalakis, whose term ends in January 2027.
