*The Lempert Report — April 6, 2026*

When the Grandson Bites Back: How One Family’s Outrage Just Changed What’s Inside Your Reese’s

Just last week on April 1, 2026, The Hershey Company made an announcement that sounded like it had to be a joke. After weeks of viral outrage, media appearances, and a LinkedIn letter that shook one of America’s most beloved candy brands to its chocolate-coated core, Hershey declared it would reverse course and return all Reese’s products to their classic milk chocolate and peanut butter recipes. April Fools’ Day. You can’t make this up. Except this time, nobody was laughing — least of all the 70-year-old grandson of the man who invented the peanut butter cup, who spent six weeks watching a corporation scramble to protect a legacy it had been quietly dismantling one cheaper ingredient at a time. This is not a prank. This is a $12 billion company being brought to its knees by a single bite of a Valentine’s Day candy.

That man is Brad Reese. His grandfather, H.B. Reese, invented the peanut butter cup in 1928. On Valentine’s Day 2026, Brad picked up a bag of Reese’s Unwrapped Chocolate Peanut Butter Crème Mini Hearts — a seasonal product his family’s name is plastered across — took a bite, and threw the bag in the trash.

“It didn’t taste like milk chocolate,” he told NBC News. “It tasted cheap.”

He was right. What happened next is one of the most revealing food industry stories of the year — one that touches on skimpflation, consumer trust, the cost of cocoa, tariffs, and the price a brand pays when it quietly rewrites the recipe that built it.

Reading the Label That Changed Everything

When Brad Reese flipped over that Valentine’s bag and read the ingredient list, he wasn’t just a disappointed customer. He was a man confronting corporate math applied to his family’s legacy. What he found was “chocolate candy” and “peanut butter crème” — not the “milk chocolate” and “peanut butter” that defined his grandfather’s original recipe. That distinction is not just semantics. Under FDA standards, the term “milk chocolate” requires at least 10% chocolate liquor. If a product can’t legally use that term on the front of the package, guess what? It’s not milk chocolate.

On Valentine’s Day, Brad posted an open letter to Hershey’s corporate brand manager on LinkedIn. “My grandfather built Reese’s on a simple, enduring architecture: milk chocolate + peanut butter,” he wrote. “But today, Reese’s identity is being rewritten — not by storytellers, but by formulation decisions that replace milk chocolate with compound coatings and peanut butter with peanut butter-style crèmes across multiple Reese’s products.”

The post drew 97,000 likes and more than 7 million views on X, with thousands of comments — nearly all of them agreeing with him. “I got roasted last Halloween at work for saying they aren’t the same anymore and that I don’t even eat them now,” wrote one user. “Vindicated.”

I’ve been in the food industry for more than 30 years. I’ve watched hundreds of brands quietly reformulate their products — swapping real ingredients for cheaper alternatives, trimming an ounce here, replacing cocoa butter with PGPR there. And I’ve watched consumers absorb it, complain about it, and then move on. What’s different this time is that the complaint came from an insider.

The Ingredients We Should All Know

Let me be precise about what’s actually in a classic Reese’s Peanut Butter Cup, because this is where the story gets nuanced. The standard cup’s ingredients are: milk chocolate (sugar, cocoa butter, chocolate, skim milk, milk fat, lactose, lecithin (soy), PGPR), peanuts, sugar, dextrose, salt, and TBHQ & citric acid. Real chocolate is in there — but so is PGPR, a castor oil-derived emulsifier used to reduce the amount of expensive cocoa butter needed, and TBHQ, a chemical preservative. Reese’s Peanut Butter Cups once had 9 ingredients and contained no PGPR, TBHQ, or citric acid. Today’s version has 14.

TBHQ happens to be banned at Whole Foods, which cites it as “unacceptable in food.” That tells us something. And Reese’s Peanut Butter Cups have also gradually shrunk from 0.9 ounce in the 1980s to 0.75 ounce today — nearly a 17% reduction. It’s the classic playbook of shrinkflation.

The classic cup, for all its tweaks, still qualifies as real milk chocolate. But as Brad Reese correctly identified, certain seasonal and specialty Reese’s items are now labeled as “chocolate candy” with “peanut butter creme” rather than the brand’s original milk chocolate and peanut butter formula. Reese’s Mini Eggs sold for Easter — the ones your family may have just finished off yesterday — contain no milk chocolate. Reese’s Pieces has no milk chocolate either. These are products carrying the Reese’s name and orange branding that millions of consumers assume are true to the original classic.

The Business Pressure Behind the Decision

To understand why Hershey made these moves, you have to look at the numbers. And they are not pretty.

Hershey reported full-year 2025 net sales of $11.69 billion, up 4.4%, but reported net income fell more than 60% to $883.3 million. Sales up, profits down. The culprit was commodity costs — primarily cocoa. High cocoa prices have led Hershey and other manufacturers to reformulate by using less chocolate in recent years. The company’s Q4 gross margin declined 17 percentage points, with reported and adjusted gross margin declines reflecting higher commodity costs, incremental tariff expenses and lower volume. Hershey's chocolate and candy manufactured in Mexico and shipped to the U.S. should qualify for USMCA tariff-free treatment; the tariff impact on finished Reese's products from Mexico is likely minimal. However, the tariff squeeze on raw cocoa beans imported into the company’s U.S. factories is very real, which explains why Hershey's profit margins collapsed even as revenue grew, and why there's actually a financial incentive to manufacture MORE in Mexico, not less. That's a rich irony that the administration should take note of.

More broadly, inflation, cost-of-living pressures, and shifting consumer behaviors towards healthier eating are weighing on sales across the sweets category. As household budgets tighten, consumers are also trading down, buying less frequently, or switching to cheaper options.

On a conference call with investors, Hershey CFO Steven Voskuil said: “I would say in all the changes that we’ve made thus far, there has been no consumer impact whatsoever.” Guess he wasn’t told about the millions of people who clicked on Brad Reese’s post before he jumped on the call.

The Reversal

What the food industry almost never does is reverse course. Brands reformulate forward, not backward. So the April 1 announcement from Hershey is worth noting.

Hershey said it will use classic recipes for all Reese’s products starting next year. The company acknowledged that a small portion of Reese’s products — including those mini Easter eggs — are now made with a coating that contains less chocolate, and it is now committed to shifting those products to “their classic milk chocolate and dark chocolate recipes” in 2027. What about doing it before Halloween 2026? That is the biggest candy sales period of the year. Hershey also announced it will transition to natural colors across its sweets portfolio and enhance the Kit Kat recipe to make it creamier.

This is significant. It tells us that the consumer voice, amplified by social media and given credibility by a family name, can still move a $12 billion company. But it also tells us something we should sit with: Hershey knew these products had been reformulated. They did it as part of a plan. They called it “innovation,” naturally. And they described it as having no consumer impact — right up until the moment a 70-year-old man with the right last name made it impossible to ignore.

What This Means for Shoppers Right Now

Until 2027 arrives and those classic recipes are restored, here’s what Brad Reese himself advised consumers: look for “milk chocolate” and “peanut butter” in the ingredients list, not substitutes like “chocolate candy” and “peanut butter creme.” If the front of the package doesn’t say “milk chocolate,” the FDA doesn’t allow it to be there because it isn’t.

The lesson here is one I’ve been preaching for decades: read the label. Not the front panel with its warm orange branding and promises of the perfect peanut butter-chocolate ratio. The back. The ingredient list. The nutritional facts panel. That’s where the real story is, and in this case, it took a grandson’s heartbreak to remind an entire industry of that fact.

H.B. Reese built something remarkable. His family sold it to Hershey in 1963 for $23.5 million in stock — 666,316 shares of Hershey stock. After 63 years of stock splits, those original shares now represent 16 million shares valued at over $4.4 billion, and pay annual cash dividends of $29.9 million.

For decades, Hershey honored what H.B. Reese created. The question now is whether the reversal they announced on April 1, which is almost a year away and affects only a “small portion” of products, is genuine stewardship of a great American brand, or just a clever PR move in the face of a viral moment.

Let’s be clear about what happened. A 70-year-old man posted an open letter on LinkedIn, and within six weeks, he forced a $12 billion company to reverse course on one of the most iconic candy brands in American history. That is the power of consumer trust and the very real consequence of betraying it. But the victory is incomplete. Hershey’s reversal doesn’t take effect until 2027, and the hard economics haven’t changed: cocoa prices, packaging, labor, and distribution costs are all headed in one direction — up. The classic recipe is coming back. The question that should keep Hershey’s executives up at night is this: when it does, how much are they going to charge shoppers for it... and will they pay the price?

MAHA's Moment of Reckoning

The movement that helped elect Trump is now threatening to undo his coalition — and the food industry should pay close attention.

There’s a political earthquake rumbling beneath Washington right now, and its aftershocks are headed straight for every supermarket aisle, every food manufacturer’s boardroom, and every commodity board in America. Politico just dropped a bombshell poll, and as you’re in the food business, you need to read it carefully.

The POLITICO Poll, conducted between March 13 and 18 among more than 3,800 U.S. adults, reveals that 52 percent of Americans believe the Trump administration has not done enough to “Make America Healthy Again.” More than half the country says the movement that helped put RFK Jr. at the helm of HHS hasn’t delivered. And here’s the kicker: 41 percent of Trump’s own 2024 voters agree.

That’s not a small crack in the coalition. That’s the Grand Canyon.

The poll shows both broad frustration and dissatisfaction with the Trump administration on health priorities, and it reveals real opportunities for Democrats to make inroads with Kennedy’s MAHA supporters. Tony Lyons, president of MAHA Action, put it bluntly in a memo obtained by Politico: Republicans are only “renting MAHA voters” and have not yet “purchased” their loyalty.

For those of us who have tracked the food and grocery industry for decades, this political dynamic is not just fascinating — it’s consequential. Because MAHA voters aren’t a fringe. They’re the shoppers already reading ingredient labels, already asking about seed oils at the deli counter, already choosing brands that have pledged to go dye-free. They are, by and large, the most engaged food consumers in America we have ever witnessed.

The deregulatory instincts of the Trump administration keeps colliding with MAHA’s core demands for stricter rules. The most glaring example is the administration’s decision to let Bayer expand production of Roundup which has particularly angered MAHA voters, given Kennedy’s long history of fighting glyphosate and winning that landmark $289 million jury verdict against Monsanto.

Meanwhile, the food industry has been playing defense at a cost. Trade groups and major manufacturers have poured millions into K Street lobbying as the Trump administration has proved harder to access than in its first term. And the political math is working against them: the Politico poll found that roughly 3 in 10 voters believed the GOP was more susceptible to pressure from the pesticide industry, compared to 2 in 10 who say the same about Democrats — a perception problem that is only getting worse as MAHA voters drift.

Here’s what the food retail industry must understand right now. A separate Politico/Public First poll conducted in November 2025 found that nearly half of Americans now consider grocery prices the “most challenging” household expense, outranking housing and health care — and that creates a dangerous tension. Shoppers want cleaner food AND they want affordable food. They aren’t willing to choose. The brands and retailers who figure out how to deliver both will win.

More than a hundred bills targeting sugar, synthetic dyes, and chemical additives have been introduced across statehouses this year, nearly five times the number from last year. The federal government may be gridlocked, but the states are not waiting.

The clearest signal for food companies? MAHA supporters, the Politico Poll found, prioritize issues like combating infectious diseases, regulating “forever chemicals,” and expanding access to reproductive health care — positions that clearly cut across party lines.

The food industry’s window to get ahead of this both proactively and authentically is narrowing fast. The brands that treat MAHA as a political football to be managed will lose. The ones that treat it as a genuine signal from consumers about what they want to feed their families will lead.

This Politico poll isn’t just about midterm politics. It’s a roadmap for the food industry.

California Just Redrew the Map for Every Grocery Store, Manufacturer and State in America

Walk into any supermarket in the country today and roughly 70% of what you see on those shelves is, by most scientific measures, ultra-processed food. Not just the obvious stuff — the chips and the soda and the hot dogs. We’re talking about the breakfast cereals, the flavored yogurts, the packaged bread, the frozen meals, the snack bars we tell ourselves are healthy. U.S. adults on average receive 53% of their daily calories from ultra-processed foods, according to the CDC — and for children, that number climbs to 62%.

We’ve known about this for years. We have studied it, debated it, written about it, and largely done very little about it — until now.

What California Has Already Done — and What’s Coming Next

Last October, Governor Gavin Newsom signed AB 1264 into law — and with that signature, California became the first state in the nation to legally define ultra-processed food. The “Real Food, Healthy Kids Act” makes California the first state in the United States to define and ultimately phase out unhealthy ultra-processed foods from what’s projected to be over one billion meals served to California schoolchildren annually.

California’s new law broadly defines “ultra-processed food” as any food or beverage that contains one or more ingredients with specified technical effects — such as emulsifiers, stabilizers, colors, flavors, flavor enhancers, and non-nutritive sweeteners — and either contains high amounts of saturated fat, sodium, or added sugar, or contains a non-nutritive sweetener or certain other specified substances.

The timeline is deliberate. The law officially took effect January 1, 2026, with schools beginning to phase out restricted ultra-processed foods starting in 2029, with a full vendor prohibition by 2032 and full school implementation by 2035.

California didn’t stop there. On March 25, 2026, just a couple of weeks ago, Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel introduced AB 2244, and this bill goes further than schools.

AB 2244 would establish a first-of-its-kind “California Certified” seal that food manufacturers could place on products that meet clear standards for not being ultra-processed — modeled after the USDA Organic label. And here’s where it gets real for grocery retailers: grocery stores generating more than $10 million in annual revenue, and carrying at least 25 certified products, would be required to display those items prominently in high-traffic areas such as entrances or checkout zones. FMI–The Food Industry Association reports the average annual sales per supermarket in 2024 was $37,013,912.

California is telling grocery stores where to put food on their shelves — a move that should give every category manager in this country a splitting headache.

This Is Not Just a California Story

I’ve watched California reshape national food policy for over three decades. The organic certification movement. The food dye bans. The additive restrictions. Every single time, the same thing happens: California moves, the industry grumbles, adapts, and then the rest of the country follows. Lawmakers in other states watch what happens, consumers in other states demand the same, and manufacturers who don’t want to produce separate SKUs for California, versus everyone else, reformulates nationwide.

More than half the nation began working on food dye bans after California passed one in 2024. The same domino effect is already in motion for UPFs. Texas passed a law requiring warning labels on products containing any of 44 specific additives — a measure that the food industry challenged on First Amendment grounds, and a federal judge temporarily blocked. Louisiana and Wisconsin followed with their own versions. The legislative momentum is unmistakable.

Assemblymember Gabriel said it himself on a press call: “We would love to see Washington follow our lead. But until then, there’s no reason companies couldn’t use the California seal in grocery stores outside of California.”

He’s right. And companies will. Because the consumer demand is already there, the regulatory pressure is building, and the financial incentive of preferred shelf placement is not something manufacturers or retailers are going to ignore. And then there is California’s undeniable leadership in health and wellness — the state is consistently the largest organic food market in the country, and that gravitational pull on national consumer preferences is only growing.

What This Means for CPG

It is estimated that a third of packaged foods would be considered not ultra-processed under California’s definition, according to nutrition policy researcher Alyssa Moran of the University of Pennsylvania. That means roughly two-thirds of the packaged food supply — the products manufacturers have spent decades engineering for palatability, shelf life, and cost efficiency — are now in California’s crosshairs.

The California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment is required to produce a more specific definition for UPFs by July 1, 2026. That definition, once finalized, becomes the compliance target. Manufacturers who want the “California Certified” seal will need to reformulate. Those who don’t will find their products relegated to wherever the store chooses to put them — which, under AB 2244, will not be the high-traffic entrance zones reserved for certified products.

What This Means for Grocery Retailers

The shelf-placement mandate in AB 2244 is the provision the grocery industry needs to pay the closest attention to right now. This is not a labeling requirement that affects manufacturers. This is a direct operational directive to retailers — telling them how to merchandise their stores, where to place products, and by implication, what their store layout should prioritize.

Think about what this actually means at store level. Entrances and checkout zones are prime real estate. Those placements are negotiated, paid for, and strategically managed. AB 2244 would effectively require retailers — once they carry a threshold number of certified products — to give that prime real estate to “California Certified” non-UPF foods. That is a fundamental shift in how category management works. It inverts the traditional pay-to-play model of retail placement.

For grocery retailers, this should be a wake-up moment. The risk is real: if you ignore it, wait for the law to pass, and then scramble to reorganize your store layout and renegotiate vendor relationships under regulatory pressure, you’ve already lost.

Consumer awareness of the “California Certified” designation will spread — just as USDA Organic spread far beyond the farmers’ markets where it first took hold.

What This Means for the Other 49 States

Here is the scenario I want every food retailer, manufacturer, and state legislator to sit with: it is 2028. The California definition of ultra-processed food is finalized. Hundreds of products carry the “California Certified” seal. Manufacturers have reformulated the products they want to sell into California schools and preferred shelf positions. Consumer awareness of the seal is growing.

That is how national change happens. Not through federal mandates. Through the quiet, powerful, relentless force of an informed consumer who has been given a new lens through which to evaluate what she’s buying.

UPFs have become a governance issue that sits awkwardly between public health, procurement rules, education policy, and corporate accountability.

Washington has been slow. The MAHA Commission’s final strategy report, released in September 2025, called for the government to “continue efforts” to define ultra-processed foods — falling well short of the decisive action many had hoped for. The FDA and USDA are still seeking public comment on how to define the category. While federal agencies deliberate, California is building compliance frameworks, certification programs, shelf-placement mandates, and ingredient disclosure databases.

My Take…

I’ve said for years that the future of food retail belongs to the store that helps consumers make better decisions — not just more decisions. California’s twin-track approach is to define what ultra-processed means, then reward the foods that don’t qualify. In my view, this is the most coherent food policy framework any U.S. government body has produced.

It’s not perfect. The scientific debate over UPF definitions is real, and there will be products that fall into gray areas. But imperfect action is better than perfect inaction — and California has been proving that in food policy for 30 years.

For CPG and store brands, the message is simple: the cost of reformulation today is far less than the cost of regulatory non-compliance tomorrow. For retailers, the message is equally clear: the stores that win the next decade will be the ones that made it easy for shoppers to find better food.

And the Reese’s and California stories are not separate. They are the same story — one told by a family name, the other told by a legislature. Both are forcing the food industry to answer a question it has spent decades avoiding: what, exactly, is in your food?

A word to those other 49 states: pay attention!

California just drew the map. The rest of the country will follow.
