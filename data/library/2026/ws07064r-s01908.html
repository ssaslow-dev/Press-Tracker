Newsom signs first-in-the-nation bills targeting federal immigration enforcement in California

By Jenny Huh
Return to Homepage
  • Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package of bills addressing federal immigration enforcement in California, including a ban on "shock gloves" and a 25% annual tax on private, for-profit detention facilities that contract with ICE.
  • The new laws also prohibit federal agents from using state resources for enforcement staging, processing, detention, or operations, and create independent state investigations into fatal shootings involving federal immigration agents.
  • The White House and Department of Homeland Security responded to the new laws, stating that federal immigration enforcement is a federal responsibility and that ICE will continue deporting criminal illegal aliens despite California's legislation.

Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package of bills Tuesday morning addressing federal immigration enforcement in California, setting up another potential clash between the state and the Trump administration over who has authority where.

For California Democratic lawmakers, these efforts began in January, at the start of the legislative session, with several of the bills set to take effect soon.

"California is proud of the fact that we offer sanctuary," Democratic Assemblymember Alex Lee, co-author of several of the signed bills, said.

By law -- Senate Bill 54 -- California state and local law enforcement agencies cannot assist with federal immigration enforcement efforts.

One of the new laws temporarily bans "shock gloves," which can deliver an electric shock when they come into contact with skin.

Concerns over the electroshock technology grew after reports that ICE may begin using the gloves. Law enforcement officials, including Sacramento County Sheriff Jim Cooper, strongly opposed California's ban, arguing the gloves could be an effective de-escalation tool.

"It is insane that Democrat politicians are taking this tool our of the tool kit of law enforcement," Republican Assemblymember Carl DeMaio said. "Well then why don't you study it first, and then if there's a problem, you can ban it."

But Assemblymember Lee countered that the devices should not be considered a de-escalation tool once an officer has made physical contact with someone.

"Once you lay hands on someone, you're not really de-escalating anymore; you're subduing, you're dominating someone," Lee said.

The ban starts in January and will remain in place until 2030. It also requires a state study of safety and community impacts. The law does not apply to conventional tasers, stun guns, or similar technologies utilized by law enforcement.

Another law takes effect July 2028 and would impose a 25% annual tax on the gross income of private, for-profit detention facilities that contract with ICE. There are eight such facilities in California, operated by two companies -- The GEO Group and CoreCivic. 

"There are massive corporations here in our state that are profiting off of family separation, human suffering," bill author Democratic Assemblymember Matt Haney said. "We might not be able to control everything about federal immigration policy, but we do have the ability to regulate those who are doing business in our state."

Haney said facilities that do not want to pay the tax could leave California.

The revenue would be used for immigration services, including legal assistance for detainees.

The bill was amended from its original form, which proposed a 50% annual tax on the gross receipts of these facilities. Implementation was also delayed until 2028. Gross income starting in 2027 will be taxed. 

"To make sure that our state resources are used to protect our residents and not to harm them. These are taxpayer dollars. They should be benefiting them," Democratic Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel said.

Under two bills by Gabriel, starting in January, federal agents would be barred from using state resources, including state-owned property such as parking lots, for enforcement staging, processing, detention, or operations.

The measures would also create independent state investigations into fatal shootings involving federal immigration agents.

"We want to make sure that we're not part of [immigration operations], that we're not assisting it," Gabriel said.

Legal challenges may follow over the question of state versus federal authority.

The White House responded to the new laws, writing, "Gavin Newsom has no authority over federal law enforcement. The Trump administration will not abide by his unconstitutional legislation. Our law enforcement officers will continue arresting and removing criminal illegal aliens from American communities while radical Democrats in California refuse to cooperate and instead choose to release criminal illegal aliens from their jails into communities to terrorize innocent Americans."

And the Department of Homeland Security, which oversees ICE, responded, "Enforcing federal immigration laws is a clear federal responsibility under Article I, Article II, and the Supremacy Clause. No tax will stop ICE from deporting criminal illegal aliens to make California safe again. We need California to cooperate with our officers and stop RELEASING criminals from their jails into California's neighborhoods. 7 of the 10 safest cities in America cooperate with ICE."

The email statement continued, "ICE is constantly assessing the needs of our officers in the field to ensure they have the tools and equipment necessary to safely arrest and remove criminal illegal aliens from our country. Every decision is made with careful consideration and appropriately reviewed to ensure that any technology ICE utilizes is consistent with all applicable law enforcement policies and standards. Our officers are highly trained in de-escalation tactics and regularly receive ongoing use of force training. The latest ICE use of force policy is available here. While California sanctuary politicians continue to release pedophiles, rapists, gang members, and murderers onto their streets, our brave law enforcement will continue to risk their lives to arrest these heinous criminals and make California safe again."

Other immigration bills signed by Newsom in this more-than-20-bill-package include a ban on local, state, and federal law enforcement using extreme masking.

The bill by Democratic state Senator Scott Wiener went through several amendments and legal challenges before reaching the governor's desk.

Supporters of the bill say the intention of Senate Bill 1004 is to limit the use of ski masks or any unnecessary obscuring of law enforcement officials' faces. Supporters say identification is a crucial part of holding agencies accountable.

The bill does offer exemptions for safety reasons, for instance, like with motorcycle helmets and SWAT gear.

Another Wiener bill, called the "No Kings Act," was also signed by Newsom.

This would allow people to sue federal immigration agents for alleged constitutional violations. Supporters say under current law, individuals may sue state and local law enforcement officers for constitutional violations but that federal officers are largely shielded from similar lawsuits.

Another bill would require clear identification on rental vehicles if these cars are being used by law enforcement for detention or arrest purposes in the state. Another protects people from civil arrest while they are accessing California courthouses for lawful purposes.

The bill package also includes barring immigration officials who have committed serious misconduct from becoming peace officers or public employees in California.
