California Creates First State-Level Non-Ultraprocessed Food Label Amid Federal Definition Gap

State certification program launches ahead of federal standards, raising compliance questions for food manufacturers

California has enacted the nation’s first state-level certification program for non-ultraprocessed foods, establishing a voluntary front-of-package seal intended to help shoppers identify products free from industrial additives. Gov. Gavin Newsom signed Assembly Bill 2244 into law on Monday, directing the California Department of Public Health to design a label modeled after the USDA Organic seal and to accredit certifying agents by June 1, 2028.

The legislation moves forward despite the absence of a federal definition for ultraprocessed foods (UPFs). The U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the Department of Agriculture are currently collaborating to establish uniform national standards, but no timeline for completion exists. California’s preemptive action creates an immediate regulatory framework that could serve as a de facto national standard or trigger a patchwork of conflicting state requirements.

Under the new law, grocery retailers must prominently display products bearing the “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified” seal. The state health department will also maintain a public online registry of certified items. While the specific criteria for the consumer-facing label are still being developed, existing California statute defines ultraprocessed foods for school meals as items containing high levels of saturated fat, sodium, added sugar, or non-nutritive sweeteners, including both artificial options like aspartame and plant-based alternatives such as stevia.

Industry experts warn that state-level certification ahead of federal guidance introduces significant compliance complexity. Anna Rosales, vice president of science and policy at the Institute of Food Technologists, noted that manufacturers, retailers, and institutions may soon face divergent interpretations of what qualifies as ultra-processed across different jurisdictions. If other states adopt their own definitions before Washington acts, food companies could be forced to navigate multiple, potentially contradictory labeling regimes for the same products.

The scientific foundation for regulating UPFs relies primarily on the Nova classification system, developed in the early 2000s by nutritional epidemiologist Carlos Monteiro at the University of São Paulo. Nova categorizes foods by processing degree rather than nutrient content alone, flagging products containing preservatives, flavor enhancers, and other industrial substances not typically used in home cooking. U.S. researchers widely use Nova because no domestic equivalent exists, though UC Davis experts have cautioned that most evidence linking UPFs to adverse health outcomes remains observational.

Recent clinical research has begun to strengthen that evidentiary base. A study published last year by USC’s Keck School of Medicine found associations between increased UPF consumption and higher prediabetes risk using blood sample analysis, moving beyond self-reported dietary data. Nevertheless, the distinction between minimally processed and ultraprocessed foods remains difficult for consumers to parse without standardized labeling, a gap AB 2244 aims to address.

Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), the bill’s author, framed the measure as a bipartisan effort to protect families while federal agencies remain stalled. The law does not ban any products or mandate reformulation; it creates a market signal through verified labeling. Certification will be voluntary, meaning participation depends on whether manufacturers believe the seal carries sufficient consumer recognition to justify the cost of third-party verification.

The California Department of Public Health must now draft technical specifications for the certification process, select accredited agents, and finalize the visual design of the seal well before the 2028 accreditation deadline. Stakeholder input from food scientists, nutritionists, and industry representatives is expected to shape how strictly the state interprets “non-ultraprocessed” for retail products versus the existing school meal definition.
