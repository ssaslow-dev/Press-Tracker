How 2026’s Wild Harvest Is Reshaping Wine

The 2026 vintage is garnering headlines for all of the wrong reasons. After a spring of grape-destroying hail and frost caused losses of up to 90% in regions of the US and France, harvest kicked off weeks ahead of the “normal” schedule in France, California, Spain and Italy, with very little to show.

The French Ministry of Agriculture is forecasting the lowest production levels in 70 years, and California growers are expecting, in some cases, 50% less than usual.

Amid record-low wine consumption levels, a micro yield is causing less concern than it would have 10 or 20 years ago, but vintners are concerned that climate change is becoming, instead of just a perennial problem, an existential crisis.

Julien Brustis, winemaker and CEO at Château Tour des Termes in Saint-Estèphe, explains that while he, like many of his colleagues, looks to the past for inspiration, current and future conditions require a shift in mindset and approach.

“We look at what made Bordeaux great in the past, but we’re not trying to recreate the past,” Brustis explains. “We use today’s knowledge to think about what will make Bordeaux great in 2055. And the rule behind it is quite simple: no compromise between great wine and a better future.”

Why Winemakers Value Old Vines—and How a New Seal Help You Find Them

“Old vines” may sound straightforward, but it’s one of the wine world’s more ambiguous terms. Does it mean vines that are 20 years old? Thirty? Fifty? And when those words appear on a bottle, how can shoppers know exactly what they mean?

A new seal from the Old Vine Registry aims to provide an answer. Launching October 1, the OVR Seal will identify wines made with grapes from registered vineyards that meet specific age requirements.

“One of the problems with the generic term ‘old vines’ is that wine producers can put those two words on their labels without any real regulatory oversight and without providing any additional information to the consumer,” explains wine journalist Alder Yarrow, the architect and manager of the Old Vine Registry.

“The Old Vine Registry defines an old vineyard as one that was planted at least 35 years ago at minimum,” Yarrow continues. The OVR Seal divides qualifying vineyards into five age categories: 35+, 50+, 75+, 100+, and 150+ years, provided that at least 85% of the grapes come from officially registered vineyards.

The seal provides far more detail than a simple “vieilles vignes” designation, Yarrow adds. Consumers can also scan a QR code or search the registry to learn more about the vineyard.

Dom Pérignon’s New Champagne Drop Features Damien Hirst Work

Dom Pérignon and Damien Hirst are giving Champagne a decidedly artistic upgrade with a lavish duo of bottles featuring new work from the acclaimed British artist.

The famed and at-times-controversial artist lends his considerable skill set to two new label designs, an extension of his Kaleidoscope series. 20,000 Bottles of Rare Wine Worth Millions Unclaimed After Sherry-Lehmann Implosion

Some 20,000 bottles of rare wine worth millions of dollars remain unclaimed more than three years after the implosion of New York City wine store Sherry-Lehmann, The Post has learned.

Cratefuls of rare vintages are sitting in the basement floor of a tower at Blue Hill Plaza, a nondescript office development in Rockland County, after the FBI raided the space as part of an ongoing fraud investigation, according to a lawyer representing the landlord.

The landlord, Hong Kong-based Glorious Sun, took charge of the $16 million collection after Sherry-Lehmann’s storage business defaulted on its rent at Blue Hill Plaza. Now, it is scrambling to return the wines to their original owners in order to rent the space to a paying tenant, the attorney said.

Glorious Sun even placed an ad in Wine Specator this summer.

“Attention” the ad states in bright red all caps lettering. “We may have your wine. If you stored wine with Sherry-Lehmann (or Wine Caves) please contact Blue Hill Plaza.”

THE TRANSPARENCY BEAT

What To Know About California’s New Ultra-Processed Foods Label

California Gov. Gavin Newsom signed a bill that would allow food companies to put a non-ultra-processed food (UPF) label on their products.

The law creates the first seal in the U.S. that would appear on products as long as they meet standards for not being ultra-processed, according to California lawmaker Jesse Gabriel, who sponsored the bill.

Modeled after the “USDA Organic” label -- which appears on products that meet federal standards set by the U.S. Department of Agriculture -- California’s process would be overseen by the California Department of Public Health.

Food manufacturers would apply to accredited certification agents to use a “California Certified” seal on their packaging. Products would have to be recertified every three years.

The law also requires grocery stores in California with gross sales of more than $10 million to prominently display products with the seal in a “designated high-traffic area.”

The state’s definition of UPFs includes food and beverages that contain a “specified substance” -- such as colors or stabilizers -- and either high amounts of saturated fat, sodium or added sugar or a nonnutritive sweetener.

A Landmark Bill Will Track and Disclose PFAs & Pesticide Use in California

Gov. Gavin Newsom today signed groundbreaking legislation that takes a first step in addressing the widespread use of toxic PFAS “forever chemicals” pesticides on California produce.

The new law, AB 1603, authored by Assemblymember Nick Schultz (D-Burbank), requires the state to monitor and disclose PFAS pesticide use. State agencies do not track the concerning use of toxic PFAS on crops – but that will change now that the Environmental Working Group has raised concerns.

EWG found that more than 2.5 million pounds of PFAS pesticides are applied to Golden State crops each year, contaminating produce – in addition to soil and water – sold in the state and throughout the U.S.

EWG’s research also found PFAS pesticides on nine in 10 California-grown samples of non-organic peaches, nectarines and plums tested by the state, as well as in local waterways and sediment.

The new law establishes a class-based definition of PFAS pesticides, consistent with other California laws and regulations already addressing these chemicals. That definition matters: It ensures tracking and disclosure requirements can’t be sidestepped with chemical-by-chemical loopholes.

The definition would also lay the groundwork for action that avoids “regrettable substitutions” – PFAS replacements that can also have health concerns.

Though thousands of PFAS are used today, comprehensive toxicity data is available about very few. But what is known is alarming: The health harms linked to the most well-studied PFAS, including cancer, suppression of the immune system and reproductive and developmental harm, show up at doses far lower than regulators once considered safe.

Big Food Is Paying Influencers To Combat MAHA

Some of the biggest food and agriculture corporations in the US are funding a campaign that pays social media influencers to combat elements of Robert F Kennedy Jr’s Make America Healthy Again platform, according to an investigation by the Guardian and Documented, an investigative watchdog and journalism project.

The ascension of RFK Jr to secretary of health and human services has boosted his putative campaign to end chronic illness in the US, which he believes is primarily caused by processed foods and dyes, exposure to certain chemicals, and poor farming practices, along with an overuse of what he falsely claims are dangerous vaccines.

A KFF poll from May 2026 reveals that some Maha themes resonate with a broad segment of US adults. About four in 10 say they support the Maha movement, while a majority of the public say there is not enough regulation of pesticides used in agriculture (64%) or of chemical food additives (75%).

But several large corporations are fighting back. An effort by the Center for Food Integrity, an industry-funded non-profit, tracks public sentiment around key issues central to Maha and suggests ways that agricultural interests can counter those narratives by appealing to emotion or scientific reasoning.

They call it Gaha: Growing a Healthier America. Documents show the coordinated effort is tracking Maha closely and researching ways to respond to the movement’s claims without alienating its supporters. They are focused on several key areas: seed oils, pesticides, high-fructose corn syrup, regenerative agriculture, organic farming, raw milk/dairy and “big ag”, internal documents obtained by the Guardian and Documented show.

The campaign involves deploying social media influencers with significant reach online in an effort to rebuild trust in the industries that the Maha movement has made their target. The Gaha influencer campaign officially launched late last year. In February, CFI compiled a report listing dozens of sponsored posts on platforms such as online blogs, Facebook, TikTok and Instagram about key Gaha subjects. So far, at least six influencers have signed on to the Gaha campaign, reaching a total audience of more than 2.6 million viewers.

THE JET SET

How the Travel Agent Became Chic (Again)

Remember travel agents in the ’90s? You’d pull up to a brick-and-mortar storefront, rifle through some Disney World pamphlets, and they’d circle a bunch of options in yellow highlighter. Then the internet arrived with platforms like Google Flights and Expedia, and we all became our own agents, hunched over our laptops with 14 tabs open and the creeping sense that we’d forgotten something.

But now, the travel agent has returned, rebranded as the “travel advisor,” or sometimes as a “trip designer.” They’re found across Instagram, posting a never-ending stream of curated travel content, often with a caption insinuating that all of this could be yours. And just like that—the travel agent became chic again.

The nuance here is that luxury travel advisors are fewer agents than overall trip designers, fixers, negotiators, and, on occasion, miracle workers.

At Embark Beyond, a luxury travel and destination events company, advisor Avi Hagby learned that her client was assigned a “remote stand” on a commercial flight, which means taking a bus from the gate to the plane, which is not, Avi felt, how one begins a holiday. Hagby had three hours to fix it—and a few calls later, including one to an airport authority Avi counts as a friend, the plane, with 118 passengers aboard, was miraculously moved to a proper jet bridge.

And in the world of travel advisors, stories like that are far from uncommon. Another Embark Beyond client once hosted an anniversary party on a chartered 210-foot yacht, but didn’t want their 150 guests to have to remove their heels as yacht customs demand—so every deck was carpeted for a cool $80,000. A honeymooning bride had Dyson hair dryers waiting for her at six stops across Sri Lanka, Laos, and Kenya. When another bride chipped her tooth while boarding a jet to Paris, a cosmetic dentist was booked before her first lunch. When a sheikh was traveling the Seychelles and a storm had swept the sand from their favorite beach, an advisor helped barge in sand from neighboring islands to make it right.

Want the Best Fall Foliage Drive in America? Go Here

Don’t let summer have all the road trip fun. It’s time to put on your driving gloves for fall, and according to travel experts, there’s only one road in the U.S. you need to travel this season: New Hampshire’s Kancamagus Highway.

“I often compare the emotional experience of driving the Kancamagus Highway in autumn to driving California’s Pacific Coast Highway,” Mina Shafiee, founder of Inspo Travel, shared with Travel + Leisure. “The landscapes couldn’t be more different, but both drives make the journey itself the experience.” On the Kancamagus, she added, “You’re not simply looking at fall foliage from a scenic overlook—you feel immersed in the season. There’s the crisp air, the changing canopy surrounding the road, mountain views appearing between the trees, and those moments when you want to pull over simply because the landscape has changed again.”

The Kancamagus, for the uninitiated, is technically a portion of NH Route 112, which runs east to west, connecting Lincoln to Conway. The 34.5-mile drive is a recognized National Scenic Byway, providing drivers with plenty of places to stop to learn about New England history and, naturally, to take in jaw-dropping views of the region’s famed foliage. And really, you won’t want to speed down this road one bit.

“I would build a fall weekend around it,” Shafiee said of taking the slow lane. “Pair the drive with a stay in the White Mountains—Omni Mount Washington Resort for the quintessential grand New England experience, or a smaller inn for something more intimate—and give yourself time to stop, walk, eat locally, and actually experience the season. The beauty of the Kancamagus is that the drive isn’t transportation between experiences; the drive is one of the experiences.”
