The suspense is over for authors of various LGBTQ bills who were waiting to see if they’d survive the California Legislature’s fiscal review process. They are now to be taken up for floor votes in the two chambers, with legislators racing to meet their August 31 deadline to send legislation to Governor Gavin Newsom to sign into law.

As the Bay Area Reporter noted earlier this month, a number of bills related to LGBTQ issues had been placed on the suspense file by the two appropriations committees due to their estimated costs to implement. While the panels in the state Assembly and Senate ended up advancing them, it remains to be seen if Newsom will use his veto pen on the bills.

The Democrat, who is termed out of office this year, has a history of axing bills that come with a price tag. As an expected candidate for his party’s presidential nomination in 2028, Newsom may take an even more penny-pinching approach to his review of legislation this year to bolster his fiscal restraint bona fides ahead of mounting a national race.

One of the more closely watched bills advancing is Senate Bill 1023 by gay state Senator John Laird (D-Santa Cruz) aimed at addressing hiccups with insurance policies covering the cost of an injectable form of the HIV prevention medication PrEP. Some health plans restrict injectable PrEP coverage to their medical benefit for policyholders, which causes problems for nonprofit service providers that furnish it via their own pharmacies in recouping their costs for doing so.

Laird’s bill moved off the Assembly Appropriations Committee’s suspense file August 13 and will now head to a floor vote in the Assembly. One of the Legislative LGBTQ Caucus’ priority bills this session, it is co-sponsored by gay outgoing Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara and the Department of Insurance, statewide LGBTQ advocacy organization Equality California, and nonprofit healthcare providers the San Francisco AIDS Foundation, AIDS Project LA Health, and LA LGBT Center.

“California has long been a leader in HIV prevention and public health,” stated Laird. “SB 1023 helps ensure that providers and clinics can continue offering injectable PrEP to the communities that need it most by removing barriers that limit access to care.”

Also moved off the suspense file Thursday was SB 934 authored by gay Senator Scott Wiener (D-San Francisco) which will allow people more time to bring a civil suit against a practitioner who subjected them to the debunked practice of conversion therapy to try to change their sexual orientation or gender identity. If enacted, the bill will ensure plaintiffs can cite scientific principles and consensus around proper medical care to demonstrate conversion therapy’s harm in bringing forward a malpractice claim.

Under SB 934, anyone under 18 when they were subjected to the therapy could file a claim before age 40. Those older when they underwent conversion therapy would be able to file a claim within 10 years of their last session. A person would also be able to bring a claim within five years of their discovering an injury or illness due to the efforts to change their being LGBTQ.

Also surviving the suspense vote Thursday was SB 1328 introduced by lesbian Senator Sabrina Cervantes (D-Riverside) to require California’s colleges and universities designate a confidential employee at satellite campuses, branch campuses, and outreach centers to support the needs of LGBTQ+ students, faculty, and staff. It follows on a bill passed in 2024 that required such a person on the main campuses of higher education institutions.

SB 998 by Senators Lena Gonzalez (D-Long Beach) and Akilah Weber Pierson (D-San Diego) is also moving to a floor vote. Its provisions include a requirement for the state’s Office of Civil Rights to appoint an LGBTQ+ Discrimination Prevention Coordinator to develop educational materials for school teachers, staff, governing board and body members, administrators, and other local educational agency personnel so they can identify and proactively prevent LGBTQ+ discrimination at their school sites.

The Assembly will also take up SB 1149 authored by Senator María Elena Durazo (D-Los Angeles). It aims to allow workers to take bereavement leave for chosen and extended family members, similar to previous legislation Durazo saw become law that extended paid family leave provisions to workers in need of caring for chosen or extended family members, not merely their immediate family.

Lesbian state Senator Caroline Menjivar (D-San Fernando Valley) saw her SB 608 aimed at ensuring students in grades seven to 12 can get condoms from school-based health centers move forward. Former versions of her legislation were vetoed by Newsom, so it remains to be seen if it will finally become law this year.

SB 1114 from gay Senator Christopher Cabaldon (D-Yolo) aims to strengthen privacy protections for sexual orientation, gender identity, and intersex data by limiting when state agencies can share the SOGI information. If signed into law, it will ensure the LGBTQ data cannot be disclosed outside of California state government, except under limited circumstances.

Bisexual Senator Sasha Renée Pérez (D-Pasadena) changed her SB 957 to focus on requiring social media companies to notify users when they have received an administrative subpoena. Dubbed the Stopping Harmful Information Exploitation and Lawless Data Sharing (SHIELD) Act, it would place restrictions on how such companies respond to subpoenas and allow the state’s attorney general to intervene in such matters.

Out lawmakers and community advocates are backing the bill due to concerns that gender identity and sexual orientation data collected by the online platforms could be used to endanger LGBTQ+ users of them. It will be taken up by the Assembly.

Bills move forward in state Senate

The Senate Appropriations Committee Thursday moved several LGBTQ-related bills off its suspense file and sent them for floor votes. Assembly Bill 1578 authored by gay Assemblymember Corey A. Jackson, Ph.D., (D-Moreno Valley) would require elected school board members and other public office holders across California to complete at least one hour of antihate speech training and education within six months of taking office and subsequently every four years thereafter. The requirement would begin on January 1, 2028, if his bill becomes law.

Two bills authored by gay Assemblymember Rick Chavez Zbur (D–Hollywood) received the backing of the fiscal panel. His AB 1967 would require county social workers to immediately review petitions filed by LGBTQ or straight youth seeking to enter the state’s foster care system due to being abused or neglected at home and determine whether to open a juvenile dependency case within 21 days. It also would ensure young adults ages 18 to 21 are not blocked from extended foster care when they are no longer receiving support and are at risk of homelessness.

Zbur’s AB 1930 aims to strengthen state privacy protections for confidential medical information. It comes amid politically motivated investigations being initiated by the Trump administration and Republican officials in other states who are targeting providers of abortion and gender-affirming care in California and other states.

If it is enacted, business entities in the Golden State would have to notify the California attorney general before responding to a subpoena or inquiry regarding health care they provide that is legally protected under state law. The attorney general would also be allowed to intervene in any civil action brought by a state business entity that faces a legal inquiry or summons related to the care they provide.

AB 1775 by gay Assemblymember Chris Ward (D-San Diego) would allow access to state programs by transgender veterans being forced out of the military under an executive order issued by President Donald Trump last year. It would make sure such service members discharged under the policy would still qualify for housing assistance and professional licensing support afforded to them by the state, as well as be able to utilize discharge upgrade services as they transition back to civilian life.

Trans Army veteran Kat Koehlmoos, a San Francisco resident honorably discharged under the Biden administration, told the B.A.R. it is “great news” that AB 1775 is moving on. She has been advocating for its passage as a board member of SPARTA Pride, which focuses on trans servicemembers and their families, and has several members suing the Trump administration over its transphobic executive order.

“I am glad California is stepping up at the state level to do what it can to support those being unfairly kicked out,” said Koehlmoos.

Also moved off the suspense file was AB 1836 co-authored by Ward and Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino) that would allow nonprofit hosts of community events such as Pride celebrations and other LGBTQ public gatherings to seek funding upward of $500,000 for security measures via the state’s Nonprofit Security Grant Program. It currently covers nonprofit facilities but not off-site events put on by such entities.

And AB 1540 by gay Assemblymember Mark González (D-Los Angeles) also moved on. It aims to have dedicated counselors for LGBTQ callers to California’s 988 suicide prevention hotline via state officials requesting the federal Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration allow it to have a “Press 3” option. The Trump administration eliminated the option at the national level last summer but is supposed to revive it by the end of the year after out congressmembers and their allies secured funding for the LGBTQ-dedicated counselors.

Two other bills are awaiting final votes to move on to the Assembly floor. Intersex youth and adults would be provided protection under AB 1876 authored by Assemblymember Dawn Addis (D-Morro Bay). It would clarify that intersex traits, as well as gender identity and other characteristics, are included under state law prohibiting sex discrimination by health plans and insurers.

AB 2164 authored by Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan (D-Orinda) would prohibit California’s governor from recognizing a request for extradition of a person related to their providing a legally protected health care activity in the Golden State such as gender-affirming care except under certain circumstances.

As the B.A.R. previously reported, Newsom in July signed into law AB 2003 by Assemblymember Marc Berman (D-Menlo Park) that makes permanent an online suicide prevention training program the state had piloted for middle through high school students. Expected to launch by next spring, the course will also be made available to any teacher, administrator, or family with a student enrolled in kindergarten through high school in the state.

As for the bills that make it to his desk in the coming weeks, Newsom will have until September 30 to either sign them into law or veto them.
