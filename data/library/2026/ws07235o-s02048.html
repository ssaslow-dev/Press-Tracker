The Logic of the College Pivot

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, AIPAC’s leadership development division, directed by Jonathan Kessler, pivoted to a highly strategic model. Instead of spending millions of dollars trying to persuade established, stubborn politicians to change their minds, the organization realized it was far more efficient to find future politicians while they were still in college.

The formula was simple. The organization actively monitored student body elections, particularly at top public universities, Ivy League schools, and Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs). If you won the student presidency, or if you were elected to lead the campus College Democrats or College Republicans, you were statistically the most likely person in your peer group to run for public office in twenty years.

Staffers would cold-call these student leaders within days of their election victories. They offered congratulations, invited them to elite leadership summits like the Saban National Political Leadership Training Seminar, and offered fully funded trips to Israel through initiatives like the Campus Allies Mission.

From Student Senate to Capitol Hill

For many of these students, this early outreach built the foundation of their future political careers.

  • Bakari Sellers: In 2004, Sellers was elected student body president of Morehouse College. Within a week, he received a call from AIPAC’s Jonathan Kessler. The organization flew him to national seminars, introduced him to donor networks, and eventually gave him a primetime speaking slot at their national policy conference. Sellers has explicitly credited this early exposure with building the financial and professional network that helped him become the youngest member of the South Carolina state legislature.

  • Senator Cory Booker (D-NJ): While Booker’s intensive Jewish text study began through a close personal friendship with Rabbi Shmuley Boteach during his Rhodes Scholar years at Oxford, his introduction to the broader pro-Israel donor network was deeply intertwined with these student-era relationships.

  • Representative Glenn Ivey (D-MD): Long before winning his seat in Congress, Ivey was actively engaged by pro-Israel student networks during his undergraduate years in student politics at Princeton University.

  • Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-CA): Gabriel was targeted and recruited while serving as the student body president of UC Berkeley. Alongside his classmate Daniel Frankenstein, Gabriel was heavily involved in student senate organizing, establishing a template for campus advocacy that he eventually carried into the California State Assembly.

  • Representative Nick Miccarelli (R-PA): Recruited during his undergraduate years while serving as a leader of the College Republicans, Miccarelli used the policy training and network from the Campus Allies program to transition directly into state-level electoral politics.

The Corporate and Media Off-Ramp

Not every college student who goes through a political pipeline ends up on a ballot. The true scale of this talent acquisition strategy is that it seeds pro-Israel advocates across all major sectors of American influence.

  • Venture Capital: Daniel Frankenstein, who partnered with Jesse Gabriel as student activists at UC Berkeley, used his campus network to eventually co-found JANVEST Capital Partners, a venture capital firm that invests exclusively in Israeli technology startups.

  • Political Strategy & Lobbying: Alex Schriver, who was recruited through the campus pipeline while serving as the National Chairman of the College Republican National Committee, used those connections to transition directly to Capitol Hill as Chief of Staff to U.S. Representative Bradley Byrne. Today, he operates as a senior public affairs executive and lobbyist.

  • Corporate Law & Presidential Campaigns: Micah Fielden was recruited immediately after completing his term as Student Body President of the University of Kentucky. He used the connections made on his campus trip to secure a role on Mitt Romney’s 2012 presidential campaign, eventually transitioning into a career at elite corporate law firms.

  • Media & National Journalism: The social networks built at these student summits also shaped media landscapes. For example, national journalist Erielle Reshef, who is married to Daniel Frankenstein, was also deeply involved in the same network of campus advocacy during her formative years.

The Invisible Infrastructure

At its peak, this campus initiative brought four hundred to five hundred college student leaders to Washington twice a year. This annual cohort typically included up to three hundred student body presidents and over one hundred leaders of College Democrats and College Republicans from across the nation.

Only a tiny fraction of these students ever run for Congress. Most of them become corporate lawyers, venture capitalists, public relations executives, or behind-the-scenes political consultants. They do not wear name tags identifying themselves as former campus recruits, and they do not list student leadership seminars on their professional resumes.

This is not a shadow government. It is simply a highly disciplined talent filter that understood how to play the long game. By investing in twenty-year-old student leaders, they built an invisible, bipartisan infrastructure that still defines American foreign policy and industry decades later.
