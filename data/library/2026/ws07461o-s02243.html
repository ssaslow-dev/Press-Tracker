Minneapolis killings spur Sacramento lawmaking

FILING SUIT: A bill that would strengthen Californians’ ability to sue federal officials on claims of violating their constitutional rights has taken on new urgency as lawmakers rush to respond to the fatal shootings in Minnesota.

San Francisco state Sen. Scott Wiener announced the legislation last year, long before federal agents fatally shot Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis this month amid an immigration crackdown in the city. But the so-called “No Kings Act” — which lawmakers will likely advance from the Senate today — gives Democrats a new avenue to respond to the killings.

Wiener told reporters at a news conference this morning that the events this month “have absolutely cast a spotlight on this bill in terms of why it’s so important.”

“Our local police officers and state police officers are already subject to this standard,” Wiener said. “If a police officer shoots your mother in the face or publicly executes an ICU nurse or kidnaps a preschooler and then holds the preschooler hostage as leverage to get the parents to come into custody, you can sue those police officers right now. You can’t sue federal agents effectively.”

After the weekend shooting of Pretti, Democrats returned to Sacramento this week determined to respond to the incident with additional legislation. Republicans blasted what they cast as California’s overly permissive posture on immigration.

Assemblymembers Jesse Gabriel and Juan Carrillo are already planning bills that would prohibit state land from being used for immigration operations and require the attorney general to investigate shootings involving federal immigration agents, as Nicole Norman reports.

The legislation comes as another Wiener bill banning federal agents from wearing masks remains tied up in federal court. The state isn’t enforcing the law — which took effect on Jan. 1 — as officials wait to see whether a judge will block it, as requested by the U.S. Department of Justice.

Asked if a court decision striking down the mask law could negatively affect his lawsuit bill, Wiener said the two aren’t the same because “we actually created a new obligation applicable to local and federal law enforcement, and so that’s a different legal issue to be litigated here.”

“We’re not creating a new obligation,” he added during the news conference. “We’re just saying you’re obligated to follow the U.S. Constitution, federal law. You can be sued if you don’t follow it.”

However, Wiener, who’s running to succeed former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi in Congress, told Playbook in an interview he expects a federal challenge if the bill is signed into law “because they like to sue us when they can.”

“But we think we have a very solid legal basis for this bill,” he added.

WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW TODAY

STOP THE SCROLL: A trial kicking off today in Los Angeles will weigh one of the most fraught questions of the digital age: whether social media giants are responsible for addicting kids, Tyler Katzenberger and Christine Mui report this morning.

It marks the first time that tech behemoths behind social media platforms that reshaped modern life and communication — specifically Meta and YouTube — will be forced before a U.S. jury to confront allegations that their product designs fostered youth health risks like depression and anxiety. Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg and Instagram head Adam Mosseri could be called to testify.

“This is the first time families have ever had their right to a day in court,” said Matthew Bergman, an attorney representing the 19-year-old Northern California woman, identified only by initials as K.G.M. in court documents, who’s suing the companies. ”This is a historic point.”

The trial began this morning with jury selection in Los Angeles Superior Court and is a bellwether for hundreds of similar suits pending in California state court. A verdict in favor of K.G.M. could require companies to pay damages or alter their platform designs — and encourage them to settle in the other lawsuits.

IN OTHER NEWS

NO TIKTOK TESTIMONY: TikTok, another defendant in the social media addiction case, reached a confidential settlement to avoid trial hours before jury selection began, as Tyler reports for POLITICO Pro subscribers.

A plaintiff attorney familiar with the deal said that the parties in the case reached a closed-door agreement Monday evening before notifying a California state court judge. The attorney was granted anonymity to share details about private discussions.

A fourth defendant, Snapchat parent company Snap Inc., reached an undisclosed settlement last week.

RECOVERY TUG-OF-WAR: Donald Trump is pushing to take over wildfire recovery in Los Angeles, Liam Dillon reports.

Trump signed an executive order Friday clearing the way for the federal government to strip authority from the state and local governments to permit the rebuilding of homes and other structures destroyed in the January 2025 fires.

“I want to see if we can take over the city and state and just give the people their permits they want to build,” Trump told the California Post when he signed the order.

The president’s move is an unprecedented incursion by the federal government into disaster recovery. Issuing permits for home rebuilding have long been under the control of individual cities and counties, and it’s unclear how the order would affect the ongoing process.

The president’s order calls on the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the Small Business Administration to consider new regulations to preempt local and state permitting rules and allow builders to certify to a federal agency that they’re in compliance with health and safety regulations to receive approval to build.
