Food Labels Tout Non-Ultraprocessed Food in Bid to Fix US Diets

Skye Witley

Packaged food labels boasting products that aren’t ultraprocessed are popping up in grocery aisles across the country, with California shoppers soon to see a state-sponsored version hitting shelves.

A new state law enacted last week joins an emerging crop of third-party certifications aiming to help consumers identify minimally processed foods.

Their rising popularity dovetails with growing concerns over the health impacts of ultraprocessed products containing industrial ingredients, which accounted for more than half the calories Americans consumed between August 2021 and August 2023, according to a 2025 report from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

In response, the White House, federal courts, Congress, and states are all seeing heightened activity around ultraprocessed products made by food and beverage giants.

“The reason why front of package labels matter is because consumers only spend a few seconds making decisions about what to purchase, and so they rely on shortcuts to be able to make those decisions, particularly around nutrition information, because nutrition information is very complex,” said Lindsey Smith Taillie, a nutrition epidemiologist and co-director of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill’s Global Food Research Program.

California’s state-backed seal follows the launch of three privately offered certification programs enabling manufacturers to market their products as non-ultraprocessed, the first of which began operating in December 2024.

Proponents argue the labels will help consumers distinguish healthier options while incentivizing manufacturers to invest in less processed products. California Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D), who sponsored the bill behind the state’s new seal, likened it to the USDA Organic label, which helped standardize a market worth $70 billion in 2025.

“You’re giving consumers more information,” Gabriel said in an interview. “But I think you could also imagine a lot of scenarios where companies will want the benefits of being able to advertise and put the seal on their product — and so they may be inclined to reformulate.”

Some public health experts, however, are concerned the rapid deployment of these labels, which all use different criteria, risks confusing consumers.

“Labels are a roadmap for industry and for consumers, and if you have the wrong map, you’re going to lead consumers in a wrong direction,” said Dariush Mozaffarian, a public health scientist and director of the Food is Medicine Institute at Tufts University.

Industry Pressure

These proliferating options for marketing food as non-ultraprocessed are designed to appeal to an industry under pressure.

There’s no federal standard informing what is or is not ultraprocessed, but the White House is currently reviewing a definition advanced by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., which he’s said may eventually inform federal labeling standards.

California and Arizona are the only states with statutory definitions, though other states have attempted to establish legal definitions.

Several leading food manufacturers are also defending against lawsuits alleging ultraprocessed foods contributed to plaintiffs’ development of diseases such as type 2 diabetes.

Food companies have cited the scrutiny in filings to the US Securities and Exchange Commission this year, and in August, CEOs urged the White House to delay defining ultraprocessed foods for fear it could intensify the litigation.

Those pressures could “increase our compliance costs or adversely affect consumer demand for certain of our products,” The Campbell’s Company said in its annual 10-K form filed Sept. 24 to the SEC.

The Power of Labels

Because California’s seal may not hit grocery shelves for several years, private certifiers argue their labels can fill the gap and help consumers vote with their dollars.

“We collectively have the power to change the way our food is grown and made without relying on government action,” said Megan Westgate, the CEO of Non-UPF Verified, one of the three private certifiers deploying these labels.

Westgate, whose organization urged Newsom’s office to veto the state-backed seal, said her label is already being deployed on hundreds of products.

California’s new law, meanwhile, gives the Department of Public Health until June 2029 to finalize its seal.

A government-backed seal can help eliminate consumer skepticism about the varying standards and profit motives of private labels, said Alyssa Moran, a University of Pennsylvania professor who advised Gabriel’s office on the label.

Two of the privately-endorsed non-ultraprocessed labels are offered by nonprofits that levy verification fees for their seal’s use, as does the third label offered by technology company WISECode.

Public Health Implications

Critics say the patchwork of labels with varying thresholds risks confusing consumers and is too incremental to substantially change consumer’s diets in a way that will improve their health.

“The reason why governments like to introduce them is because it lets them — I don’t know a nicer way to say this — basically claim to have taken action while avoiding the complex legal or regulatory process that would be required to do the mandatory warning labels,” said Smith Taillie.

Warning labels can incite legal challenges. A federal judge recently blocked a Texas law mandating warnings on products containing certain ingredients on free speech grounds, after which Louisiana delayed its implementation of a similar law by a year. A New Mexico warning label targeting products made with PFAS was also blocked by a federal judge in September.

There’s alos concern that companies could intentionally place non-ultraprocessed labels on products with poor nutrient profiles as a way to make them appear healthier.

“You are going to end up with a version of all-natural Cheetos that are still made through the same processes of Cheetos, but they’re not using any of those additives,” Smith Tallie said.

California’s approach isn’t perfect, Moran said. But it provides a testing ground to assess how well the labeling scheme influences consumers and industry.

“Now we’ll have the data to be able to kind of build on California’s experience and improve our food policies for the future,” she said.
