California steps out front in targeting ultraprocessed food

10/07/26 6:03 AM By Tim Hearden No Comments
  • California created a first‑in‑the‑nation voluntary “non‑ultraprocessed” food seal, launching in 2029, even as federal agencies work toward a unified UPF definition.
  • Experts warn this could lead to state-by-state inconsistencies for manufacturers, while supporters say it gives consumers clearer guidance similar to the USDA Organic label.
  • The law bars products with chemical additives, synthetic dyes, flavor enhancers or nonnutritive sweeteners.

As the federal government is still trying to determine what it is, California has taken another step in targeting ultraprocessed foods, establishing a first-in-the-nation, state-backed voluntary certification and seal for foods that qualify as non-ultraprocessed.

Under AB 2244, authored by Asm. Jesse Gabriel, D-Encino, the state Department of Public Health will accredit certifiers by June 1, 2029, and the seals will begin appearing on eligible grocery store packaging.

Large grocery chains making more than $10 million in annual sales and that stock at least 25 certified non-UPF product types must prominently display or group these items using special signage or physical separation.

The bill was cosponsored by the Environmental Working Group, whose senior vice president for California, Bernadette Del Chiaro, said it will set “a higher bar for what we consider healthy food and helping consumers make wise choices.

“Consumers deserve labels they can trust,” she said in a statement. “And families deserve a simple way to tell which foods are closer to what comes from a kitchen rather than a factory.”

But food industry experts say the prospect of states taking actions on ultraprocessed foods before a unified definition is formed could cause complications.

Anna Rosales, vice president of science and technology at the Institute of Food Technologists, responded in a blog post that the new law “raises a more complicated question: What are the implications of individual states creating certification programs around UPFs while the federal government is still working to define the term?”

She says the timing “highlights the importance of establishing a consistent, science-based framework as UPF policy continues to evolve.”

Federal definitions proposed

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and USDA just submitted the federal government’s first official proposed definition of UPFs to the Office of Management and Budget for final review. However, the agencies didn’t disclose specific ingredient thresholds or processing standards.

The agencies propose a rule to end the practice where food companies make “Generally Recognized as Safe” (GRAS) determinations for food additives without notifying the Food and Drug Administration. The agencies are taking public comments through Dec. 5 and must review and respond to them before issuing a final rule.

For California to move forward with its own definitions isn’t unprecedented, noted Selina Wang, a professor of cooperative extension at the University of California, Davis.

“I think there were things that were added to Prop 65 that were not on the FDA’s list,” Wang told Agri-Pulse, referring to the state’s voter-approved law that requires businesses to warn people before exposing them to chemicals known to cause cancer, birth defects or reproductive harm.

However, if states begin imposing their own definitions of UPFs, it could create a checkerboard of different rules that manufacturers must navigate, she argued.

“I think what California is trying to do is make it easier for consumers to be aware, especially of how children are exposed to a lot of these ultraprocessed foods and marketing,” Wang said. “I can see that. … But manufacturers are selling these products around the country, so I guess they will have to figure out state by state how they must disclose ingredients. They may have to meet different requirements to sell products into California.”

Under AB 2244, products containing chemical additives, synthetic dyes, flavor enhancers or nonnutritive sweeteners do not qualify for the seal. The bill builds on prior legislation by Gabriel, AB 1264 in 2025, which defined ultraprocessed foods and set strict school nutrition phase-outs.

Food classifications

Some 15 countries define UPFs by using the Nova food classification system created in 2009 by a team of researchers in Brazil, which groups foods into four categories:

  • Unprocessed or minimally processed.
  • Processed culinary ingredients.
  • Processed foods.
  • Ultraprocessed foods.

That last category, ultraprocessed foods, consists of industrial formulations made mostly or entirely from substances extracted from foods or synthesized in a laboratory.

However, California defines food categories based on a hybrid model, which requires both specific industrial ingredients and high saturated fat, added sugar or sodium counts.

“I think the research has shown that ultraprocessed foods are not good for human health,” said Wang, director of the UC Davis Olive Center. “I think to the extent possible, people are more aware of potential negative health outcomes from these foods, which is not a bad thing. At the same time, there are still a lot of things we don’t know, including very clear and practical definitions. There’s still a lot of work that needs to be done.”

For instance, whole wheat bread or yogurt from the store may be processed, but they’re better nutritionally than many other ultraprocessed food options, Wang said.

“There’s a spectrum of good and bad,” she said. “I think there’s a lot of nuance there and we have not figured out how to communicate clearly yet. This is kind of getting ahead of that.”

The UPF bill was among a package of 10 health-related bills signed by the governor, including legislation to create a program to find locally based vendors to provide healthy foods to California public schools.

Like the USDA Organic seal

“While Washington D.C. is paralyzed by inaction, Republicans and Democrats in California are joining forces to empower consumers to avoid harmful ultra-processed foods,” Gabriel, the bill’s author, said in a statement.

“Like the USDA Organic label, this new seal will provide consumers with clear, trustworthy information and make it easier for them to locate healthier foods that are free from harmful additives. Parents shouldn’t need a Ph.D. in chemistry to understand what they’re feeding their kids. California began this movement three years ago by banning the most dangerous food additives, and with Governor Newsom’s continued leadership we are taking another big step forward today.”
