Meet the Guy Actually Protecting America’s Food Supply

As a presidential candidate and later as Secretary of Health and Human Services, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has talked endlessly about the dangers lurking in common foods, whipping up fear and outrage at the way federal regulators do—and for the most part, don’t—police ingredients. Yet it’s another guy, one you’ve probably never heard of, who is actually doing the work of regulating chemicals in our food supply.

Meet Jesse Gabriel, the Democratic Assemblyman from California, who, as I write in a new piece for Mother Jones, has stepped up to serve as America’s shadow FDA chief. Since 2023, he’s passed four laws, one each year, that ban certain dyes and additives from commercial food products and school lunches, establish the nation’s first statutory definition of ultra-processed foods, and introduce a voluntary seal that helps consumers identify less processed foods on grocery store shelves.

How has he done it? By casting the new regulations as common sense, science-based, and bipartisan—all the things that conventional wisdom says are dead in the Trump era. “Jesse Gabriel is determined to make progress, not just drop bills and issue press releases,” Scott Faber, senior vice president for government affairs at Environmental Working Group, told me. In a few short years, “he’s passed more food legislation than the most heralded food legislators have in their whole careers.”

In a world of endless bad news, it’s an encouraging story. It’s also one that ripples far beyond California. Food manufacturers generally don’t reformulate their products for just one state, but for the whole country. And other states often follow California’s legislative lead. Similar food policy bills have since been introduced in 43 states. Fifteen have passed legislation.

With the midterms looming, and Democrats still searching for messages that resonate beyond their base, Gabriel is proving that smart food safety regulations are winning politics.

One of Jesse Gabriel’s food policy victories was a bill defining ultra-processed foods, now signed into California law. Meanwhile, Consumed readers have plenty of their own ideas about how to recognize those foods at the grocery store. Our community thread provoked plenty of discussion, including some well-respected nutritionists. Their frameworks ranged from more emotional definitions (like my “you know it when you see it” concept) to the formal and very specific. Here’s a quick rundown for anyone seeking to filter what they see on grocery store shelves.

Listen to David Kessler!

Former FDA commissioner David Kessler, who made his name fighting Big Tobacco, filed a citizen’s petition last year asking the government to revoke the safety status of “refined carbohydrates used in industrial processing.” These include refined sweeteners (things like corn and glucose syrups and dextrose); refined flour and starches that are “subjected to food extrusion technology” (think cereal puffs and chips); and sucrose, refined flours, or starches paired with unpronounceable additives like emulsifiers and dough conditioners.

The move was tactically brilliant. If no longer considered safe, these ingredients would have to be removed from products, thereby eliminating the lion’s share of products considered ultra-processed.

Robert F Kennedy Jr. told 60 Minutes that he would take action, but … we’re still waiting. In the meantime, Jerold Mande, CEO of Nourish Science and a friend of Consumed’s, suggests that using Kessler’s definition is a (relatively) simple way to identify UPF and limit them in your diet: “It’s only one category of UPF but it’s the one causing the most harm.”

Resist the Evil Twins

Nutritionists, as you may know, are not immune to jargon. In the discussion of ultra-processed foods, two phrases you hear A LOT are “energy dense” and “highly palatable.” Translated into English, they mean “lots of calories” and “irresistible.” Ultra-processed foods put them together in an unholy fashion. Think Pringles (mostly potatoes, but you could eat a whole can), or sugary coffee drinks.

The definition comes from the book Food Intelligence, written by journalist Julia Belluz and former NIH researcher Kevin Hall. (You can read our interview with them here.) That guidance has served reader Becky Daniel, who avoids foods “with high concentrations of salt/fat/sugar not found naturally [and] foods that invite you to overconsume without feeling full.” I like this because it automatically rules out foods like hummus or peanut butter, which are processed and sometimes high calorie but offer plenty of nutrients and fill you up.

Pick the Plain Jane

Another reader, Bertrand, who works for a food app for diabetics, says he thinks of ultra-processed foods as food wearing too much makeup—makeup you can’t wash off. “You take a product (often low-quality) and add color, aroma or taste to make it more desirable and easier to sell,” he explained. “So when you read the label, if you spot colorings (natural or not), flavorings (natural or not) or sweeteners, you can be pretty sure it’s a UPF.” This, I think, is particularly useful in categories like yogurt and bread where some are highly processed and others are not.
