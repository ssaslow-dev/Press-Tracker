Healthcare advocates criticize Newsom’s budget proposal over lack of plan to offset federal cuts

The Department of Finance says the administration is not in a position to backfill federal cuts with looming budget deficit

By Jenny Huh
Published 6:01 PM PST January 14, 2026

SACRAMENTO, Calif. — Healthcare advocates rallied at the California Capitol on Wednesday to criticize Gov. Gavin Newsom’s initial budget proposal, arguing it fails to protect vulnerable residents from looming federal healthcare cuts.

The demonstration follows Newsom’s release of his proposed budget for the next fiscal year, which acknowledges reductions stemming from Congress’ sweeping federal spending package — dubbed the “Big Beautiful Bill” by its supporters — but does not include funding or a back up plan to offset the loss of benefits for individuals.

Advocates say the burden -- attributable to Congress and the so-called Big Beautiful Bill -- has now shifted to state and local leaders.

“We know that you are not responsible for these awful cuts,” said Judy Mark of Disability Voices United. “But now the responsibility does lie in your hands.”

Democratic Assemblymember Mia Bonta of Oakland, chair of the Assembly Health Committee, joined protesters and called for new revenue to preserve healthcare access.

“Generating the revenue that we need is necessary to preserve and restore healthcare access, and we must make that a top priority this year,” Bonta said.

State officials estimate the federal changes could cost California an additional $1.4 billion, including $1.1 billion for Medi-Cal, the state’s Medicaid program that covers millions of low-income residents, seniors and people with disabilities.

“This is federal leadership at its worst,” said Democratic Sen. Smallwood-Cuevas of Los Angeles.

Among those impacted is Amanda Parker, a Medi-Cal recipient who relies on the program to cover medication and round-the-clock care for epilepsy.

“It provides me with my epilepsy drugs and other drugs that support me to keep me alive,” Parker said.

Healthcare advocates say Newsom’s proposal absorbs new administrative costs tied to federal eligibility requirements but does not address how the state will respond if millions lose coverage or see benefits reduced.

“There isn’t really a plan to address potentially the loss of coverage for millions of people and what happens to them,” said Amanda McAllister-Wallner, executive director of Health Access.

“Our message to him is, well then, what’s your solution?” she added, noting that Newsom has strongly opposed the one-time billionaire's tax, which has been proposed to counter the anticipated federal funding cuts, without proposing an alternative of his own.

Jim Mangia, president and CEO of St. John’s Community Health, said the state’s values should be reflected in its spending priorities.

“We have to demand that the values of California be reflected in that budget,” Mangia said.

In response, the Department of Finance said the administration is not in a position to backfill the federal cuts given projected budget deficits in the coming years. 

H.D. Palmer, the spokesperson, also noted that the federal cuts are likely to have local effects as well, as counties have historically been the health care providers of last resort.

We also reached out to the Democratic leaders of both houses, Senate President Pro Tem Monique Limón and Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas.

A spokesperson for Pro Tem Limón wrote back, "The Pro Tem absolutely understands and believes that it is a priority to protect folks’ right to health care and the Senate will do everything it can to protect Californians’ access to health care." The office did note, though, California will not be able to fill the gap in its entirety.

A spokesperson for Assembly Speaker Rivas referred us to the joint statement he provided along Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel. In it, Rivas wrote in part, “California’s budget calls for caution. With Trump targeting our state, we must defend vulnerable communities and protect essential programs that help families put food on the table and see a doctor."

Advocates say they plan to continue pressing lawmakers as budget negotiations unfold ahead of May revisions and the June 15 deadline for a final spending plan.
