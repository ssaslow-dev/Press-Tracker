What’s really in what we eat? California approves the first label for non-ultra-processed foods

Francesca Biagioli

Posted on 10/09/2026

A new label would help U.S. shoppers spot non-ultra-processed foods. Passed unanimously, it now awaits the governor's signature.

Figuring out what’s actually in your food isn’t always straightforward. One glance at the label and you’re staring down a wall of ingredients, unfamiliar names, and numbers that tell you almost nothing about how processed the product really is.

California is trying to fix exactly that. The state legislature has passed AB 2244, a bill that, if signed by Governor Gavin Newsom, would create an official certification program for non-ultra-processed foods.

The goal is a clearly recognizable seal on the packaging, the “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified” mark, letting shoppers spot at a glance which products meet certain criteria.

What makes the approach interesting is that it flags a positive trait directly, rather than just warning shoppers about what to cut back on.

The bill was introduced by a Democratic member of the California State Assembly, and it sailed through with a strikingly lopsided vote: 32-0 in the Senate and 72-0 in the Assembly.

Now it’s on Governor Newsom’s desk. He’ll have to decide whether to sign it into law or veto it.

How the new label would actually work

If the law takes effect, a company won’t be able to simply decide on its own to slap “non ultra-processed” on a package.

The label can only be used after certification by a body accredited by the California Department of Public Health (CDPH).

The law requires the CDPH to accredit these certifying bodies by June 1, 2029. From there, it’s up to them to check products and determine whether they qualify for certification under the law.

The standardized seal can read “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified Food Standard” or the shorthand “Non-UPF Certified”, placed on the front of the package, the information panel, or elsewhere, as long as it doesn’t cover mandatory info like the nutrition facts table.

Which foods qualify

The program isn’t limited to a specific product category: it covers foods and beverages sold at retail, meaning the stuff we normally find on supermarket shelves and in grocery stores. Only products meeting California’s criteria for non-ultra-processed status will get the mark.

Certification isn’t permanent, either: every product has to be recertified at least every three years. Change the recipe, and a new check is needed before the label can keep being used.

One of the more interesting pieces of the bill is transparency. Certifying bodies will have to report product information to the Department of Public Health, including product name, ingredients, nutritional values, manufacturer and brand, identification codes, and certification date.

The department will also need to set up a public webpage listing every food currently certified as “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified.”

Certifying bodies must register with the department and renew their accreditation every year, and the CDPH can review their procedures and paperwork or run audits whenever it wants.

The law also sets penalties for misuse of the label. Slapping the logo on a product that doesn’t meet the requirements, or certifying foods without being an accredited body, would be illegal.

Supermarkets will have to make it visible, too

This isn’t just about packaging. The law also spells out specific obligations for larger grocery chains. Once the standardized label exists, stores with annual revenue above $10 million that carry more than 25 certified products will need to make at least 3 of them clearly identifiable.

They can do this by physically separating the products, using signage, or other visual cues that let shoppers tell them apart from everything else on the shelf.

California already had a definition of ultra-processed

The bill doesn’t come out of nowhere. It builds on a legislative path California had already started on ultra-processed foods. In 2025, Governor Newsom signed AB 1264, the Real Food, Healthy Kids Act, which introduced a statutory definition of ultra-processed foods tied to school nutrition rules and kicked off a process to limit certain products in California schools.

AB 2244 would be the logical next step: not just defining what counts as ultra-processed, but building a visual tool that lets shoppers spot what doesn’t.

Behind the initiative is the growing scientific interest in a possible link between heavy consumption of ultra-processed foods and a range of health problems.

In the statement announcing the bill’s passage, Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel’s office cites research linking high consumption of these foods to cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, certain cancers, metabolic disorders, and other chronic conditions.

That doesn’t mean every processed food is something to avoid. California’s own framing draws a line between “processed” and “ultra-processed.”

It’s precisely this fuzzy boundary that the bill’s backers point to as a reason for an easily recognizable certification system.

If Newsom gives the green light, California could become the first US state with an official certification program for non-ultra-processed foods, a label meant to make that information obvious the moment you’re standing in the aisle.
