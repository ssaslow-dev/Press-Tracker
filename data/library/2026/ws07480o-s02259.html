The tax proposal Bay Area CEOs don’t hate

DIFFERENT TAX VIBES — Large corporations and billionaires in the Bay Area are rallying to kill a wealth-tax proposal, but there’s apparently one tax that the city’s business community would be happy to see enacted on the broader public if it will save the region’s rail and bus systems from a death spiral.

Financial powerbrokers in San Francisco and the region have started pouring money into a campaign to raise sales taxes to spare BART, Muni and other transit systems from severe service cuts. Organizers told Playbook that they have already raised nearly $3 million.

Among the large donors backing the transit-tax proposal … crypto billionaire Chris Larsen, Facebook parent company Meta, biotech giant Genentech and some of the city’s largest real-estate and construction firms.

The measure faces an uphill slog as supporters work to convince Bay Area voters — who could decide a host of other possible tax measures in the same year, including a related parcel tax to support Muni in San Francisco, a proposed CEO tax in San Francisco and a potential parcel tax to help Oakland fix its budget deficit.

Organizers behind the campaign, known as Connect Bay Area, said they have spent two years preparing to sell the tax, including by working with the powerful Bay Area Council to ensure that business interests and Silicon Valley are part of the coalition.

The message to bring corporations and business groups to the table: Without public transit, downtown San Francisco and the tech-dominated region’s economic recovery could unravel.

Without more funding, BART projects that it will have to stop train service at 9 p.m. and eliminate all weekend service, in addition to cutting two lines entirely. Caltrain estimates that it will need to make similar cuts and only offer one weekday train per hour. Muni is looking at making 50 percent service reductions.

“When you give them actual numbers, that makes the difference,” Maggie Muir, a veteran Democratic consultant who’s advising the campaign, said of efforts to recruit business leaders, labor unions and others to support the cause. “When you say a BART train once an hour, why even take it?”

But convincing the wider public across five Bay Area counties will be an even tougher lift. Organizers say the most challenging group of voters will likely be tax-skeptical suburban commuters in places like San Mateo, Santa Clara and Contra Costa counties. But they already have a message in mind: warn them how not acting could create a traffic nightmare on freeways and bridges.

“I’m afraid of a car-magedon,” said San Mateo County Supervisor David Canepa, who supports the measure. “We already have the transit riders. We’re going to need suburban commuters. That’s who’s going to bring us to the finish line.”

ON THE HILL

KIM’S ‘NON-STARTER’ — Young Kim was among the frontline House members who last fall advocated for a one-year extension of expiring Affordable Care Act tax credits. But last week, the California Republican voted against a three-year subsidy extension even as vulnerable Republicans including Rep. David Valadao broke with their party and supported it.

Kim’s vote last week came after Proposition 50 passed in November, drawing her out of her purple district into a solidly Republican one. She’s now contending with fellow GOP Rep. Ken Calvert for one of the few safe seats left behind, and both candidates have been playing up their MAGA bona fides.

“I agree with President Trump that we need to fix this program so we can deliver better healthcare and lower premiums for all Americans,” Kim told Playbook in a statement explaining her vote last week. “I supported a short-term extension to give Americans price certainty and peace of mind as we work toward real reforms. However, Obamacare is not sustainable, and a three-year extension with no meaningful reforms to address runaway prices, waste, and abuse in the program is a non-starter.”

REDISTRICTING WARS

PROP 50 LIVES — California Democrats will likely be able to use their gerrymandered congressional map in this year’s midterm elections after a panel of federal judges Wednesday upheld it, Lindsey Holden and Josh Gerstein report. In a 2-1 ruling, the panel rejected a request to block the map from the California Republican Party and President Donald Trump’s Justice Department, which sued the state after voters in November approved new boundaries, drawn in a bid to create five additional blue seats.
