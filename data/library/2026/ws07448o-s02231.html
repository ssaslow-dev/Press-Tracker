Throw everything at the wall, see what sticks

By LINDSEY HOLDEN and NICOLE NORMAN

DRIVING THE DAY: PROP 50 LIVES ON — California Democrats this year will get the chance to pick up as many as five blue congressional seats after the Supreme Court today turned down a bid to halt the state’s gerrymandered redistricting effort, Lindsey and Josh Gerstein report.

Acting without any indication of dissent, the high court rejected an emergency request from the California Republican Party and the Trump administration for an injunction blocking the Proposition 50 congressional map state voters approved last year.

COLD SNAP: California Democrats are throwing everything at the wall in response to the ongoing federal immigration crackdown.

The flurry of proposals in recent days, spurred by the fatal shootings of Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minnesota, include blocking car rentals for federal agents, yanking tax breaks for companies that contract with the Department of Homeland Security, banning former ICE agents from public-sector jobs in the state and barring local officers from taking side jobs as federal agents.

It’s the most aggressive posture Sacramento lawmakers have taken on immigration during Donald Trump’s second term as they seize on national outrage to push back on the president’s mass-deportation agenda.

“It’s complicated because the federal government controls DHS, they control ICE, they control [Customs and Border Protection],” said Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who plans to introduce a bill that would prohibit ICE from using state resources like buildings and parking lots. “It’s incumbent upon us to think about all of the levers that we have at our disposal, all the tools that we have at our disposal to push back.”

How many of these bills will stick is another story. Any legislation fighting the federal government will almost undoubtedly result in lawsuits and may be tied up in court for years. Case in point: state Sen. Scott Wiener’s bill to ban masks for federal law enforcement, which is still on hold a month after it was supposed to take effect.

State Sen. Sasha Renée Pérez, whose bill to require plain-clothed federal law enforcement to clearly identify themselves is also hung up in court, told Playbook she was willing to push those legal boundaries.

“We are going to have to kind of test the limits of the [state] Constitution, because that is what’s happening right now,” she said.

Some of the ideas address scenarios that are still somewhat murky. For example, lawmakers haven’t presented data on how many cars ICE agents are renting in California, or how many local and state police officers are moonlighting in immigration enforcement.

But the politics of the moment are crystal clear — a striking contrast to this time last year, when lawmakers largely adopted a wait-and-see posture toward the new administration, allocating funds for immigration legal aid but not attacking ICE directly. Democrats back then were coming off a 2024 election that dealt them bruising blows on border protection.

Now, with the unpopular immigration sweeps and large federal agent deployments to blue cities, the pendulum has swung in the other direction.

Here are some of the ways California’s deep-blue Legislature wants to take on ICE:

Federal agents need not apply

A bill introduced by Assembly Speaker Rob Rivas and Assemblymember Mark González would effectively ban anyone who engaged in immigration enforcement during Trump’s second administration from being employed at a state, county or local public agency, or as a law enforcement officer in the state.

Assemblymember Isaac Bryan is carrying a bill that would prevent local law enforcement from taking second jobs with DHS. The lawmaker said in a statement the legislation is needed to “close this loophole in state law that allows for local law enforcement to moonlight, and volunteer, with the Department of Homeland Security.”

A tax disincentive for businesses

Two bills from Assemblymembers Matt Haney and Alex Lee would slap a financial punishment on private businesses that contract with ICE or other immigration-enforcement agencies. Haney’s bill would impose a 50 percent tax on profits made from operating ICE-funded private immigrant detention facilities, legislation that appears to apply only to two companies, CoreCivic and The GEO Group. Lee’s bill would withhold certain state tax benefits from companies that have contracts with the Department of Homeland Security. Lee called the bill a “big leverage push” and hopes it will discourage the private sector from doing business with ICE.

We’ll see you in court

Wiener and state Sen. Aisha Wahab are carrying the “No Kings Act,” which would allow Californians to sue government officials, including federal immigration officers, for alleged violations of their constitutional rights. Wiener said at a news conference last month that recent events have “absolutely cast a spotlight on this bill in terms of why it’s so important.”

State Sen. Susan Rubio’s “Keep Courts Safe from ICE Act” would require California state courts to allow remote appearances. Rubio said in an interview she hopes the bill will ease the minds of those too afraid to show up to court out of fear of being detained by immigration officers.

Creating more roadblocks for ICE

Assemblymember Jessica Caloza wants to prevent ICE from renting cars in the state. Though it is unclear to what extent the agency relies on rentals in their operations, the Los Angeles-area Democrat said in a news conference that “there are countless reports from community members on companies renting vehicles to ICE as they carry out the reckless immigration raids.”

Another bill to be introduced by Gabriel and Assemblymember Juan Carrillo would ban federal immigration officers from using state resources. The idea has been taken up by several cities and counties throughout California after Chicago Mayor Brandon Johnson signed an executive order to create “ICE Free Zones” and keep federal immigration officers from using city-owned property in their operations. Los Angeles County Supervisor Lindsey Horvath pushed for the ban for county properties after federal immigration enforcement took place at Deane Dana Friendship Park and Nature Center in San Pedro.

LABOR VS. AI: Top labor leaders from across the country delivered an ultimatum to Gavin Newsom today: regulate artificial intelligence, or face their wrath in 2028, Tyler Katzenberger and Brock Hrehor report.

“It’s time that the governor listened to us,” Lorena Gonzalez, president of the California Labor Federation, told reporters at the group’s headquarters in downtown Sacramento. “If he doesn’t want to talk to us? Well, when he’s on the campaign trail, he can talk to my colleagues around the nation.”

The news conference marked a broadside on Newsom from a half-dozen leaders of the AFL-CIO, one of the nation’s most powerful labor groups. Gonzalez bashed the California governor for failing to meet labor’s demands and siding with “his Big Tech billionaire friends,” as did AFL-CIO heads from Iowa, traditionally an early nominating state, and from swing states Nevada, North Carolina and Georgia.

The California Labor Fed began unveiling its latest AI agenda this week. Proposed measures include a bill that would require employers laying off workers due to AI to give advance notice and another that would again attempt to require human oversight over algorithms used to make discipline or firing decisions.

HUNTINGTON STRIKES BACK: Huntington Beach will appeal a ban on the city’s voter ID law to the Supreme Court, after California’s top court rejected the city’s petition for review.

The City Council’s unanimous vote on Tuesday to petition the nation’s highest court comes after the California Supreme Court left in place an appeals court ruling striking down the law, infuriating California Republicans and cheering Democrats. The ruling was celebrated as a victory by Attorney General Rob Bonta and Secretary of State Shirley Weber, who said Huntington Beach’s voter ID measure was unlawful.

Huntington Beach’s law represents one of several efforts by Republicans to require voters to show ID at the polls. Shasta County has a measure on the June ballot that would require voters to present ID, and a statewide voter ID ballot measure spearheaded by Assemblymember Carl DeMaio surpassed 1 million signatures in January, according to its backers. — Brock Hrehor

LAYOFFS HIT WAPO: The Washington Post told employees today that it will begin sweeping layoffs, the latest blow to the storied newspaper under billionaire owner Jeff Bezos, confirming weeks of speculation about drastic newsroom cuts, Finya Swai reports.

“We have grappled with financial challenges for some time. They have affected us in multiple rounds of cost cuts and buyouts, along with periodic constraints on other kinds of spending,” The Post’s Executive Editor Matt Murray said in a newsroom note seen by POLITICO, stating that the reductions would impact “nearly all news departments.”

The size and scope of the layoffs are not immediately clear. But hundreds of Post employees could lose their jobs, according to Post reporters, with sections including sports, metro, books and international coverage hit particularly hard.
