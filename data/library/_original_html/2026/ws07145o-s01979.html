Key Findings

The California election has entered its final stretch, with the governor’s race at the top of the ticket and 52 House races that will help determine the party in control of the 120th Congress. The November 3 ballot also includes 14 state propositions—five legislative measures and nine citizens’ initiatives—the most since the November 2016 election. The 2025–26 legislative session ended in late August with a flurry of bills sent to Governor Newsom to sign or veto in September. The recent trends in job growth, consumer prices, and interest rates send mixed signals about the US and California economy.

These are the key findings of the Californians and their Government survey on topics including the November races, legislative ballot measures, and citizens’ initiatives, as well as state and national issues that was conducted September 4–September 10, 2026:

  • Xavier Becerra (D) continues to lead Steve Hilton (R) by a wide margin (60% to 38%) in the governor’s race. Democratic candidates lead Republican candidates in local House district races by a similarly wide margin (62% to 36%). Overwhelming majorities of partisans support their party’s candidates while independents favor the Democratic candidates. Sixty-five percent say that the gubernatorial candidates’ positions on affordability are very important in determining their vote. Fifty-eight percent say that voting is more important this year than in past midterms. After reading the ballot titles and labels, majorities would vote yes on Proposition 1 (55%; Housing Bond) and Proposition 2 (60%; Rainy Day Fund). Three other legislative ballot measures fall short of a majority.
  • Fifty-two percent would vote yes on Proposition 40 (Imposes One-Time Tax) after reading the ballot title and label. Majorities would also vote yes on Proposition 3 (58%; Extending Tax on High Incomes), Proposition 37 (61%; Loan Program for Middle-Income Home Buyers), Proposition 38 (55%; Immunology Bond), Proposition 41 (51%; Prohibits New State Taxes Excluding Revenues from State Spending Limit), and Proposition 42 (54%; Prohibits New State Property Taxes). Partisans vary in their support for these citizens’ initiatives. Three other citizens’ initiatives fall short of a majority. Overwhelming majorities agree that there are too many propositions on the state ballot.
  • Majorities of adults and likely voters think that the state is headed in the wrong direction, and overwhelming majorities expect the state to have bad times financially in the next 12 months. Fifty-one percent of adults and likely voters say that they approve of Governor Gavin Newsom’s job performance, while 45 percent of adults and 48 percent of likely voters approve of the state legislature. Majorities of adults and likely voters report that recent price increases have caused financial hardships for their household. Seventy percent of Californians think that the state is divided into economic groups, the haves and have-nots. Majorities of adults and likely voters say that they can trust the state government in Sacramento to do what is right “only some of the time.”
  • Overwhelming majorities of adults and likely voters think the nation is generally going in the wrong direction and expect the nation to have bad times financially in the next 12 months. Twenty-three percent of adults and 27 percent of likely voters say they approve of President Donald Trump’s job performance, while 17 percent of adults and 16 percent of likely voters approve of the US Congress. Seven in ten Californians are dissatisfied with the availability of affordable health care and believe that the US government is responsible for ensuring that all Americans have access to health care. Fewer than half say the major parties have a lot or some good ideas. Overwhelming majorities say they can trust the federal government to do what is right “only some of the time.”

November Races and Legislative Ballot Measures

The top two vote-getters in the California June primary for statewide elected offices and for federal and state legislative seats are on the November ballot. At the top of the ticket is the governor’s race, with voters deciding who will replace Gavin Newsom, who is termed out of office. There are 52 House races in California that will help determine the party in control of a closely divided US Congress. Voters will also decide on 14 state propositions this fall—five legislative ballot measures and nine citizens’ initiatives—including several tax measures and multibillion-dollar state bonds.

Gubernatorial Election. California likely voters say they would choose Xavier Becerra (D) over Steve Hilton (R) by a wide margin (60% to 38%) if the November 3 election for governor were held today. In our July survey, Becerra led Hilton by 25 points (61% to 36%). Today, partisans overwhelmingly support their party’s gubernatorial candidate: 93 percent of Democrats support Becerra, and 92 percent of Republicans favor Hilton, while independents lean toward the Democratic candidate (57% Becerra, 38% Hilton). Majorities favor Becerra over Hilton across age, education, gender, homeownership, and income groups and in the state’s major regions except for the Central Valley (46% Becerra, 52% Hilton).

Affordability and the cost of living have been the top issue on Californians’ minds in our recent polling. Sixty-five percent of likely voters say that the gubernatorial candidates’ positions on affordability are very important in determining their vote (30% somewhat, 5% not too important). In our February survey, the gubernatorial candidates’ positions on affordability were very important to 61 percent of likely voters and to majorities across parties, regions, and demographic groups. Today, majorities across demographic groups and the state’s major regions hold this view. Notably, partisans are largely in agreement: 69 percent of Democrats, 65 percent of Republicans, and 59 percent of independents say that the gubernatorial candidates’ affordability positions are very important. Becerra supporters (66%) express this opinion as often as Hilton supporters (65%). Seven in ten or more of those who live in the Inland Empire (79%) or Los Angeles (70%), those who have incomes of $40,000 to $99,999 (74%), those who have children 18 or younger in their household (74%), Latinos (71%), renters (70%), and women (70%) say the gubernatorial candidates’ positions on affordability are very important in determining their vote.

Many Californians are watching news about the gubernatorial candidates. About six in ten likely voters say they are following news about candidates for the governor’s election either very (18%) or fairly (40%) closely. By comparison, in our May survey before the June primary, about seven in ten likely voters were very (23%) or fairly closely (48%) following the news about candidates in the governor’s election, including majorities across partisan groups (68% Democrats, 76% Republicans, 65% independents) and demographic groups and regions. Similar shares were closely following the news about candidates in the gubernatorial election in September 2018 (21% very, 41% fairly), while fewer were in September 2022 (17% very, 33% fairly), and overwhelming majorities were in September 2021 (40% very, 41% fairly) prior to the special election on the recall of Governor Newsom. Today, partisans vary in following the news about the gubernatorial candidates at least fairly closely (57% Democrats, 68% Republicans, 50% independents), as do Becerra and Hilton supporters (53% to 69%).

House Races. When asked about their House district race, most likely voters say they would vote for the Democratic candidate over the Republican candidate by a wide margin (62% to 36%). Likely voter preferences were similar in July (63% Democrat, 35% Republican), May (64% Democrat, 35% Republican) and February (62% Democrat, 36% Republican). Today, likely voters’ preferences for the Democratic candidate and the Republican candidate are similar in the governor’s race and the House race. More than nine in ten Democratic and Republican likely voters would choose their party’s House candidate, while independents say they would vote for the Democratic candidate over the Republican candidate (59% to 36%). Majorities say they would vote for the Democratic candidate in their House district races across demographic groups and major state regions with the exception of the Central Valley (47%).

Many Californians believe that their vote matters in the 2026 midterm election. When asked about the importance of voting this year compared to past midterm elections, 58 percent of likely voters say it is more important, 39 percent say it is about as important as in past years, and only 2 percent say voting is less important to them. Californians are more likely to say that voting in the midterm is more important to them this year than they were in September 2022 (43%) and in September 2018 (52%). Today, Democratic likely voters (69%) are much more likely than Republicans (50%) and independents (45%) to say that voting in this midterm is more important this year. Majorities across the state’s regions say that voting in this midterm election is more important this year. Those who say they would vote for Democratic candidates (64%) are more likely than those supporting Republican candidates (49%) in their local House races to say that voting is more important to them this year than in past midterms.

Legislative Ballot Measures. We also gauged support for five state propositions that the state legislature placed on the November 3 ballot. Respondents read the full ballot titles and labels.

  • Proposition 1 (Authorizes Bonds for Housing Affordability Programs). Fifty-five percent of likely voters say they would vote yes on this legislative statute that authorizes $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds for housing affordability programs; 43 percent would vote no. Support for Proposition 1 varies across partisan groups (75% Democrats, 19% Republicans, 56% independents) and between homeowners and renters (49% to 72%). Half or more across the state’s major regions would vote yes on Proposition 1.
  • Proposition 2 (Increases Rainy Day Fund). Sixty percent of likely voters say they would vote yes on this legislative constitutional amendment that increases the state’s rainy day fund, approved by voters in 2014 to provide funding for economic downturns; 39 percent would vote no. Support for Proposition 2 varies across partisan groups (82% Democrats, 22% Republicans, 60% independents). Majorities would vote yes on Proposition 2 across age, education, gender, homeownership, and income groups and the state’s major regions.
  • Proposition 4 (Repeals Prohibition Against Public Funding of Election Campaigns). Forty-two percent of likely voters would vote yes on this legislative statute that repeals a prohibition on state and local governments offering public funding of candidate election campaigns; 53 percent would vote no. Support for Proposition 4 varies across partisan groups (55% Democrats, 19% Republicans, 41% independents). Half or more would vote no across the state’s major regions.
  • Proposition 5 (Changes Recall Election Process for Statewide Officers). Thirty-five percent of likely voters say they would vote yes on this legislative constitutional amendment that fills recall vacancies by subsequent special election or appointment; 59 percent would vote no. Support for Proposition 5 falls short of a majority across partisan groups (40% Democrats, 34% Republicans, 28% independents) and across demographic groups and the state’s major regions.
  • Proposition 43 (Limits Voters’ Ability to Raise Revenues for Local Government Services). Forty-three percent of likely voters say they would vote yes on this legislative constitutional amendment that increases the percentage of voters needed to approve local special taxes from a majority to two-thirds; 51 percent would vote no. Support varies across partisan groups (33% Democrats, 61% Republicans, 43% independents) and state regions (60% Central Valley; 50% Orange/San Diego; 43% Los Angeles; 35% Inland Empire; 32% San Francisco Bay Area).

When we asked likely voters which of the 14 state propositions they are most interested in, 9 percent chose one of the five legislative ballot measures (5% Proposition 1; 1% Proposition 2; 1% Proposition 4; 1% Proposition 5; 1% Proposition 43). In contrast, 56 percent are most interested in one of the three citizens’ initiatives: Proposition 39 (23%; Prohibits Citizens from Voting Unless They Present Government-Issued Identification), Proposition 3 (17%; Extending Existing Tax on High Incomes), and Proposition 40 (16%; Imposes One-Time Tax). Partisans vary sharply in having the most interest in Proposition 39 (7% Democrats, 52% Republicans, 23% independents), Proposition 3 (26% Democrats, 3% Republicans, 14% independents), and Proposition 40 (20% Democrats, 7% Republicans, 19% independents).

Citizens’ Initiatives on the November Ballot

In addition to the five legislative measures, the November ballot includes nine citizens’ initiatives. Most likely voters agree with the statement that there are too many propositions on the state ballot (37% strongly agree, 40% somewhat). Most across demographic, partisan, and regional groups agree at least somewhat. Three in ten or more across partisan, demographic, and regional groups strongly agree with this view, with the exception of 18- to 34-year-olds (16% strongly).

Majorities of likely voters say they would vote yes (58%, 40% no) on Proposition 3 (Extending Tax on High Incomes), which would make existing voter-approved tax rates for individuals earning over $371,000 permanent. Support for Proposition 3 varies across partisan groups. Proposition 3 garners majority support among most demographic and regional groups, but opinions are more divided among likely voters who are white (48% support), age 55 and older (46%), and reside in the Central Valley (48%).

Six in ten likely voters (61%) would vote yes on Proposition 37 (Creates Loan Program for Middle-Income Home-Buyers); about one in three would vote no (35%). If passed, it would authorize $25 billion in bonds to offer eligible California residents fixed-rate mortgages of newly constructed homes below $1.5 million. Solid majorities of Democrats (76%) and independents (62%) would vote yes, compared to fewer Republicans (35%). Majorities across demographic and regional groups would vote yes.

Majorities of likely voters (55%, 42% no) support Proposition 38 (Authorizes Bonds For Immunology Medical Research), which would authorize $8.4 billion in general obligation bonds for immunology and immunotherapy research. Half or more across demographic and regional groups support Proposition 38, with exceptions among likely voters who are white (46%) and those in the Central Valley (44%).

Four in ten likely voters (43%, 55% no) say they would vote yes on Proposition 39 (Prohibits Citizens from Voting Unless They Present Government-Issued Identification), which would invalidate mail ballots that do not have the last four digits of a government-issued identification number written on the envelope and prohibits in-person voting without presenting government-issued identification. Over eight in ten Republicans (85%) are in support, compared to far fewer independents (43%) and Democrats (19%). Support falls short at half or below among most demographic and regional groups, with exceptions among those age 55 and older and residents in the Central Valley and Inland Empire (57% each).

After reading the ballot title and label, 52 percent of California likely voters say they would vote yes on Proposition 40 (Imposes One-Time Tax), which would impose a 5 percent tax on certain taxpayers with assets over $1 billion; 46 percent would vote no. About seven in ten Democrats (72%) would vote yes on this citizens’ initiative, compared to nearly half of independents (46%) and just two in ten Republicans (21%). The share of likely voters in support varies across demographic and regional groups, and support for Proposition 40 declines sharply as age increases.

A slim majority of likely voters would vote yes (51%, 44% no) on Proposition 41 (Prohibits New State Taxes Excluding Revenues from State Spending Limit), which would nullify state taxes enacted after January 1 this year that exempt their revenues from the voter-approved state spending limit and requires audits of programs funded by new special taxes. Three in four Republicans and half of independents (51%) support this proposition, compared to about four in ten Democrats (38%). Support varies across demographic and regional groups.

When it comes to Proposition 42 (Prohibits New State Personal Property Taxes), which would prohibit new state taxes imposed on the ownership of personal property other than real estate, a majority of likely voters would vote yes (54%, 43% no). Partisan views vary, with three in four Republicans (74%) and six in ten independents (61%) saying they would vote yes, compared to four in ten Democrats (39%). Half or more across most demographic and regional groups are in support, but this share falls short of half among likely voters in the San Francisco Bay Area (45%), those earning under $40,000 (43%), renters (41%), and those age 18 to 34 (36%).

Just one in three likely voters in the state say they would vote yes (34%, 61% no) on Proposition 44 (Requires Community Health Clinics Spend 90% of Revenue on Program Services), which would impose penalties on nonprofit community clinics providing primary care to medically underserved areas and populations that spend less than 90 percent of revenue on program services. Roughly one in three across partisan groups would vote yes, and about four in ten or fewer across demographic and regional groups are in support.

When it comes to Proposition 45 (Modifies Environmental Review)— which would amend the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) to expedite environmental review for certain projects by setting deadlines to complete environmental review and resolve lawsuits challenging project approvals— likely voters are divided (49% yes, 46% no). Roughly half or fewer among partisans would vote yes if the election were held today. Views vary across demographic and regional groups, with likely voters who are Latino (55%) and those earning $40,000 to $99,999 (56%) among the most likely to express support.

State of the State

Majorities of California adults (54%) and likely voters (52%) say the state is headed in the wrong direction. This belief is held by nearly all Republicans (94%) and a majority of independents (55%), compared to one in three Democrats (32%). Residents in the Central Valley (62%), the Inland Empire (62%), and Orange/San Diego (56%) are more pessimistic about the direction of the state than those in Los Angeles (48%) and the San Francisco Bay Area (46%).

Californians are much more pessimistic about economic conditions over the next year, with seven in ten adults (71%) and likely voters (73%) as well six in ten or more across parties (61% Democrats, 90% Republicans, 72% independents) expecting bad economic times for the state. About seven in ten Californians have been pessimistic about the economy since June 2025, and majorities have held this view since November 2021. Today, economic pessimism is widespread, with two in three or more across regions and demographic groups expecting bad economic times in the next year.

When asked if California is divided into two economic groups, the haves and have-nots, seven in ten adults (70%) and likely voters (69%) say the state is divided this way. When asked which economic group they are in, 55 percent of adults and 63 percent of likely voters say they are part of the haves.

About six in ten Californians and half of likely voters say recent price increases have caused financial hardship for them and their household. Findings were similar in May and over half of Californians have said price increases have caused hardship each time we’ve asked this question dating back to March 2022. Today, majorities of Democrats (55%), independents (55%), and Republicans (51%) say recent price increases have caused hardship, as do majorities across regions. Hardship declines sharply across income groups, and there are notable differences across racial/ethnic groups, with Latinos (68%) and African Americans (64%) more likely than Asian Americans (46%) and whites (52%) to say recent price increases have caused hardship.

In addition to rising prices, many Californians say the cost of housing places a financial strain on their family, with 24 percent saying a lot of strain and 20 percent saying a little strain. Views were nearly identical last December (25% a lot, 20% a little). The share saying housing costs place financial strain on their family decreases sharply with rising income (67% less than $40,000, 57% $40,000 to $99,999, 32% $100,000 or more). Majorities of African Americans (54%) and Latinos (52%) say housing costs place a strain on their finances, compared to fewer than four in ten Asian Americans (37%) and whites (36%).

Half of adults and likely voters continue to approve of the way Gavin Newsom is handling his job as governor. Approval is shaped by partisan views with most Democrats approving, while independents are divided, and nearly all Republicans disapprove. Residents in Los Angeles and the San Francisco Bay Area are more approving than those elsewhere. Majorities of African Americans, Latinos, and Asian Americans approve, while fewer whites do so.

Views of the California State Legislature are somewhat similar. Forty-five percent of adults and 48 percent of likely voters approve of the job the state legislature is doing. Partisans disagree on legislative approval with two in three Democrats approving, while nine in ten Republicans and half of independents disapprove. Across regions and demographic groups, approval is highest among San Francisco Bay Area residents (55%), college graduates (53%), Asian Americans (52%), and Latinos (51%).

When asked about trust in government, four in ten Californians say they can trust the state government in Sacramento to do what is right just about always (4%) or most of the time (36%), while six in ten say state government can be trusted only some of the time (59%). Views are nearly identical when asked about trust in local government (4% just about always, 37% most of the time, 58% only some of the time).

State of the Nation

Just two months before the consequential 2026 midterm elections, about eight in ten Californians (79% adults, 77% likely voters) think things in the nation are generally going in the wrong direction. Most Democrats (94%) and independents (80%) share this view, while about half of Republicans (52%) think the nation is going in the right direction. Overwhelming majorities across regions and demographic groups say the country is headed in the wrong direction. Californians overall are more likely to hold this pessimistic view than adults nationwide (69%), according to an August Reuters/Ipsos poll. Overwhelming majorities of Californians also expect bad economic times for the country over the next year (76% adults, 75% likely voters).

Meanwhile, about a quarter approve of the way Donald Trump is handling his job as president (23% adults, 27% likely voters). Even fewer approve of the way Congress is handling its job (17% adults, 16% likely voters). This is a record low approval rating of Donald Trump across both his terms. Overwhelming majorities of Democrats (94%) and independents (78%) disapprove of Donald Trump; two in three Republicans (66%) approve—a significant decline from 84 percent in February 2025. Two in three or more Californians across demographic groups and regions disapprove of the president and Congress.

When asked about their level of trust in the federal government in Washington, eight in ten California adults (80%) and likely voters (83%) say they can trust it to do what is right only some of the time. In comparison, far fewer trust the federal government just about always (2% adults, 2% likely voters) or most of the time (16% adults, 13% likely voters). The overall share saying they trust the federal government only some of the time was similar last June (79%). Today, two in three or more across partisan groups, regions, and demographic groups say they trust the federal government to do what is right only some of the time.

Seven in ten Californians say they are very (41% adults, 46% likely voters) or somewhat (29% adults, 26% likely voters) dissatisfied with the availability of affordable health care in the country. Overwhelming majorities of Democrats and independents are dissatisfied, including more than half of Democrats (55%) who say they are very dissatisfied. A majority of Republicans say they are satisfied (10% very, 46% somewhat). About two in three or more across regions and demographic groups say they are dissatisfied.

Seven in ten adults and two in three likely voters also believe that the federal government is responsible for ensuring that all Americans have access to health care, while about three in ten believe health care access is an individual’s own responsibility. Nine in ten Democrats and seven in ten independents say ensuring access to health care is the federal government’s responsibility, while seven in ten Republicans say it is an individual’s responsibility to secure their own access to health care. Residents in the San Francisco Bay Area and Los Angeles are more likely than residents elsewhere in the state to say the federal government has this responsibility. Seven in ten or more African Americans (85%), Latinos (75%), and Asian Americans (71%) say ensuring access to health care is the federal government’s responsibility, compared to a smaller majority of white adults (57%). The share who views this as the federal government’s responsibility declines with rising age and income.

Four in ten Californians say the Democratic Party has a lot (11%) or some (29%) good ideas, while an additional three in ten (28%) say it has a few good ideas. About a quarter (23%) say it has almost no good ideas, and one in ten are unsure (9%). About two in three Democrats say their party has a lot or some good ideas, while two in three Republicans (66%) say the Democratic Party has almost no good ideas. African Americans and Latinos are more likely than Asian Americans and whites to say the Democratic Party has at least some good ideas.

About a quarter of California adults say the Republican Party has a lot (9%) or some (17%) good ideas, while an additional two in ten (22%) say it has a few good ideas. More than four in ten say it has almost no good ideas (44%), and one in ten are unsure (10%). Seven in ten Republicans say their party has at least some good ideas. Seven in ten Democrats and four in ten independents say the Republican Party has almost no good ideas. Across regions, residents in the Central Valley are the most likely to say the Republican Party has at least some good ideas. A majority of San Francisco Bay Area (53%) residents and nearly half of Los Angeles (48%) residents say it has almost no good ideas.

When asked about politicians who identify as democratic socialists, about three in ten Californians say they have a lot (12%) or some (19%) good ideas, while two in ten say they have a few good ideas (20%) and three in ten (31%) say they have almost no good ideas. However, nearly two in ten (17%) are unsure. Notably, a majority of Democrats say these politicians have at least some good ideas, compared to three in ten independent and 5 percent of Republicans. Seven in ten Republicans say these politicians have almost no good ideas.

San Francisco Bay Area residents are the most likely to say democratic socialist politicians have at least some good ideas while Inland Empire residents are the least likely to say so. Young adults age 18 to 34 (38%) are more likely than adults 55 and over (28%) to think these politicians have good ideas. College graduates (38%) are more likely than residents who have no college experience (25%) to say this (33% some college). According to a July Reuters/Ipsos poll, Californians’ views of the ideas of the Democratic Party and Democratic socialist politicians are similar to the views of Americans overall, while Californians’ views of the Republican Party’s ideas are much more negative.

Methodology

The PPIC Statewide Survey has been conducted since April 1998 to provide a voice for the public, inform policymakers, encourage discussion, and raise awareness of critical issues facing California. The coauthors of this report include survey director Mark Baldassare, who holds the Miller Chair in Public Policy; associate survey director and research fellow Dean Bonner, survey analyst Lauren Mora; and survey analyst Deja Thomas, who was the project manager for this survey. The Californians and Their Government survey on state, local, and national issues is supported with funding from the Arjay R. and Frances F. Miller Foundation. The PPIC Statewide Survey invites input, comments, and suggestions from policy and public opinion experts and from its own advisory committee members, but survey methods, questions, and content are determined solely by PPIC’s survey team. We thank Paul Lewis and Eric McGhee for helpful reviews of an earlier report draft.

Findings in this report are based on a survey of 1,745 California adult residents. The median time to complete the survey was 14.2 minutes. The survey was conducted from September 4–10, 2026.

The survey was conducted by Ipsos, using its online KnowledgePanel, in English and Spanish according to respondents’ preferences. KnowledgePanel members are recruited through probability-based sampling and include both those with internet access and those without. KnowledgePanel provides internet access for those who do not have it and, if needed, a device to access the internet when they join the panel. KnowledgePanel members are primarily recruited using address-based sampling (ABS) methodology, which improves population coverage, particularly for hard-to-reach populations such as young adults and minority groups. ABS-recruited Latinos are supplemented with a dual-frame random digit dialing (RDD) sampling methodology that targets telephone exchanges associated with areas with a higher concentration of Latinos to provide the capability to conduct representative online surveys with Latinos, including those who speak only Spanish. KnowledgePanel’s recruitment was originally based on a national RDD frame and switched to the primarily ABS-based methodology in 2009. KnowledgePanel includes households with landlines and cell phones, including those with cell phones only and those without phones. ABS allows probability-based sampling of addresses from the US Postal Service’s Delivery Sequence File (DSF). The DSF-based sampling frame used for address selection is enhanced with a series of refinements—such as the appendage of various ancillary data to each address from commercial and government data sources—to facilitate complex stratification plans. Taking advantage of such refinements, quarterly samples are selected using a stratified sampling methodology that aims to retain the representativeness of the panel. KnowledgePanel recruits new panel members throughout the year to offset panel attrition.

To qualify for the survey, a panel member must be age 18 or older and reside in California. A general population sample of Californians was selected using Ipsos’s PPS (probability proportional to size) sampling procedure to select study-specific samples. Briefly, to select such samples, the panel is first weighted to population benchmarks and those panel weights are used as the measure of size for a PPS sample selection that yields a fully representative sample. A total of 1,811 respondents completed the survey out of 3,046 panelists who were sampled, for a response rate of 59 percent. To ensure the highest data quality, we flagged respondents who sped through the survey, which we defined as completing the survey in one-fourth of the overall median time (less than 4 minutes). We also flagged respondents if their self-reported age or gender did not match the data stored in their profile. A total of 66 cases were removed after this review process, resulting in 1,745 total qualified and valid cases.

Accent on Languages, Inc., translated new survey questions into Spanish, with assistance from Renatta DeFever.

We use the US Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey’s (ACS) Public Use Microdata Series for California (with regional coding information from the University of Minnesota’s Integrated Public Use Microdata Series for California) to compare certain demographic characteristics of the survey sample—region, age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, and income—with the characteristics of California’s adult population. The survey sample was closely comparable to the ACS figures. We also used voter registration data from the California Secretary of State to compare the party registration of registered voters in our sample to party registration statewide. The sample of Californians is first weighted using an initial sampling or base weight that corrects for any differences in the probability of selecting various segments of the KnowledgePanel sample. This base weight is further adjusted using an iterative proportional fitting (raking) procedure that aligns sample demographics to population benchmarks from the 2024 ACS data as well as party registration benchmarks from the California Secretary of State’s voter registration file.

The sampling error, taking design effects from weighting into consideration, is ±3.0 percent at the 95 percent confidence level for the total unweighted sample of 1,745 adults. This means that 95 times out of 100, the results will be within 3.0 percentage points of what they would be if all adults in California were interviewed. The sampling error for unweighted subgroups is larger: for the 1,475 registered voters, the sampling error is ±3.3 percent; for the 1,103 likely voters, it is ±3.8 percent. Sampling error is only one type of error to which surveys are subject. Results may also be affected by factors such as question wording, question order, and survey timing.

We present results for five geographic regions, accounting for approximately 90 percent of the state population. “Central Valley” includes Butte, Colusa, El Dorado, Fresno, Glenn, Kern, Kings, Madera, Merced, Placer, Sacramento, San Joaquin, Shasta, Stanislaus, Sutter, Tehama, Tulare, Yolo, and Yuba Counties. “San Francisco Bay Area” includes Alameda, Contra Costa, Marin, Napa, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Solano, and Sonoma Counties. “Los Angeles” refers to Los Angeles County, “Inland Empire” refers to Riverside and San Bernardino Counties, and “Orange/San Diego” refers to Orange and San Diego Counties. Residents of other geographic areas are included in the results reported for all adults, registered voters, and likely voters, but sample sizes for these less-populous areas are not large enough to report separately.

We present results for non-Hispanic whites, who account for 35 percent of the state’s adult population, and also for Latinos, who account for 38 percent of the state’s adult population and constitute one of the fastest-growing voter groups. We also present results for non-Hispanic Asian Americans, who make up about 17 percent of the state’s adult population, and non-Hispanic African Americans, who comprise about 5 percent. Results for other racial/ethnic groups—such as Native Americans—are included in the results reported for all adults, registered voters, and likely voters, but sample sizes are not large enough for separate analysis. Results for African American and Asian American likely voters are combined with those of other racial/ethnic groups because sample sizes for African American and Asian American likely voters are too small for separate analysis. We compare the opinions of those who report they are registered Democrats, registered Republicans, and decline-to-state or independent voters; the results for those who say they are registered to vote in other parties are not large enough for separate analysis. We also analyze the responses of likely voters—so designated per their responses to survey questions about voter registration, previous election participation, intentions to vote this year, attention to election news, and current interest in politics.

Methodology

The PPIC Statewide Survey has been conducted since April 1998 to provide a voice for the public, inform policymakers, encourage discussion, and raise awareness of critical issues facing California. The coauthors of this report include survey director Mark Baldassare, who holds the Miller Chair in Public Policy; associate survey director and research fellow Dean Bonner, survey analyst Lauren Mora; and survey analyst Deja Thomas, who was the project manager for this survey. The Californians and Their Government survey on state, local, and national issues is supported with funding from the Arjay R. and Frances F. Miller Foundation. The PPIC Statewide Survey invites input, comments, and suggestions from policy and public opinion experts and from its own advisory committee members, but survey methods, questions, and content are determined solely by PPIC’s survey team. We thank Paul Lewis and Eric McGhee for helpful reviews of an earlier report draft.

Findings in this report are based on a survey of 1,745 California adult residents. The median time to complete the survey was 14.2 minutes. The survey was conducted from September 4–10, 2026.

The survey was conducted by Ipsos, using its online KnowledgePanel, in English and Spanish according to respondents’ preferences. KnowledgePanel members are recruited through probability-based sampling and include both those with internet access and those without. KnowledgePanel provides internet access for those who do not have it and, if needed, a device to access the internet when they join the panel. KnowledgePanel members are primarily recruited using address-based sampling (ABS) methodology, which improves population coverage, particularly for hard-to-reach populations such as young adults and minority groups. ABS-recruited Latinos are supplemented with a dual-frame random digit dialing (RDD) sampling methodology that targets telephone exchanges associated with areas with a higher concentration of Latinos to provide the capability to conduct representative online surveys with Latinos, including those who speak only Spanish. KnowledgePanel’s recruitment was originally based on a national RDD frame and switched to the primarily ABS-based methodology in 2009. KnowledgePanel includes households with landlines and cell phones, including those with cell phones only and those without phones. ABS allows probability-based sampling of addresses from the US Postal Service’s Delivery Sequence File (DSF). The DSF-based sampling frame used for address selection is enhanced with a series of refinements—such as the appendage of various ancillary data to each address from commercial and government data sources—to facilitate complex stratification plans. Taking advantage of such refinements, quarterly samples are selected using a stratified sampling methodology that aims to retain the representativeness of the panel. KnowledgePanel recruits new panel members throughout the year to offset panel attrition.

To qualify for the survey, a panel member must be age 18 or older and reside in California. A general population sample of Californians was selected using Ipsos’s PPS (probability proportional to size) sampling procedure to select study-specific samples. Briefly, to select such samples, the panel is first weighted to population benchmarks and those panel weights are used as the measure of size for a PPS sample selection that yields a fully representative sample. A total of 1,811 respondents completed the survey out of 3,046 panelists who were sampled, for a response rate of 59 percent. To ensure the highest data quality, we flagged respondents who sped through the survey, which we defined as completing the survey in one-fourth of the overall median time (less than 4 minutes). We also flagged respondents if their self-reported age or gender did not match the data stored in their profile. A total of 66 cases were removed after this review process, resulting in 1,745 total qualified and valid cases.

Accent on Languages, Inc., translated new survey questions into Spanish, with assistance from Renatta DeFever.

We use the US Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey’s (ACS) Public Use Microdata Series for California (with regional coding information from the University of Minnesota’s Integrated Public Use Microdata Series for California) to compare certain demographic characteristics of the survey sample—region, age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, and income—with the characteristics of California’s adult population. The survey sample was closely comparable to the ACS figures. We also used voter registration data from the California Secretary of State to compare the party registration of registered voters in our sample to party registration statewide. The sample of Californians is first weighted using an initial sampling or base weight that corrects for any differences in the probability of selecting various segments of the KnowledgePanel sample. This base weight is further adjusted using an iterative proportional fitting (raking) procedure that aligns sample demographics to population benchmarks from the 2024 ACS data as well as party registration benchmarks from the California Secretary of State’s voter registration file.

The sampling error, taking design effects from weighting into consideration, is ±3.0 percent at the 95 percent confidence level for the total unweighted sample of 1,745 adults. This means that 95 times out of 100, the results will be within 3.0 percentage points of what they would be if all adults in California were interviewed. The sampling error for unweighted subgroups is larger: for the 1,475 registered voters, the sampling error is ±3.3 percent; for the 1,103 likely voters, it is ±3.8 percent. Sampling error is only one type of error to which surveys are subject. Results may also be affected by factors such as question wording, question order, and survey timing.

We present results for five geographic regions, accounting for approximately 90 percent of the state population. “Central Valley” includes Butte, Colusa, El Dorado, Fresno, Glenn, Kern, Kings, Madera, Merced, Placer, Sacramento, San Joaquin, Shasta, Stanislaus, Sutter, Tehama, Tulare, Yolo, and Yuba Counties. “San Francisco Bay Area” includes Alameda, Contra Costa, Marin, Napa, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Solano, and Sonoma Counties. “Los Angeles” refers to Los Angeles County, “Inland Empire” refers to Riverside and San Bernardino Counties, and “Orange/San Diego” refers to Orange and San Diego Counties. Residents of other geographic areas are included in the results reported for all adults, registered voters, and likely voters, but sample sizes for these less-populous areas are not large enough to report separately.

We present results for non-Hispanic whites, who account for 35 percent of the state’s adult population, and also for Latinos, who account for 38 percent of the state’s adult population and constitute one of the fastest-growing voter groups. We also present results for non-Hispanic Asian Americans, who make up about 17 percent of the state’s adult population, and non-Hispanic African Americans, who comprise about 5 percent. Results for other racial/ethnic groups—such as Native Americans—are included in the results reported for all adults, registered voters, and likely voters, but sample sizes are not large enough for separate analysis. Results for African American and Asian American likely voters are combined with those of other racial/ethnic groups because sample sizes for African American and Asian American likely voters are too small for separate analysis. We compare the opinions of those who report they are registered Democrats, registered Republicans, and decline-to-state or independent voters; the results for those who say they are registered to vote in other parties are not large enough for separate analysis. We also analyze the responses of likely voters—so designated per their responses to survey questions about voter registration, previous election participation, intentions to vote this year, attention to election news, and current interest in politics.
