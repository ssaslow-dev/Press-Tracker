California is one step closer to becoming the first state in the nation to define Jewishness as not only a religion but also an ethnicity.

The bill behind that push, SB 1387, would add “Jewish” to the list of ethnicities on government forms. It cleared a major legislative hurdle this week along with five other bills that Jewish California, an advocacy coalition of mainstream Jewish groups, says can solve significant problems facing the Jewish community.

The bills advanced out of the Senate or Assembly appropriations committees on Aug. 13 and now head to final floor votes before the Legislature’s Aug. 31 deadline. Gov. Gavin Newsom then has until Sept. 30 to decide their fate.

Appropriations committee votes are widely considered the toughest test a bill faces short of the governor’s desk. In a marathon session on Aug. 13, the committees, which screen every bill expected to cost the state significant money, worked through more than 1,000 bills at once, killing many without any public debate.

“We’re incredibly grateful that the legislature has said year over year that we stand with the Jewish community and has largely continued to push the most important bills to our community forward,” Jewish California CEO David Bocarsly said. “It’s a big step, but it’s by no means the end.”

SB 1387, authored by Sen. Henry Stern (D-Sherman Oaks) would let people voluntarily identify as ethnically Jewish on state forms, such as those for unemployment claims or hate-crime reporting. Supporters say SB 1387 would help the state better track antisemitism, target public health outreach and understand the size of California’s Jewish population. However, the bill has sparked debate, including over concerns about how sensitive identity data is stored by the state. The bill passed the Assembly Appropriations Committee 11-0 on Aug. 13 after previously clearing the full Senate 33-0.

The other five bills address areas such as security, religious accommodation and voter guides.

Two come from Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino). One is AB 1836, which would put $100 million into the State Nonprofit Security Grant Program and also let nonprofits use that money to protect off-site events — Pride marches or religious festivals, for example — not just ones in their own buildings. The other bill, AB 395, would ask schools and public agencies to try not to schedule graduations or major meetings on religious holidays.

The “Safe Worship Zone Act,” or AB 2664, from Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan (D-Orinda) would create a 100-foot buffer zone around the entrances of houses of worship, so congregants cannot be confronted or blocked on their way in or out, while preserving the right to protest nearby. The bill is backed by an interfaith coalition.

AB 1763, from Assemblymember Alex Lee (D-San Jose) would guarantee K-12 students an excused absence for religious holidays without requiring a principal’s approval.

And a measure, AB 1853, from Assemblymembers Gail Pellerin (D-Santa Cruz) and Marc Berman (D-Menlo Park) would restrict candidate statements in official voter guides, barring hateful or discriminatory content and giving election officials authority to reject noncompliant submissions. That bill followed an incident earlier this year in which a voter guide containing a candidate statement filled with antisemitic conspiracy theories was mailed to 23 million California households.
