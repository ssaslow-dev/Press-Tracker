Senate Hopeful Brian Goldsmith Pitches a ‘Fresh Perspective' for District 24

By Arya Rahimian,

2026-04-01

LOS ANGELES - Brian Goldsmith, a Los Angeles native, former journalist, Democratic consultant, and first-time candidate, is running to represent California's 24th State Senate District, a sprawling, diverse seat stretching from Agoura Hills and Calabasas through Beverly Hills, West Hollywood and the Pacific Palisades down to the South Bay beach cities.

The seat is being vacated by term-limited Sen. Ben Allen, and Goldsmith has emerged as the race's leading fundraiser, raising more than $1 million and securing endorsements from figures such as former Speaker Nancy Pelosi, former Secretary of State Hillary Clinton, and businessman Rick Caruso.

A graduate of Harvard College and Stanford Law School who spent more than a decade as a political commentator at CBS News, Yahoo News, and CNN, Goldsmith says the January 2025 wildfires and what he describes as California's pattern of "good intentions and bad governance," compelled him to move from advising candidates to becoming one. The Westside Current sat down with Goldsmith to discuss his platform and vision for the district.

At the top of the interview, Goldsmith emphasized that the issues most people talk about in their daily lives are state and local matters, not Washington, D.C., even though the press is far more focused on national politics. "You gotta look at these contests, these offices, because they actually have a profound effect on your daily life," he said.

Goldsmith described his path to candidacy as a gradual evolution. After covering politics at outlets including The Atlantic, Yahoo, and CBS News, where he served as Katie Couric's political producer, he left journalism during the first Trump administration to focus on advocacy and helped his friend Pete Buttigieg's presidential campaign. He also launched a startup, giving him firsthand experience with the regulatory and financial burdens facing California entrepreneurs.

But he said two developments pushed him from advising to running. The first was the 2024 election, including not just the outcome but the way California became a central part of the case against Vice President Kamala Harris. "As much as I disagreed with a lot of people making that argument, we have to be honest enough to admit that the state has failed in a number of areas," Goldsmith said, citing the cost of living, challenges facing small businesses and what he described as years of fraud and mismanagement in state government.

The second catalyst was the January 2025 Palisades fire, which devastated communities within the district. Goldsmith grew up on the Westside, attending the Leo Baeck Temple school and then Harvard-Westlake, and said the fires hit close to home. Though he was not personally displaced, he and his wife took in a family of four who lost their home, and he said many friends and neighbors are still struggling to rebuild.

"We now know that the Palisades fire was entirely preventable, and that it was mismanaged at every stage - before, during, and after - at all levels of government," Goldsmith said. "I think we need more people in office who can take a fresh perspective, who can disagree with the people in power when they're doing something wrong."

The Toll of the Fires

Goldsmith pushed back against the perception that the fire's impact was limited to the wealthy. He pointed to the trailer park in Malibu, to families who invested everything they had into homes that happened to sit on expensive land, and to the broad economic effects on domestic workers, landscapers and small businesses that depended on residents who are no longer there.

"It's a multidimensional failure, and the cast of victims is not just the stereotype that has been perpetuated," he said. He noted that the absence of federal aid has compounded the pain, arguing that if the same disaster had occurred in another state, federal funds would have arrived far more quickly. Many fire survivors, he said, were "house rich" but not cash rich, and now face the challenge of fighting insurance companies, draining savings and confronting serious doubts about the safety of returning to their lots.

Housing

On housing, Goldsmith said he would evaluate each piece of legislation on its merits and does not support a one-size-fits-all approach to density. He opposed SB 79, calling it "far too blunt of an instrument" that punished communities that had already done the hard work of local planning to meet their housing goals.

Instead, he outlined a multi-pronged strategy: leveraging publicly owned land for below-market-rate housing development, converting empty commercial buildings to residential use, which he said is already being proven in Manhattan, and speeding up the state's permitting and regulatory processes.

"There's no reason that the cost of building a unit of housing in California should cost twice what it does in Colorado," Goldsmith said. "That's not the land. That's the cost of the house. A lot of that is embedded regulatory and permitting costs."

He drew on his journalism background to make the case for deadlines. "As a journalist, one of the most powerful tools we had was, you had a deadline," he said. "I think too much of construction is just open-ended."

Goldsmith also advocated expanding manufactured and modular housing, which he said has improved dramatically in quality while offering significant cost and speed advantages. "We don't do anything else the way we did 40 or 50 years ago. Why are we still building all houses the way we did 40 or 50 years ago?" he said.

He noted that a colleague elected to the Santa Barbara County Board of Supervisors ordered an audit of publicly owned land that had been deemed environmentally sensitive, only to find that roughly half of it was not and included underutilized parking lots, office buildings and undeveloped industrial zones with existing infrastructure that could support housing.

Homelessness

On homelessness, Goldsmith did not mince words. "In the richest state, in the richest country, that we have this many people who are unhoused - there is nothing progressive about allowing anyone to sleep on the street even for one night," he said. He called the situation a "scandal" and cited LAUSD's estimate that thousands of its students are functionally homeless and noted that four Angelenos die on the streets every day.

Goldsmith said the state should stop managing the crisis and start solving it, with the goal of ending the era of encampments. He called for rapidly standing up dignified, clean shelter, rather than $800,000-per-unit permanent housing, and deploying proven drug treatment and mental health services through effective nonprofits. He highlighted organizations such as the Downtown Women's Center and Hope the Mission as models of accountability.

"Some of them won't take public money because it comes with too many strings attached," Goldsmith said. "If they're doing great work on the ground and they won't take Sacramento's money, it's not the groups that should have to change. It's Sacramento that should have to change."

He said that once sufficient shelter capacity is in place, anti-camping ordinances should be enforced, giving people multiple chances to accept services before being placed into programs. For those who are unable to make decisions for themselves, Goldsmith said the Care Courts established by Proposition 1 are a step in the right direction, but that the pathway into involuntary treatment is "far too restricted and narrow."

"Sometimes people present a false choice between involuntary treatment and voluntary treatment," he said. "In many of these cases, it's a choice between involuntary treatment and folks literally dying on the streets."

He pointed to the work done in San Francisco under Mayor Daniel Lurie and in San Jose under Mayor Matt Mahan as models that the state should replicate.

Public Safety and Proposition 36

Goldsmith said he supported Proposition 36 at the time of the vote, not retroactively, and that he took criticism from fellow Democrats for doing so, just as he did for supporting Nathan Hochman for Los Angeles County District Attorney. He described both as answers that sought a middle ground between accountability for repeat offenders and pathways to rehabilitation for first-time, nonviolent offenders.

"Prop 36 was about striking the right balance between holding folks more accountable and providing the care and the services that people need and also providing for the small businesses that were the target of repeated smash and grabs, seemingly without consequence," Goldsmith said. He added that the challenge now is for the state to fund the measure's full implementation.

Entertainment Industry

Goldsmith called the entertainment industry a critical economic engine of the district and emphasized that it supports a broad ecosystem of middle-class jobs and small businesses, not only movie stars. He praised the bill authored by Sen. Ben Allen and Assemblymember Rick Zbur that dramatically expanded California's film and television tax credit.

"The film and TV tax credit that passed was a floor, not a ceiling," Goldsmith said. "I think we ought to be doing more."

He called for expanding the credit both above and below the line to remain competitive with other states and countries that are attracting California's productions and jobs. He also stressed the importance of returning to legislation after it passes to evaluate what is working and what isn't, a practice he said Speaker Robert Rivas has started through outcomes reviews.

"In California, I think too much of our focus has been on how many billions of dollars are we spending, what are our good intentions - and not as much on outcomes and results," Goldsmith said.

Jewish Identity and AB 715

Goldsmith spoke openly about his Jewish identity and its role in his candidacy. His wife serves as vice president of Wilshire Boulevard Temple, and his son attends a Jewish day school. He said the 24th District likely has the largest concentration of Jewish voters outside New York, and that the community is facing record levels of antisemitism.

"You can disagree with Prime Minister Netanyahu's policies in Israel - that's totally legitimate. Half of Israelis disagree with his policies," Goldsmith said. "That's not what we're talking about. We're talking about people being targeted in the United States based on who they are. And that is wrong."

He said he was a strong supporter of AB 715, the antisemitism prevention legislation, and that he personally lobbied members of the state Legislature on its behalf. He drew a contrast with some of his opponents, saying he is "not ducking that issue" and is not "saying different things to different groups."

Goldsmith pointed to his early work with Democratic Majority for Israel, which he described as focused on defeating antisemitism in the United States, and said his advocacy is one reason he has been endorsed by the Legislative Jewish Caucus and leaders such as former Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel and Julie Platt, the second woman in history to chair the Jewish Federations of North America. Former Sen. Barbara Boxer serves as the honorary chair of his campaign. He also noted the support of non-Jewish allies such as Rick Caruso.

Personal Life and Closing

Goldsmith, who lives in the Beverly Hills–West Hollywood area with his wife and two children, said family is the center of his life. When he is not on the campaign trail, he tries to put the phone down and focus on his kids. The family frequents farmers markets around the district, enjoys cooking, and building Legos with his son.

Goldsmith said he is the first in his family to run for office, though public service runs in the family. His father led an economy and jobs committee under Mayor Antonio Villaraigosa, and his mother has spent three decades working on behalf of foster children through the Alliance for Children's Rights.

He summarized his goals in four pillars: making it easier and more affordable to live in California, including for small businesses and families; taking on quality-of-life concerns around crime and homelessness with proven strategies; spending less time on performative politics and more time addressing the big challenges of the future, from disruptive technologies to climate resilience; and building coalitions in the state Legislature to pass bills and deliver results.

"I've heard different politicians get cheap applause by saying, ‘I'm not going up there to make friends, I'm going up there to make a difference,'" Goldsmith said. "Well, you're a legislator. You have to be able to build relationships and find common ground in order to pass bills and make a difference. And that's what I would set out to do."

The primary election for California's 24th Senate District will take place on June 2, 2026.
