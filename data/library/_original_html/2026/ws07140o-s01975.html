Newsom’s deference and an AI proxy war

DRIVING THE DAY — Gavin Newsom is giving Kamala Harris the right of first refusal in the 2028 presidential race, telling CNN’s Jake Tapper that he would not run if Harris launched another bid for the White House. As Melanie writes, they were Newsom’s most definitive comments yet about a potential collision of ambitions with his fellow Californian.

Newsom cast his thinking in politically strategic, not sentimental, terms, suggesting it would benefit every other primary candidate if they did compete. “First of all, electorally, it’s a gift from God for everybody else. They can enjoy the hell out of it. Mutually assured destruction. It services no greater good,” Newsom said.

RACE AGAINST ROBOTS — The race to succeed Nancy Pelosi is suddenly turning into a proxy fight over the Democratic Party’s approach to regulating AI amid escalating warnings about the dire risks of advanced models.

Scott Wiener, a prominent state senator and the front-runner in the race, has been one of the most prolific authors of AI safety regulations in the country. But Pelosi’s preferred successor, Supervisor Connie Chan, is now scrambling to outflank him on the issue, arguing his proposals do not go far enough and repeatedly hitting Wiener for Anthropic’s $500,000 contribution to an outside PAC supporting him.

“Scott Wiener has been saying that he’s been writing AI legislation. But we know who’s writing it with him, and it’s people like AI companies, Anthropic,” Chan told Playbook. “The effective guardrail is actually preventing the industry to develop any further, and so that they (cannot) create damage that we cannot recover from.”

Chan hasn’t gone as far as endorsing a national pause on advanced AI models or a ban on superintelligence, as some progressives like Sen. Bernie Sanders propose. But she wants to require that labor unions — who she’s closely aligned with — be involved in AI deployment decisions, and she’s called for an “immediate moratorium” on all data center projects across the country.

Wiener has taken a more measured approach, calling on AI companies to slow development until safety guardrails are enacted. He and his supporters say Chan’s positions are vague and unrealistic, arguing that Congress can make AI safer without killing innovation in a sector that has fueled San Francisco and Silicon Valley’s economic recovery.

“That is not actual constructive policy, that is political grandstanding,” Wiener told Playbook, referring to Chan’s more hardline approach. “She has all sorts of hot takes now. Some of us have actually been working on this issue for years.”

Wiener’s policy platform also calls for a host of accountability measures — including the creation of a new federal AI regulatory agency, minimum safety standards for advanced models, whistleblower protections and “steep fines” for companies that don’t comply — though he doesn’t call for an outright pause on AI development or bans on data centers. Instead, Wiener wants to require data center developers to show their projects won’t harm water supplies or raise electricity bills.

The contrast between Wiener and Chan comes as polling suggests that voters across the country are increasingly skeptical of AI and data center development — and amid high-profile calls from AI leaders to slow down. The race in San Francisco, home to much of the AI sector, will test how much anti-AI sentiment resonates with an electorate on the front line of the technology’s development.

“Wiener’s positions resonate with voters here because they’re more up to speed and they’re more knowledgeable about the benefits, as well as the dangers,” said Sam Singer, a veteran San Francisco consultant who works for a PAC that supports Wiener.

Tensions over AI have been heightened as Pelosi campaigns hard for Chan and hosts multiple Washington fundraisers on her behalf. Pelosi was a vocal opponent of SB 1047, Wiener’s 2024 proposal that aimed to set a first-in-the-nation standard for AI safety regulations, including a “kill switch” requirement for large models that could behave dangerously.

Pelosi, at the time, sided with prominent venture capitalists, including Ron Conway and Marc Andreessen, in opposing the bill. Newsom vetoed it, and Wiener’s allies argued Pelosi’s opposition was an effort to undercut him amid rumblings that he was preparing to run for her seat.

“While Washington sat on its hands, Scott began fighting the AI corporations to adopt strong safety regulations,” Joe Arellano, a spokesperson for Wiener, told Playbook.

Last year, Wiener succeeded in passing SB 53, a scaled back AI-safety law that requires major companies to reveal their safety protocols. But Wiener has recently invoked SB 1047 to criticize Pelosi, arguing that the U.S. would be more prepared for escalating concerns about AI dangers had the bill become law.

Wiener’s criticism came after Pelosi and a host of her allies in Congress, including Rep. Zoe Lofgren, released a letter chiding him for launching an AI-powered chatbot to mimic Chan. (Pelosi called the chatbot offensive, and Wiener eventually took it down).

Chan has in recent days dismissed Wiener’s criticism of her chief supporter, saying, “I’m not Nancy Pelosi.” Chan questions SB 1047’s significance, arguing that her proposals would go further by not allowing dangerous AI models to be developed in the first place. She also argues that SB 1047 wouldn’t have included some potentially dangerous models because its regulations would only have applied to larger models.

“Our approach to AI, clearly, is very people-centered,” Chan said. “We’re going to bring unions to the table. It means that we want human oversights.”

GOVERNOR'S RACE — FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: OPENING ARGUMENT — Xavier Becerra is releasing his first ads of the general election, with a heavy emphasis on his biography and an obligatory jab at Trump. The statewide digital and television ads, part of a seven-figure ad buy, have English- and Spanish-language versions with a similar look but subtle differences.

The English-language ad, “100% Californian,” emphasizes his father’s roots as a union worker and ticks through Becerra’s biography as California attorney general and health secretary in the Biden administration. In the ad in Spanish, “Cien por ciento,” Becerra highlights his parents’ immigrant background and laments the high cost of living, lack of affordable housing and insufficient health care that Californians face today.

CAMPAIGN YEAR — FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: EYE ON THE BALL — Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas will today endorse Chris Kluwe in the race for a legislative seat in Orange County. Kluwe, a former NFL player and civil-rights advocate, is running for the Assembly seat in District 72 (Huntington Beach, Newport Beach), against Republican Gracey Van Der Mark.

Rivas’ endorsement suggests that Democrats are once again prioritizing their efforts to flip the seat held by outgoing Assemblymember Diane Dixon, a Republican who Democrats unsuccessfully targeted in 2024. Rivas said Kluwe is “ready to bring strong, common-sense leadership to the State Assembly and stand up for Orange County families.”

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: WIENER’S BLITZ — Wiener’s campaign will today launch a pair of new TV ads, including a spot meant to be a throwback to entrances from the sitcom “Cheers.” The spot was filmed at Harrington’s, a historic Irish pub in the Financial District, and features bar patrons repeatedly invoking the name “WIENER!” It will air on broadcast, cable and digital platforms as part of a six-figure buy.

The second spot features young parents, an immigrant activist and a diabetic patient talking about his legislative record on issues like parental leave, drug prices and housing construction.

FOR GOOD MEASURE — FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: HOME RUN — Proponents of Proposition 37, a bond measure aimed at making homeownership more affordable for the middle-class, are up with their first ad today. The eight-figure buy on broadcast, cable and digital platforms touts the low-interest down payment loan program that would start under the initiative.

EDUCATION — FUNDING FIGHT — The California Teachers Association is taking Newsom to court over a maneuver in this year’s budget deal that withholds $3.9 billion in funding for public schools that the state is using temporarily as a loan to other parts of the budget. The powerful labor union, which is no stranger to publicly feuding with the governor over the budget, plans to announce a lawsuit today over the deferral that they have argued “ignores the state constitution.”

A broad coalition of education groups, including teachers unions and administrative groups, campaigned to oppose Newsom’s deferral plan that lawmakers ultimately approved. The funding, the school groups argue, is critical to help several school districts pay off large contracts they signed this year with teachers amid a wave of labor tension and strike threats across the state. Department of Finance officials have defended the deferral as necessary to “mitigate against potential future reductions in revenues” as the state stares down years of projected budget deficits. — Eric He

HEALTH — FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: JUNK FOOD JUNKET — Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel will appear in Washington today alongside congressional Democrats to drum up opposition to a piece of draft legislation he says would be “a slap in the face to the MAHA movement.”

A food industry-backed draft by Florida Republican Rep. Kat Cammack known as the FRESH and Affordable Foods Act would preempt state food regulations and overhaul how ingredients are reviewed in the U.S.

“The industry understands that it’s losing. It’s losing in blue states like California. It’s losing in red states,” he said. “So now they’re going to go to Congress and try to preempt all that.”

The MAHA-backed push to regulate ultraprocessed foods has been met with fierce opposition in D.C. from industry groups like the Consumer Brands Association, who also pushed back against several of Gabriel’s UPF-focused efforts. The group that represents household names like Doritos and Kraft Mac & Cheese has thrown its weight behind Cammack’s proposal and is part of a group spending big in the midterms to back candidates that support their ideas.

While HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. hasn’t weighed in on Cammack’s proposal, it would undo regulations he has largely encouraged states to pursue.

“If Republican leadership lets that proposal get traction, it tells you everything you need to know about where they really stand,” Gabriel said. — Nicole Norman

With that in mind, California Currents has a guide to the battle that will likely determine whether the state can meet its ambitious climate goals.

TOP TALKERS — SLOW YOUR ROLL — Harris joined calls to slow down the pace of artificial intelligence development, warning Monday that the technology could “escape human control” without stronger safeguards. “The frontier of artificial intelligence is advancing at an alarming speed,” the former vice president said in a statement on X, arguing that slowing the development of the most advanced AI systems doesn’t mean giving up on the potential “life-saving benefits in fields like medicine.”

SKID ROW TROUBLES — The largest provider of housing for formerly homeless residents in Los Angeles’ Skid Row area has warned city officials it is running out of money and will collapse unless it receives more public funding or other assistance, its leader told Liam Dillon. The prospect of SRO Housing’s failure puts thousands of the city’s most vulnerable tenants at risk and creates a significant new problem for Mayor Karen Bass.
