IN THE RED — Eric Jones, the Our Revolution-backed Democrat challenging longtime Rep. Mike Thompson in the Napa Valley, is now trying to beat him by courting the right.

In recent weeks, Jones has touted endorsements from all six Republican opponents and one independent that he and Thompson faced in the June primary. He launched a group titled Californians Against the Democratic Establishment to boost his outreach to GOP and independent voters. And he’s been spotted at at least two Republican fundraisers (Jones said he attended the events, but did not donate).

It’s a stark contrast (though not the only recent exception) to many other Democrats who’ve waged anti-incumbent, generational challenges across the country this cycle by running as ultra-progressive Democrats or democratic socialists.

Jones, a 35-year-old venture capitalist who appealed to progressives in the primary — and finished nearly 20 percentage points behind Thompson — told Playbook that voters at both ends of the political spectrum are frustrated by the cost of living and the status quo in Washington. Thompson, 75, has represented the district for nearly 30 years and is backed by much of the Democratic establishment.

“Even though our values may be different, we all recognize the problem, which is corruption in establishment politics,” Jones said. “The flavor of anti-establishment will be different all across the country. But the agreement is that this Democratic Party is not for us.”

There is logic to Jones’ strategy. Thompson, who had strong support among Democratic primary voters, received 41 percent of the district’s vote in June — compared with 22 percent for Jones and nearly 21 percent for Republican Ray Riehle (who’s leading Jones’ outreach to Republicans). It’s those latter voters who may be up for grabs.

To that end, Jones, who has poured more than $5.3 million into the race, has Republicans advising him now, too. His campaign advisers include heavyweight Democratic consultants like Mark Longabaugh, Julian Mulvey, Steve Spinner and Robb Korinke, but also veteran Republican strategist Rob Stutzman and Crystal Martin, a GOP consultant from the Central Valley.

Jones, who supports expanding Medicare and banning corporate PAC money in elections, said he’s made inroads with Republican voters by talking about issues like term limits and banning members of Congress from trading stocks as he campaigns in rural parts of the district. Swaths of Yuba and Sutter counties in the Central Valley and Sierra Foothills were recently drawn into the district when voters approved California’s new, Democratic-drawn congressional map.

But … he has to sell it. And Thompson’s campaign has pounced on Jones’ overtures to Republican voters, portraying him as a political chameleon.

“We don’t know what Eric Jones believes because he changes his mind every time he enters a new room,” said Thomas Dowling, Thompson’s campaign manager. “He’s lied about the stocks he owned, he’s lied about his career as a San Francisco venture capitalist, and he’s lied about when he moved to the district.”

(Jones says he has been a resident of Napa since 2021, though he previously told Playbook that he forgot to update his old San Francisco voter registration until January 2025. Jones also said at a March event that he doesn’t own stocks. His campaign said the $500,000 to $1 million of “ownership in stock” listed on his candidate financial disclosure is a dividend-paying stake in a private company, not a publicly-traded stock.)

LOS ANGELES

D.C. SCRUTINY — House Republicans are investigating “potential waste, fraud and abuse” of federal homelessness dollars in Los Angeles, and they want to hear from Karen Bass. The Los Angeles mayor was asked to testify before the DOGE subpanel of the House oversight committee on Sept. 15, according to a letter from GOP Rep. Tim Burchett.

The invitation was first reported by LAist, which has published multiple stories about apparent misuse of funds by the LA Homeless Services Authority.

A spokesperson for Bass did not say whether Bass planned to accept the invitation and told Playbook the mayor’s office “recently received the letter and is currently preparing a response.” Bass told a Fox 11 reporter she is “talking to my lawyers” about it.

Bass has frequently touted the relationships she formed across the aisle during her time in Congress, but the tone of Burchett’s letter indicates she may face a grilling before the committee. He writes that her administration’s approach to homelessness has led to “deteriorating public health, increased lawlessness and a bleak environment for small businesses.”

Rep. Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on House oversight, laced into Republicans for their “partisan effort.”

“This is clearly another attempt by Donald Trump and House Republicans to launch a witch hunt against Mayor Karen Bass and Los Angeles,” the former Long Beach mayor said in a statement.

NEWSOMLAND

EARLY REVIEWS — The South Carolina Republican Party was urging followers — apparently with little success — to attend Newsom events as he worked the Lowcountry, The Post and Courier reported.

“Come out, bring a sign, and help us remind Gavin that while we’re always happy to welcome visitors, we’re not interested in bringing California politics to South Carolina,” read one email.

The newspaper reported no protesters at a Jasper County event. Instead, Newsom had a crowd of about 150 people on their feet. The paper quoted Hardeeville resident Betty Thomas as describing his appearance as inspirational, saying, “It makes me want to get out there even more and do everything I can possibly do to get the Democratic vote.”

Not that the GOP didn’t have plenty to say. Alan Wilson, the heavy favorite to win the governorship in November, wrote of Newsom’s visit on X, “Maybe he’ll run into some of the Californians who fled the mess he created back home. Higher costs. Bigger government. Failed liberal policies. California can keep it. Don’t California My South Carolina.”

TECH TALK

ON ZUCK’S DIME — Cash-strapped California just won a potential $2.1 billion payout from tech giant Meta’s multistate child safety settlement — the largest share any U.S. state or territory is receiving from the total pot of up to $18 billion.

Now the challenge: figuring out how to spend it, Tyler Katzenberger reports for POLITICO Pro Technology: California Decoded.

Lawmakers who spoke with Decoded pitched nearly a dozen competing ideas for spending the payout. Some wanted to invest the money back into enforcing social media laws. Others thought that, with Republican-backed federal health care cuts reducing California’s budget, the settlement cash should backfill behavioral health services or suicide prevention programs.

But with only so much money to go around — and strings attached to more than a third of the settlement funds — lawmakers likely won’t be able to fund all of their ideas.

“Many of those proposals cost money,” said Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel. “We’re going to need to think very thoughtfully and carefully consider how we can best deploy those resources to support young people.”
