The Legislature fights back

By BLAKE JONES, RACHEL BLUTH, MELANIE MASON and DUSTIN GARDINER

CAUGHT IN A JAM — Gavin Newsom usually gets what he wants from the California Legislature, which made his concessions in an eleventh-hour wildfire liability push all the more striking.

A final deal reached over the weekend dropped the governor’s most contentious proposals, including eliminating insurance companies’ ability to recoup losses from a wildfire recovery fund, limiting payouts for emotional distress and preventing local governments from recovering the full cost of replacing damaged infrastructure.

The rare stumble — coming in the final months of Newsom’s tenure — marked an uncommon show of defiance by a Legislature that hasn’t overridden a veto in decades and has acquiesced when the governor made late pushes for a suite of environmental laws in 2022 and an infrastructure package the following year.

Lawmakers, staffers and other negotiators who spoke to Playbook attributed the governor’s loss to a slew of factors, including the tight timeline on which he demanded major changes to a politically fraught policy with billions of dollars at stake.

“My hope is that the incoming governor is going to be in lockstep conversations with us, so we can get to solutions before the last day of session,” state Sen. Lola Smallwood-Cuevas said. (A spokesperson for Xavier Becerra, the front-runner to succeed Newsom, did not respond to a request for comment on the deal.)

Members often attribute their willingness to acquiesce to the governor to the leverage he holds by having power over whether their bills are signed or vetoed — which is still the case this year. But soon, he’ll lose that authority, which is making at least one Democratic lawmaker openly consider a precedent-breaking veto override.

“I would hope our leadership would look at that, because he’s not going to be here next year. There will be nothing sensitive if we override vetoes and he’s not here,” said state Sen. Caroline Menjivar.

To some members, Newsom had simply asked for too much. The governor met resistance in the Senate, in particular, where the energy committee chair is state Sen. Ben Allen, a Democrat from a wildfire-stricken Los Angeles region currently in a tight election for statewide insurance commissioner. He said Newsom went a “step too far” in proposing to eliminate the ability of insurance companies to recoup some of their losses from power companies, a move that insurers and consumer advocates said could raise premiums and lower coverage in fire-prone areas.

The governor’s plan set off an all-out lobbying fight after he unveiled it earlier this month. Outside groups poured millions of dollars into swaying legislators, and a broad coalition of insurers, local governments and consumer advocates came out against it.

“The primary challenge was the volume of the opposition from the institutional stakeholders — including insurance companies, hedge funds, and trial lawyers — who have made billions off the current system,” said Newsom adviser Ann Patterson.

In a year where political campaigns have increasingly railed against utilities, taking any action that could be seen as a win for utilities was a political nonstarter for some.

“What he was doing was incredibly unpopular,” said a senior Senate staffer granted anonymity to describe private negotiations. “When he runs for president in two years, I’ll be happy. I’ll be happy they can’t use this against him.”

LAST MINUTE LEG — The Legislature is set to work late into the night today.

On the docket for the last official day of session are the last-minute deals between lawmakers and Newsom on setting rules for data centers’ electricity use and limiting the effects of a new tax credit cap on recipients in Hollywood (but not for the state’s research industry).

Legislators before midnight are expected to pass a deal to resurrect a scientific research bond and place the $7.5 billion measure on the March 2028 ballot. They’ll also be able to vote on an agreement lawmakers reached shortly before 10 p.m. Friday to divvy up smaller-than-expected proceeds from the state’s carbon auctions.

The much-narrowed wildfire liability package will be taken up Tuesday morning, as Camille von Kaenel reported over the weekend for Pro subscribers, after the session’s official end. Lawmakers added an urgency clause to the bill allowing for a later vote prompted by negotiations that dragged on into early Saturday morning. It needs a two-thirds vote to pass.

Other legislation POLITICO’s California team has been tracking:

Chatbots — State lawmakers made final amendments Friday to a closely watched bill on kids’ use of AI chatbots, clarifying when models can “remember” prior conversations and somewhat narrowing instances in which families could sue developers, among other changes, Chase DiFeliciantonio reports for Pro subscribers. The bill, SB 1119 from state Sen. Steve Padilla, comes as OpenAI and other chatbot developers face a slew of lawsuits alleging the use of such AI models contributed to child harms or teen deaths by suicide.

AI fight — Rachel Bluth writes that Assemblymember Liz Ortega is hoping last-minute amendments will help her resurrect a health care AI regulation bill that failed on the Senate floor last week. Ortega will have another shot at a floor vote today, but AB 2575 still faces opposition from hospitals, doctors and CalChamber.

Ticket sales — A two-year bill from Democratic Assemblymember Isaac Bryan that would ban “speculative” tickets and make other changes to online ticket sale practices was hit with late opposition from the bill’s sponsor, the California chapter of the National Independent Venue Association. After being dissatisfied with late amendments, the association sent a floor alert urging state senators to vote against the bill it had once championed.

ICE pushback — An Assembly bill backed by Speaker Robert Rivas that would bar ICE agents from public jobs in the state still awaits a vote in both chambers. The bill, co-authored by Los Angeles Democrat Mark Gonzalez, has received a few rounds of late amendments in response to concerns from law enforcement groups.

License plate readers — Assemblymembers will also vote on a revamped proposal from Democratic state Sen. Sabrina Cervantes that supporters argue will crack down on misuse of data from automated license plate readers, a la Flock Safety. The amended bill would require public agencies to archive most license plate reader data after 60 days, starting in 2028.

While public agencies may still access archived data with a California warrant, law enforcement associations remain opposed to the legislation, citing procedural concerns with warrant language and the blocking of already-accessible data. Newsom vetoed similar legislation from the Riverside legislator last year, which would have required the deletion of most ALPR data after 60 days.

Teen social media restrictions — Senators are slated to vote Monday on an Assemblymember Josh Lowenthal bill that would ban allegedly addictive social media features, like infinite scrolling and personalized content feeds, for kids under 16. Tyler Katzenberger reports that Lowenthal made last-minute tweaks to the bill Friday aimed at boosting LGBTQ+ advocates’ role in implementing the ban, in response to concerns that it could limit some teens’ ability to find supportive communities online.

And lawmakers sent these bills to Newsom:

Sexual assault liability — Lawmakers on Sunday approved a measure that offers school districts and other public agencies some protections from the rising costs of sexual assault lawsuits, Eric He reports for Pro subscribers. SB 577, which passed the Senate and the Assembly on party-line votes, now heads to Newsom’s desk. It would raise the standard of proof that alleged victims must meet in older cases and limit how much a public entity is required to pay when it is found to be only partially liable for the abuse.

Social media harms — On the heels of California’s landmark $18 billion settlement against Meta, lawmakers are calling for Newsom to raise penalties for social media giants found liable for mental harms to children. The proposal from Lowenthal threatens penalties of up to $1 million per child harmed by negligent platform design.

Seal of approval — Lawmakers will ask Newsom to literally make their mark on the war against ultraprocessed foods with a bill from Democratic Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel that would create the first-of-its-kind seal on foods that meet the standard set by the same lawmaker last year.

Housing Hail Mary — Legislation to advance an affordable housing development from Oakland Assemblymember Buffy Wicks is on its way to Newsom’s desk, despite its focus on a Los Angeles-based project opposed by local officials, among them Mayor Karen Bass. The proposal would allow the California Coastal Commission to extend its lapsed approval of the development, lifting a key obstacle for the project.

Antitrust powers — A bill that would expand the state’s century-old antitrust legislation is headed to Newsom’s desk after a final vote on Sunday, Nicole Norman reports. The California Chamber of Commerce, the bill’s main opposition, removed the legislation from its “Cost Drivers” list following amendments. The bill also received late support from the office of Attorney General Rob Bonta, who argued in a letter obtained by POLITICO that “California law currently lacks a clear state prohibition on monopolization.”

Coal no more — In the latest battle over the environment between California legislators and the Trump administration, lawmakers are asking Newsom to thwart plans for a Trump-backed coal export facility in Oakland. The bill, authored by Democratic Assemblymember Mia Bonta, would bog down developers with environmental impact reports for high capacity coal projects and any subsequent changes to them.

Environmental reviews — State legislators are asking Newsom to reinstate certain environmental reviews they’d pulled back last year to speed up development. State Sen. Catherine Blakespear’s bill would limit exemptions for advanced manufacturing projects and revive some protections for endangered species.

Too sweet — Sacramento lawmakers want Newsom and California customers to rethink any future drive-thru orders for sugary drinks. A bill from Democratic state Sen. Akilah Weber would require chain restaurants to label extra sweet drinks in drive-thru menus with an icon to signal added sugar content exceeding 100 percent or more of the daily recommended serving.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: RACE HEATS UP — Karen Bass is ramping up her attacks on mayoral rival Nithya Raman’s record on the city council. The campaign has launched NoShowNithya.com to call out Raman’s missed votes and meetings over her six-year tenure in City Hall.

“Nithya Raman is asking the voters of Los Angeles for a promotion when she’s failed to do her job — racking up 3000 missed votes and the second-worst attendance record on the City Council while ignoring pleas from her constituents to do the bare minimum,” said Bass campaign spokesperson Alex Stack. “If Angelenos can’t trust her to show up for work, we sure as hell shouldn’t give her a promotion to be our next mayor.”

The website builds on the salvos lobbed by Bass when the two met for a mayoral debate at the Sherman Oaks Homeowners Association earlier this month, in which Bass blasted Raman for missing “thousands of votes.” Raman shot back that many of those absences were due to her roles on other governmental boards, including some that Bass had appointed her to.

WAITING FOR JON VOIGHT — Read Daniel Miller on his search for Jon Voight at the Beverly Glen Deli, a strip-mall outpost atop Bel Air … Tapped a year earlier to “Make Hollywood Great Again,” the actor has focused on developing a federal film tax incentive meant to level the global playing field after a decadeslong exodus of production overseas. Members of Congress are actively working on legislation that could be introduced as soon as September, according to two people familiar with the bipartisan process who were granted anonymity to discuss private deliberations.

Amid a summer of Hollywood conniptions and crises — much of it over Paramount Skydance’s prospective acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, but at least some of it over runaway production — the “Heat” and “Ali” actor’s brief seemed more urgent than ever. And I wasn’t the only one looking for Voight.

Across Los Angeles, entertainment insiders have been asking one another for weeks what the Oscar winner for “Coming Home” was up to — and whether he could deliver. They are counting on him, despite the uncomfortable incongruity for many in deep-blue Hollywood that Voight, an avowed Trump loyalist dating back a decade, has been cast as the industry’s hoped-for savior. STANFORD DAILY SUIT — A federal judge declared that the Trump administration’s efforts to deport noncitizens for political speech run counter to the Constitution’s First and Fifth Amendments, Gregory Svirnovskiy and Ben Johansen report.

Northern District of California Judge Noël Wise, a Biden appointee, sided on Friday with the Stanford Daily, which sued Secretary of State Marco Rubio, arguing that his use of the Immigration and Nationality Act to deport noncitizen students had foreign students refraining from writing opinion pieces critical of the administration.

PROP 40 PLAN — Proponents of the California wealth tax plan to combat a deluge of spending against them with a form of personalized outreach called relational organizing, SEIU-UHW President Dave Regan told the San Francisco Chronicle. Though other campaigns have used the tactic of deploying supporters to contact people they know about the measure, Regan said “it’s not been done at this scale before.”
