The Shadow Food Safety Czar: How Jesse Gabriel is Rewriting the Rules of the American Diet

Nana Wu

In the quiet corridors of the California State Capitol, far removed from the hyper-partisan theater of Washington, D.C., a pragmatic assemblyman has emerged as the most influential figure in American food safety. While federal agencies remain paralyzed by industry lobbying and political infighting, California Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel has successfully navigated the treacherous landscape of food regulation, turning what was once dismissed as niche "California woo-woo" into a potent, bipartisan legislative model that is now being replicated across the nation.

As the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) movement—bolstered by the alliance between Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Donald Trump—struggles to translate its populist rhetoric into meaningful federal policy, Gabriel’s methodical, state-level approach has quietly achieved what Washington has failed to do for decades: setting enforceable standards for the food supply.

The Regulatory Vacuum: A System Built on Loopholes

For decades, the average American consumer has operated under the assumption that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) serves as a vigilant guardian of the national diet. However, as Gabriel discovered in 2023, that assumption is largely a myth.

The core of the problem lies in the "GRAS" (Generally Recognized as Safe) loophole. Under current federal law, food manufacturers are permitted to self-certify that new chemical additives are safe, effectively bypassing rigorous FDA scrutiny. This systemic failure has allowed substances like Red No. 3—a colorant linked to cancer in animal studies—to remain in our food supply for years after it was banned in cosmetics.

"I always assumed there was someone in Washington, D.C., watching my back," Gabriel remarked during a recent interview. "When I learned that wasn’t the case, it actually made me angry."

This discovery was the catalyst for Gabriel’s pivot from a traditional progressive legislator to a de facto national leader in food safety. By targeting the pervasive use of toxic chemicals in ultraprocessed foods, he has exposed the fragility of a federal system that has essentially outsourced public health protection to the very industries it is tasked with regulating.

A Chronology of Reform: From "Skittles" to School Lunches

The trajectory of Gabriel’s legislative impact began in 2023, but its roots are in the broader dissatisfaction with the status quo.

October 2023: Gabriel signs the California Food Safety Act into law. Despite intense pushback from the confectionary industry, the bill bans four major chemical additives—Red No. 3, potassium bromate, brominated vegetable oil, and propylparaben. It is quickly dubbed the "Skittles ban" by the press, capturing global attention.

2024: Building on his initial success, Gabriel introduces the California School Food Safety Act. By focusing on school lunches, he frames the issue not as "nanny-statism," but as a moral imperative to protect children. The bill passes with unanimous support from state legislators, including Republicans.

2025: Gabriel achieves another landmark victory with the Real Food, Healthy Kids Act. This legislation sets the nation’s first legal definition of ultraprocessed foods, creating a regulatory framework to restrict high-sugar, high-salt, and chemical-laden products from public school vending machines and cafeteria menus.

September 2026: Governor Gavin Newsom signs Gabriel’s fourth major food safety bill, establishing a voluntary labeling system to help consumers identify minimally processed foods, securing the support of even the California Grocers Association.

The MAHA-MAGA Disconnect: When Rhetoric Meets Reality

The rise of the MAHA movement has brought food safety to the center of the national conversation, yet the movement’s actual legislative output has been lackluster. While Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has used his platform to label ultraprocessed foods as "poison," his tenure as head of Health and Human Services (HHS) has been defined by corporate capture and minimal action.

When HHS finally introduced its proposed GRAS reforms in August, it did little more than require companies to "inform" the FDA when they unilaterally decide a new additive is safe. This "check-the-box" regulation is viewed by critics as a betrayal of the movement’s original promise to dismantle industry influence.

In contrast, Gabriel’s success is rooted in his refusal to adopt the combative, populist framing of MAHA. Instead, he utilizes a "proud incrementalist" strategy, educating colleagues on both sides of the aisle. By treating food safety as a non-partisan issue of parental rights and public health, he has stripped industry lobbyists of their traditional defenses.

Supporting Data: Why the Model Works

Gabriel’s legislative victories are not mere anomalies; they represent a shifting political tide. According to polling from the progressive firm Navigator Research, 84 percent of Americans believe it is the government’s responsibility to protect the food supply from harmful ingredients. Less than 50 percent believe the government is currently succeeding in that mission.

The efficacy of Gabriel’s approach is validated by the rapid "California Effect." Because California represents the largest economy in the U.S., manufacturers often find it fiscally impractical to maintain separate supply chains for the state. Consequently, when California bans a chemical, it effectively forces a nationwide reformulation. This has triggered a ripple effect, with similar food policy bills introduced in 43 states and enacted in 15 of them—an even split between red and blue states.

The Fight Against Federal Obstruction

As Gabriel’s influence grows, so too does the pushback from the food industry and its allies in Congress. The threat of "preemption"—a federal override that would strike down state-level protections in favor of weaker national standards—has become the new battleground.

In 2025, an attempt by Senator Roger "Doc" Marshall to include state law preemption in a MAHA-aligned bill was thwarted by a public outcry. Similarly, when Representative Kat Cammack proposed the "FRESH Act," which would have essentially codified industry interests at the expense of state safety laws, Gabriel mobilized the California delegation to stop it in its tracks.

Gabriel views these federal attempts as a "Trojan horse." He warns that if the federal government moves from "inaction to obstruction," it will not only halt progress but actively endanger public health. "This would move the federal government… from inaction to obstruction—to actually getting in the way of public health, getting in the way of laws that are meant to protect our kids," he said.

Implications for the Future

The implications of Gabriel’s work for the 2026 midterms and beyond are significant. Democrats, often criticized for failing to connect with voters outside of their coastal base, have in food safety a potent, unifying issue. By championing clean, safe, and transparent food systems, politicians can tap into a deep-seated, cross-party desire for accountability.

Gabriel’s career, characterized by his ability to maneuver complex political environments, suggests that the future of American regulation may not lie in the top-down mandates of a stagnant Washington, but in the laboratories of state democracy. He has proven that in an era of hyper-polarization, the right policy solution and the right messaging are often the same thing: common sense.

"Eighty percent of Democrats and Republicans in Washington don’t agree on what day of the week it is," Gabriel noted with a wry smile. "But when it comes to food safety, it’s fairly unprecedented, the depth of the support and the depth of the response. I can’t figure out why people wouldn’t want to talk about it."

As he continues to build a coalition of farmers, parents, and skeptical legislators, Gabriel is not just passing laws; he is fundamentally altering the power dynamics of the food industry, proving that even a single state can force a nation to clean up its plate. Whether he intends to become the "national" czar or remains in Sacramento, his blueprint has already been written—and it is currently being used by legislatures across the country to reclaim control over the American diet.
