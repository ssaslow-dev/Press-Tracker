California bans law enforcement use of 'shock gloves' and imposes tax on private detention facilities

By Staff (ABC10)
Return to Homepage
  • Governor Gavin Newsom has signed a series of bills in California aimed at protecting communities, enhancing public safety, and increasing oversight of federal immigration enforcement.
  • Two new laws in California prohibit law enforcement agencies from using wearable electroshock devices and impose a tax on private detention facilities.
  • The legislation is part of a broader package addressing immigration detention and law enforcement practices, reflecting ongoing disagreements between California and the federal government on immigration enforcement.

Governor Gavin Newsom has signed a slate of bills into law desinged to protect communities, strengthen public safety, and expand oversight of federal immigration enforcement in California.

California has enacted two measures targeting the use of wearable electroshock devices by law enforcement and the operation of private detention facilities in the state.

One new law Assembly Bill 2760, authored by Democratic Assemblymember Dr. LaShae Sharp-Collins, prohibits law enforcement agencies from using wearable electroshock devices, commonly described as "shock gloves," in California. The ban applies to state and local agencies as well as federal law enforcement agencies operating in the state. Agencies also cannot use state funds to purchase the devices and must update their use-of-force policies to prohibit their use.

The law defines a wearable electroshock device as a weapon designed to temporarily incapacitate someone through an electric shock and designed to be worn as a glove. Traditional conducted-energy weapons such as handheld Tasers are specifically excluded from the definition.

The ban is currently set to remain in effect through January 1, 2030.

A separate measure establishes a new tax on private detention facilities. Private detention facility operators will be subject to an annual tax equal to 25% of their gross income for the applicable fiscal year.

The two measures are part of a broader package of California legislation addressing immigration detention and law-enforcement practices. The laws come as California and the federal government continue to differ over how immigration enforcement should be carried out in the state.
