California is now the second state to prohibit the sale of weight loss and muscle building-marketed products to minors, following New York.

September 29, 2026

By: Mike Montemarano

California Gov. Gavin Newsom just signed a bill (AB 2030) into law prohibiting dietary supplements and other products marketed for weight loss and muscle building from being sold to minors.

A previous version of the legislation failed to advance in California in 2024, which would have tasked the state’s Department of Public Health with working alongside the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to produce a list of products that would be subject to the ban. In 2022, Newsom vetoed a similar bill, objecting on the grounds that the California Department of Public Health didn’t have the capacity to put together a list of dietary supplements subject to the ban.

New York was previously the only state to have successfully passed such a sales ban, met with legal challenges.

“Nonprescribed, medically unapproved weight loss or muscle-building products can have dangerous side effects and are too often mislabeled or tainted with harmful products like steroids, yet nearly 1 in 10 adolescents have used such products,” Newsom’s office said in a statement, citing a 2024 review. “In addition to the immediate risks, youth who use over-the-counter diet pills are six times more likely to be diagnosed with an eating disorder within three years than non-users,” Newsom’s office stated, citing a survey conducted by the Strategic Training Initiative for the Prevention of Eating Disorders (STRIPED) and others.

The Council for Responsible Nutrition (CRN) stated that it was deeply disappointed with Newsom’s signing of AB 2030. “The law’s overly broad approach could create unnecessary barriers to lawful products, confuse consumers, and leave responsible businesses facing uncertainty about which supplements fall within its restrictions,” the association stated.

That’s because, like New York’s law, the bills language targets the language with which products are marketed instead of targeting products shown to pose a risk to public health. CRN has petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court to review New York’s age restriction on supplements for similar reasons, arguing that it raises First Amendment concerns by restricting how companies can communicate truthful information about legal products.

“This is a deeply disappointing outcome for consumers and responsible businesses, and a missed opportunity for California to pursue a more thoughtful approach,” said Steve Mister, president and CEO of CRN. “Protecting young people from eating disorders is an important objective that we share. But that objective deserves policies grounded in evidence, not broad restrictions that cast suspicion on lawful products without establishing that those restrictions will address the problem.”

Importantly, the California law allows courts to consider statements or images that “imply” muscle building and weight loss, claims about metabolism or strength, or how products are positioned in stores or online when enforcing the ban.

“We did not simply object to this legislation. We pressed for a better, more defensible approach,” said Mister. “Our position has been consistent: identify the actual risk and address it directly. Responsible companies should not have to navigate an uncertain boundary between ordinary nutrition claims and age-restricted products, and consumers should not be left to assume that a sales restriction is proof a product is unsafe.”

“CHPA is disappointed that AB 2030, as signed into law, does not strike a better balance between our shared goal of protecting minors and preserving access to safe, beneficial products for adults who use them responsibly. We remain concerned that the law’s broad criteria could restrict access to dietary supplements containing ingredients with legitimate uses beyond weight loss or muscle building, including ingredients widely found in products that support general health, nutrition, and wellness,” said Carlos I. Gutiérrez, vice president of state and local government affairs at the Consumer Healthcare Products Association. Throughout the legislative process, CHPA worked constructively with lawmakers to address these concerns. We remain committed to working with California policymakers and public health officials on approaches that protect young people while preserving appropriate access and choice for adult consumers.”

The supplement industry should take this law as a nationwide warning sign, said Daniel Fabricant, PhD, president and CEO of the Natural Products Association. When states contradict FDA’s regulatory framework, “the result is an increasingly unworkable patchwork of conflicting requirements for manufacturers, retailers, and consumers. NPA was able to secure a veto last time, we were the only ones who asked and were able to get it. All of the added voices on these issues diluted the industry’s credibility this go around. The industry is losing the messaging battle when 20 states have had these [age restriction] proposals in the last four years … It’s time for a clear and frank evaluation on these things and who can actually win on them. Time has shown that NPA has the track record and credentials if the industry is serious about securing its future.”

AB 2030 may become the blueprint for lawmakers across the country, Fabricant noted, and people may have different access to the same legal products depending on which side of a state line they’re on. “This is exactly why Congressman Langworthy’s federal preemption legislation is so important. Congress needs to draw a clear line and reaffirm that dietary supplements should be regulated under one consistent, science-based national framework not subjected to an ever-expanding maze of conflicting state restrictions.”

California State Assembly bill AB 2244 was passed by the state’s legislature earlier this month, and has now also become law.

The bill establishes a voluntary certification process allowing for food manufacturers to have their products evaluated by independent certifiers to determine if those products are non-ultra-processed, at which point the product would be permitted to bear a “Non-Ultra-processed Certified” seal.

The label “empowers consumers with clear, front-of-package information and improved access to healthier options through strategic, in-store placement,” Newsom’s office reported.

“California is focused on making whole, healthy foods as accessible as possible [and] making healthier choices easier. California has never been afraid to challenge the status quo, and we’ll keep innovating, trying new things, and clearing the way for the rest of the country to follow,” Newsom said.

“While Washington D.C. is paralyzed by inaction, Republicans and Democrats in California are joining forces to empower consumers to avoid harmful ultra-processed foods,” said Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), who sponsored the bill. “Like the USDA Organic label, this new seal will provide consumers with clear, trustworthy information and make it easier for them to locate healthier foods that are free from harmful additives. Parents shouldn’t need a Ph.D. in chemistry to understand what they’re feeding their kids. California began this movement three years ago by banning the most dangerous food additives, and with Governor Newsom’s continued leadership, we are taking another big step forward today.”
