A big weekend for Eric Swalwell

By MELANIE MASON, DUSTIN GARDINER and BLAKE JONES

TRUMP CARD — Eric Swalwell’s campaign for governor had a very good weekend — by both normal political standards and Trump-era ones.

On Sunday, he scooped up the endorsement of the powerful California Teachers Association, the last major labor prize the gubernatorial candidates had been jockeying for. Organized labor — like Democrats as a whole — has struggled to rally behind a single candidate. But with the support of CTA — along with SEIU’s state council, the United Food and Commercial Workers and the statewide firefighter’s union — Swalwell has locked down some of the biggest heavyweights.

The nod from the teachers came on the heels of a bombshell story by the Washington Post that FBI Director Kash Patel is pushing to release a decade-old case file pertaining to Swalwell’s links to a suspected Chinese intelligence operative. The investigation did not lead to any criminal charges, and a House Ethics probe was closed by Republican committee leaders in 2023 with no action.

This, if true, would be another norm-shattering move by the Trump administration. Releasing such material five weeks before ballots drop would, at the very least, appear to be an effort to influence the California governor’s race. A spokesperson for the FBI disputed the contentions in the Post story; Playbook’s requests for comment went unanswered.

Swalwell, in an interview with Playbook, had plenty of harsh words for the Trump administration — but he didn’t sound all that mad. Sure, the report raises fresh concerns about the politicization of the Justice Department and the president’s retribution campaign against his enemies. But it also hands Swalwell a fight he relishes, elevating him as a leading opponent to Trump.

“I believe [it] shows what has been, in part, the reason I’m running, which is: I’ve been in the arena. I’ve taken on this guy. It’s come with a cost, but it’s not going to stop me,” he said.

In a state where the president is broadly unpopular, that’s a politically desirable position to be in. Just ask Gavin Newsom.

Swalwell wouldn’t go so far to say the report is a boon to his campaign (though plenty of pundits did it for him). But in a race where the top Democratic contenders have struggled to break out, an ongoing skirmish with Patel and Trump is one way he can distinguish himself from Tom Steyer and Katie Porter.

“It validates and reinforces that we are the biggest threat to Trump in the field, the only name he knows, the only person he attacks,” he said.

But, but, but … don’t call Swalwell the favorite just yet (though he’s certainly claiming that title for himself). Polling has consistently shown that Democratic voters have not lined up en masse behind a candidate, which is why the party is now openly panicking that two Republicans may end up advancing from June’s top-two primary.

The governor’s race has yet to command Californians’ attention, and there’s no guarantee this new storyline will stay on their minds until they cast their votes. That may explain why Swalwell is kicking off the week in San Francisco this morning with a press conference. The topic: “Kash Patel’s weaponization of the FBI.”

OPENING THE BOOKS — Assembly Democrats are already trying to get their large and sometimes unwieldy caucus on the same page ahead of budget crunch time with an extensive survey about how to best handle the state’s multibillion-dollar budget deficit.

A lawmaker granted anonymity to discuss caucus conversations told Playbook the survey contained dozens of questions and asked for feedback on specific ideas, from closing prisons to increasing revenue by tightening corporate tax rules.

Nick Miller, a spokesperson for Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas, confirmed the survey was used to seek lawmaker input, saying in a statement that it “covered nearly every [Legislative Analyst’s Office] option on the budget — more choices than a Cheesecake Factory menu. Trump is coming after California. We’re doing our homework.”

Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel declined to divulge details, but he said lawmakers “will have more to share about the Assembly’s approach to the budget in the coming weeks.”

Assembly Democrats last June polled their members’ interest in revenue-raising options. Twenty said they wanted to move forward with them, 16 said they were open to the prospect in the future and 15 said they didn’t want to raise taxes. Nine members declined to take the survey.

The caucus administered that poll late into the budget negotiation process. This time around, it seems lawmakers want to move earlier to better position Rivas and Gabriel to respond to Newsom’s May budget proposal.

“Chair Gabriel has made it very, very clear that he wants every member of the caucus to be involved and to have a voice in that process,” said Assemblymember Nick Schultz. “And from what I understand — or at least the feeling that I have from my colleagues that have been here a long time — that hasn’t always been the case. I think folks were a little bit more unaware of how the whole process worked, and it definitely feels like this is more of a group exercise.” — Lindsey Holden, Nicole Norman and Eric He

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: SOCAL FOR SWALWELL — Swalwell continues to rack up endorsements from his Democratic colleagues in the California congressional delegations. The latest to endorse him for governor: Reps. Julia Brownley and Scott Peters, who both hail from Southern California. Brownley, in a statement, said she’s “seen firsthand how Eric Swalwell brings people together to take on tough challenges and deliver results for California,” while Peters praised Swalwell as “a leader who will fight to make California more affordable and level the economic playing field.”

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: FAN MAIL — GrowSF, a powerful moderate advocacy group in San Francisco, will today endorse state Sen. Scott Wiener in the race to succeed Nancy Pelosi in Congress. The group produces one of the city’s most-read voter guides, so its backing could provide Wiener a crucial boost ahead of primary ballots landing in early May. Steven Buss Bacio, co-founder of GrowSF, cited Wiener’s track record of taking on tough legislative fights, adding that “in a field defined by obstruction or aspiration, he stands apart for delivering real results.”

FEAR THE DEADLINE — This year’s candidate filing deadlines brought an extraordinary amount of chaos. There was Green Party gubernatorial candidate Butch Ware missing the ballot due to redaction errors in his tax filings. There was Westlake Village councilmember Kelly Honig missing a shot at succeeding Assemblymember Jacqui Irwin, after filing paperwork in the wrong county.

And then state Sen. Anna Caballero, already financially outmatched by Lt. Gov. Eleni Kounalakis in the treasurer’s race, will not have a candidate statement in this election’s voter guide — one of the most cost-effective ways candidates can get their message out.

A spokesperson for Caballero said the campaign intentionally didn’t send a statement for the voter guide to the secretary of state by a recent deadline.

“Under California law, candidates can only submit a Voter Information Pamphlet statement if they accept strict spending limits,” Caballero spokesperson Elisa Rivera said in a statement. “The campaign chose not to tie our hands in case our opponent, whose family is worth hundreds of millions, floods the race with unlimited personal wealth as she has done before.”

Kounalakis ended up accepting the limit, which, at nearly $8 million, far exceeds the amount she has in her account for the race.

Caballero, meanwhile, ended last year with just over $1 million on hand after spending less than half that in 2025. She would have had to raise more than many candidates for governor to hit the spending ceiling during the primary. Caballero accepted the spending limit back when she filed initial paperwork in March 2023, but could have theoretically changed course and rejected it at the last moment to try to counter Kounalakis if she did the same.

Instead, she will be the only major candidate in the race without a statement in the pamphlet.

HARD SELL — Swalwell repeatedly pitched his AI-powered fundraising start-up to fellow lawmakers and Democratic staff, NOTUS reports. One Democratic operative told the outlet Swalwell was “relentless” in peddling the company, which has received roughly $60,000 from more than a dozen Democratic campaigns. Swalwell’s former chief of staff and current campaign manager Yardena Wolf, with whom he formed the company, appeared to tout the product, known as Findster, from the congressional office.

Ethics experts said as a public official, Swalwell should not be promoting his own private interests. The sales pitch may run afoul of House ethics rules, but the committee’s chief counsel declined to comment. A spokesperson for Swalwell declined to answer specific questions about how Swalwell and Wolf promoted their company to other Democrats.

RESIGNATION ON HOLD — Progressive San Francisco Supervisor Jackie Fielder is going on medical leave to navigate a “mental health condition” after being hospitalized over the weekend, according to the San Francisco Chronicle. Fielder had originally told Mission Local she planned to resign from the board but a statement posted by her office Sunday evening said she needs time before making any major decisions.

The statement from her aides provided scant information on the specifics of her condition. “On matters of her personal health, we respectfully ask the press to give her the privacy and space necessary for healing, and not to report on unsubstantiated rumors,” Fielder’s aides said in the statement. “We urge everyone to treat this situation with the same respect and sensitivity as they would if she were recovering from any other medical emergency.”
