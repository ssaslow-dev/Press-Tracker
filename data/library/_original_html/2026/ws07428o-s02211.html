What if the biggest lever for improving the health of the next generation isn’t in hospitals or clinics, but in school cafeterias?

In this episode, Nora LaTorre, CEO of Eat Real, and California Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel join me to discuss how school food has become one of the most powerful drivers of children’s health in the United States. Schools now serve more meals than any restaurant chain in the country, which means what happens in cafeterias directly shapes the long-term health of millions of kids.

Nora explains how ultra-processed foods, chemical additives, and sugar-heavy meals are contributing to rising rates of obesity, diabetes, fatty liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction in children. Jesse shares how these concerns led to bipartisan legislation in California aimed at removing harmful additives and redefining what qualifies as real food in school meals.

We explore how policy, supply chains, subsidies, and food industry lobbying have created a system where the cheapest food is often the least healthy. At the same time, this conversation shows that change is possible when lawmakers, parents, farmers, and schools work together.

This episode also looks at the broader question of trust in government, the role of personal responsibility versus policy, and why fixing school food may be one of the most effective ways to improve public health in the United States.

This episode covers:

  • School Meals As The Largest Restaurant Chain In America

  • The Rise Of Ultra-Processed Food In Childhood Diets

  • California’s Real Food Healthy Kids Act

  • How Food Additives Enter The Supply Chain

  • Why Kids Are Developing Adult Diseases

  • Farm To School Programs And Local Food Systems

  • The GRAS Loophole And Food Safety Policy

  • Food Industry Lobbying And Regulation

  • Bipartisan Health Policy Success Stories

  • Rebuilding Trust Through Public Health Action

This conversation is for anyone concerned about children’s health, food policy, or how legislation can actually change the way Americans eat.
