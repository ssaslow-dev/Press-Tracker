California lawmakers pass bill to create non-ultra-processed food label

By Los Angeles Issue on 31 August 2026

California lawmakers have passed legislation aimed at establishing a first-of-its-kind certification for non-ultra-processed foods to help consumers identify healthier options in grocery stores.

Assembly Bill 2244, authored by Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, would create a standardized “Non-Ultraprocessed Certified” label for products that meet specific state standards. The bill, which is co-sponsored by the Environmental Working Group, has been sent to Governor Gavin Newsom for his signature.

The proposed law would prohibit products from carrying the label if they contain certain additives used to manipulate taste or quality, including dyes, flavor enhancers, non-sugar sweeteners, and various processing aids. Assemblymember Gabriel indicated that the measure is intended to empower consumers with trustworthy information and make it easier to locate foods free from harmful additives without requiring advanced scientific knowledge to interpret packaging.

In addition to the new labeling, the bill requires big grocery retailers to make certified products more visible to shoppers. Any store with more than $10 million in annual sales that carries at least 25 different types of certified non-ultra-processed products must use methods such as special signage or physical separation within the store to highlight them.

If enacted, the California Department of Public Health will manage the program by accrediting third-party agents to perform independent certifications. The department is directed to complete this accreditation process by June 1, 2029, and products would be required to undergo recertification at least once every three years. To maintain credibility, the bill includes oversight measures such as mandatory registration for certification agents, state auditing authority, and the maintenance of a public online list of all certified products.

The legislation follows a previous law by Gabriel that defined ultra-processed foods and banned them from K-12 public schools. The new bill addresses concerns over the prevalence of industrially manufactured, chemically modified foods in the American diet. Research shows these products make up more than 66% of children’s diets and more than 50% of typical adult diets in the United States.

Medical experts have associated high consumption of ultra-processed foods with an increase in obesity, heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and various mental health issues. Supporters of the bill note that while federal oversight of food additives has been slow to evolve, state-level action can provide the transparency necessary for families to choose foods closer to traditional kitchen preparation rather than industrial manufacturing.
