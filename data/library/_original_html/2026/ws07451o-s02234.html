Who will help Newsom get stuff done?

By NICOLE NORMAN and LINDSEY HOLDEN

HELP WANTED: Gavin Newsom has a raft of heavy policy ideas he wants the Legislature to take on in his final year in office. And he’s going to need some help.

As he prepares for a likely presidential run, Newsom is seeking an extension of a business tax credit he has called “wildly successful” and proposals to tackle the state’s nagging cost-of-living problem by taking on institutional investors and bringing down the cost of housing construction.

Some of those ideas are already in the works. Others will require a bit of convincing, even in the Democratic-controlled Legislature.

Renewing the CalCompetes tax credit program, which launched in 2014 to incentivize businesses to move to and grow in the state.

Any takers? With a price tag of $180 million each fiscal year, an extension would likely move through the budget-making process rather than be taken up as a policy bill. Assemblymember Sharon Quirk-Silva, the Orange County Democrat who chairs the budget subcommittee on state administration, said she would weigh the proposal. But, she said, she wants to take a hard look at what businesses the tax break has helped and how they have used it.

“When we look at extending it for five years, that is definitely a big investment, so there will be negotiations,” she said in an interview. “Where are these dollars going? Who benefits from them … and are they really hiring full-time workers? Are these diverse businesses?”

What’s next: Budget subcommittee hearings where members will consider the governor’s proposals will continue this week and into the year, but no hearings for Quirk-Silva’s subcommittee or the corresponding subcommittee in the Senate are on the docket yet.

Senate Budget Committee Chair John Laird told Playbook that he will use budget retreat this week to find the “ground truth” on this proposal and others.

Language for the extension appeared as a budget trailer bill on Monday.

Preventing institutional investors, who Newsom said are “snatching up homes by the hundreds and thousands at a time,” from buying up single-family homes.

Any takers? Assemblymember Alex Lee’s ears pricked up when he heard the governor rail on institutional investors in his annual address. The San Jose Democrat recently led an effort to stymie corporate landlords who own more than 1,000 single-family homes from purchasing additional properties, a measure that stalled in the Senate Judiciary Committee last year. He told Playbook that he was not exactly sure how the governor plans to take on the issue, but could sense the Newsom administration wanted to “land the punch” before the Trump administration.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order in January for federal agencies to prevent large investors from acquiring more single family homes, addressing what he cast earlier this year as part of an American dream “increasingly out of reach for far too many people.”

Consider Lee an enthusiastic add-on. He said “the trifecta of Donald Trump, Gavin Newsom and Alex Lee cannot be stopped.”

What’s next: The bill has not been scheduled for any hearing. Lee remains “very open to collaborating with the governor.”

The governor’s office said that Newsom’s key priorities include giving the state more power to curb unfair competition in the housing market and address the concentration of large investor ownership in communities.

Reduce the cost of construction with new building methods and technology — “worker-centered reforms that bring our brothers and sisters in labor along with us.”

Any takers? One legislative proposal already underway is AB 1406 from Democratic Assemblymember Chris Ward. It would increase the amount of money that a seller can keep if a buyer backs out of a newly built condo purchase, from 3 percent to 6 percent. The bill is backed by California YIMBY and San Jose Mayor Matt Mahan, a Newsom critic running for California governor.

“It’s almost impossible to finance the construction of condos in California,” Mahan told Playbook while visiting the Capitol to lobby on the bill. “And we think the primary causes include construction defect liability [laws] that [are] far too restrictive and [don’t] have a clear right to repair and [create] a lot of fees.”

The bill cleared the Assembly last week with just enough votes to pass and will head to the Senate for further amendments.

What’s next: Quirk-Silva, who said she is already meeting with the governor’s office on housing issues, said there is a “real consensus” for getting new dollars in the budget to bring quicker and cheaper construction to the state.

But she and others said they would push for the state to devote more money for housing subsidies than the governor proposed in his budget this month, noting that he emphasized the chronic problem of homelessness in his annual address.

She said it “takes up a lot of the air out of this committee when you’re fighting just to put basic dollars in there.”

Assembly Budget Committee Chair Jesse Gabriel said at a budget hearing last month that the proposed budget’s lack of housing dollars is “hard to ignore.”

He told Playbook he wants to keep the momentum for programs that have “proven to be effective” like the low income housing tax credit and the multifamily housing program.

Do more to protect teens online.

Any takers? Newsom in his address cited a new Australian policy that blocks teens under the age of 16 from being on social media. A spokesperson for the governor’s office, Tara Gallegos, later said Newsom was only encouraging “discussion on whether additional protections for teens and children are needed,” rather than calling for a specific policy.

But POLITICO reported in December that the governor’s office had been meeting with Assemblymember Rebecca Bauer-Kahan, a Bay Area Democrat, on ways to regulate AI chatbots for children. Last year, Newsom vetoed Bauer-Kahan’s effort to regulate the technology and opted for a much narrower bill from state Sen. Steve Padilla that requires tech platforms to identify and address suicidal ideation. In his veto note, the governor said he wanted to develop a bill that does more to regulate AI to protect children.

Assemblymember Josh Lowenthal also said today at a tech policy event in Sacramento that he’s on the same page as Newsom, and that he’s “absolutely interested” in mirroring Australia’s stronger social media protections through California legislation.

What’s next: The Legislature will work on Padilla’s SB 300, which the Senate advanced to the Assembly last week. The bill would require operators to prevent AI chatbots from producing sexually explicit materials in interactions with minors.

Legislators also have until Feb. 20 to introduce new bills, giving Bauer-Kahan and other AI-focused legislators about a month to present new ideas.

FUNDING PATCH: A spending package that would fund the vast majority of the federal government cleared a key procedural hurdle today, setting up votes later in the day to send the measure to the White House for Trump’s signature, Katherine Tully-McManus reports.

Final passage of the measure, which also includes a funding patch for the Department of Homeland Security through Feb. 13, would end the partial government shutdown that began Saturday.

Republicans stayed mostly united on the 217-215 test vote to advance the package that would fund the Pentagon and departments of Health and Human Services, Labor, Housing and Urban Development, Transportation, and Education through Sept. 30 and less than two weeks of funding for the Department of Homeland Security.

The short-term DHS funding is intended to give lawmakers time to negotiate reforms to how ICE and CBP officers execute the Trump administration’s immigration enforcement crackdown, with the hope of tamping down instances where federal agents have used lethal force, including against U.S. citizens. Republicans have their own demands, such as blocking federal funding on “sanctuary cities” that don’t cooperate with federal enforcement agencies.

STEPPING AWAY: Increasingly violent threats toward and harassment of public officials — from county clerks up to the president — are driving more and more of those figures out of their jobs, a particular concern among local election officials who have struggled with attrition for years, Andrew Howard reports.

In the years since the 2020 election, roughly 50 percent of top local election officials across 11 Western states have left their jobs since November 2020, according to a new report from Issue One, a bipartisan organization that tracks election issues and supports campaign finance reforms.

The election administration world has been grappling with a significant brain drain since the one-two punch of the 2020 pandemic and threats arising from conspiracy theories surrounding that year’s election. But the new report — which focuses on election offices in Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Utah, Washington and Wyoming — is particularly concerning because it shows departures haven’t tapered off, marking a 10-percentage point uptick since the group’s 2023 report survey.

CLINTONS IN CONGRESS: Bill and Hillary Clinton will appear before the House Oversight and Government Reform Committee on Feb. 27 and Feb. 26, respectively — meeting a noon deadline imposed by GOP leadership to avoid being held in contempt of Congress, Hailey Fuchs reports.

“Republicans and Democrats on the Oversight Committee have been clear: no one is above the law — and that includes the Clintons,” House Oversight Chair James Comer said in a statement this afternoon. “Once it became clear that the House of Representatives would hold them in contempt, the Clintons completely caved and will appear for transcribed, filmed depositions this month.”

The former president and secretary of state have long sought to negotiate the terms of their cooperation with the panel’s investigation into convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein and argued that the congressional subpoenas compelling their testimony were invalid and designed to humiliate or put them in jail.
