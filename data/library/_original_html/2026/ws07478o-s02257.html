'Precariously balanced': Budget Committee hears dire warnings about California's structural deficit

John Mulholland

A California Assembly Budget Committee hearing held on Tuesday to discuss Gov. Gavin Newsom’s recent budget proposal was marked by a stark warning from the chief of the non-partisan Legislative Analyst Office, Gabriel Patek: California’s current budget stability is built on a market peak  which, he warns, is structurally unsound and in danger of collapse.

While the governor’s proposal identifies only a $3 billion deficit, Patek alerted the committee to a much deeper $18 billion shortfall,  centered on what he called the “considerable downside risks” of a stock market fueled by AI speculation.

The hearing also served as the debut for the new vice chair, Assembly Member David Tangipa, R-Fresno, who expressed concern over “historic, ongoing structural deficits.” Tangipa warned that the proposal before the committee reflects the Newsom’s priorities, not necessarily the values of the Assembly. “Californians cannot afford budgets built on wishful thinking,” Tangipa stated, and urged colleagues to demand accountability rather than pushing costs onto future taxpayers.

In his presentation, Patek identified three primary risks he believes the administration is underestimating. First,  the volatility of the current market; Patek explained that the recent revenue windfall is tethered to a stock market that is “on fire” but increasingly fragile. The AI boom is accounting for a large part of the market acceleration. He also noted that stock prices have reached a height where expected returns have shriveled to 1.5%, a level previously seen just before the 2008 financial crisis.

“All of this is telling us that the market is at least at risk of experiencing a downturn,” Patek told the committee. “While we’ve all heard the old adage ‘this time is different,’ we cannot rule that possibility out, but in good conscience, we cannot advise the Legislature to budget with that assumption.”

The second risk is the "structural" nature of the deficit. Patek explained that despite the stock market surging roughly 50% over the last two years, the state is still facing its fourth consecutive year of deficits. This suggests that California’s spending levels have outpaced growth, leaving a gap that persists even in “good” economic times.

The third risk involves California’s constitutional spending requirements. Patek noted that for every dollar of new revenue the state receives, a significant portion is locked into mandatory obligations, making it nearly impossible to grow out of a deficit without making hard cuts or identifying a clear plan to shrink the shortfall by at least $10 Billion this year. Patek warned that waiting for a better economic climate to fix the foundation is a gamble the state is likely to lose.

“It really takes a lot more than 35 billion in higher revenues to eliminate that deficit,” Patek explained. “Each dollar that we get, a big chunk of it has to go to fulfill our constitutional obligations, not just solve the deficit. And so that makes it very implausible, in our view, that we will just naturally grow out of it.”

Elsewhere in the hearing, Erica Lee from the Department of Finance presented the administration’s counter-narrative, describing a “workload budget” that maintains current services without new investments. Lee highlighted a $42.3 billion revenue upgrade and argued that the administration is being responsible by paying down $12 billion in long-term liabilities over the next four years. She categorized the current $2.9 billion deficit as manageable through technical maneuvers, such as suspending true-up deposits into the Rainy Day Fund — a move Patek later criticized as running counter to “basic fiscal logic.”

Committee Chair Jesse Gabriel, D-Encino, emphasized that the state cannot balance its books on the backs of the most vulnerable. However, he echoed the need for transparency, demanding that the governor share any major budget solutions well before the May revision. With over 60 public hearings scheduled for the coming months, the committee now faces the task of reconciling the administration’s optimism with the systemic risks outlined by the Legislative Analyst Office.
