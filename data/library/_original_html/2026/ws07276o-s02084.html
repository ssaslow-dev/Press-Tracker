The next test for the surging left

By BLAKE JONES, MELANIE MASON and DUSTIN GARDINER

AFTER NYC — Progressives romped to victory over establishment Democrats in deep blue New York City this week, but California’s Central Valley will provide a tougher, more enduring test of the populist left’s power.

There, Randy Villegas is gauging enthusiasm for a national movement bound by support for single-payer health care, banning congressional stock trading and taxing the rich as he challenges one of House Republicans’ most politically resilient frontliners, David Valadao. Villegas shares with New York progressives not just core policy positions but Zohran Mamdani media consultant Morris Katz and supporters including Rep. Ro Khanna and Sen. Bernie Sanders.

BIG PICTURE: Villegas’ contest will be a seismometer for progressives’ political earthquake, measuring whether it’s limited to regions like Brooklyn’s “commie corridor” or will reverberate into toss-up districts critical to the balance of power in the House.

A sign of the stakes: One of the victors of New York’s Tuesday House primaries, Mamdani-backed Brad Lander, included Villegas in a verbal short list of progressive candidates he’s watching, along with Graham Platner in Maine and Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan.

“I’m going to go help some frontliners win their races,” Lander told reporters Wednesday. “I hope some moderates will come help Randy Villegas and other progressives win theirs.”

Good news for Villegas: Democrats have made up ground across the map since their brutal 2024 performance. They believe Valadao is unusually vulnerable to attacks on votes to cut health care after a similar line seemingly contributed to his only loss in 2018, the last time he faced midterm backlash against the GOP with Donald Trump in office. The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, whose first choice, Assemblymember Jasmeet Bains, lost to Villegas, has already begun hammering Valadao with more health care-related ads since the primary.

The district’s heavy Democratic voter registration advantage also grew slightly in last year’s gerrymander. And Villegas’ strong performance in the primary has vindicated his local supporters who resist conventional wisdom that moderate “Valleycrats” are better suited to appeal to the region’s voters.

“Oftentimes here, people think you can’t win if you’re too progressive,” Kings County Democratic Party Chair Cathleen Jorgensen, who has known Villegas for around a decade and endorsed him before the primary, previously told Playbook. “And I just don’t believe that. I really don’t.”

Representatives for Villegas didn’t make him available for an interview, and a spokesperson for Valadao didn’t return a request for comment.

Good news for Valadao: Moderate Democrats have repeatedly failed to capitalize on the registration edge and increase what is some of the lowest turnout in the country. Villegas has argued, on the trail and in his PhD dissertation years earlier, that both parties’ machines have failed to rally politically engaged young voters from mixed immigration status families in the Latino-majority district. But because a progressive has never before been well-funded and nationally connected enough to advance to a run-off against Valadao and test an anti-Valleycrat approach, there’s not yet evidence that turnout will jump enough to push a progressive over the top.

Tal Eslick, a Central Valley consultant and former chief of staff to Valadao, acknowledged the contest would be competitive but questioned whether Democratic, older Latino voters in the district would stomach such progressive positions.

“I just don’t think it plays well here yet,” he said. “We’re going to again test that come November, and it’ll probably happen for the first time here at some point. I don’t think it’s going to be in 2026, though.”

FROM COAST TO COAST — The convincing wins of three progressive, Mamdani-backed congressional candidates in New York inevitably has drawn more attention to Raman, Los Angeles’ own democratic socialist candidate for mayor.

To Raman’s campaign, Tuesday’s results were not a stunning gamechanger, but an affirmation of the campaign’s thesis from the start.

“It’s not specific to [the] DSA — it’s a rejection of the political establishment,” said one Raman political adviser, who was granted anonymity to discuss campaign strategy.

Raman, a two-term city council member, is not a total political outsider. For that matter, neither was Mamdani, who served in the state legislature. But just as Mamdani positioned himself as an insurgent against incumbent Mayor Eric Adams and former Gov. Andrew Cuomo, Raman is making a similar case against Bass and the array of longtime political players lined up behind her.

The Mamdani comparisons came immediately when Raman entered the race but are not a perfect fit. Mamdani’s political movement is deeply intertwined with the DSA in New York. Raman, whose surprise win in 2020 was powered by grassroots progressives, has a more fraught relationship with the left. Though DSA recommended Raman in its primary voter guide, it pointedly noted it was not an endorsement and would not commit the group’s resources to the campaign.

Raman is also running a less openly ideological campaign than her DSA counterparts in New York. Her platform emphasizes housing affordability and improving meat-and-potatoes city operations such as streetlights, not the more sweeping promises for free buses or city-subsidized grocery stores that Mamdani relentlessly pitched. And so far, the Los Angeles mayoral race has not centered on debates around America’s policy toward Israel, while the recent congressional primaries hinged significantly on that growing litmus test issue.

OFF FIELD WIN — The World Cup was supposed to be a moment of unalloyed triumph for the city of Inglewood and the owner of SoFi Stadium, its signature sports venue. But the estranged partners have been embroiled in a long-simmering legal dispute, and now the city has emerged with a victory in court, Daniel Miller writes.

Hollywood Park, the sprawling mixed-use property that includes the $5 billion-plus stadium — host to eight of the tournament’s soccer matches — filed two lawsuits against the city after it struck a deal last year with digital billboard company WOW Media to install signs in Inglewood, including near the stadium.

In one complaint, Hollywood Park, which is controlled by Stan Kroenke — the billionaire who also owns the Los Angeles Rams, SoFi’s marquee occupant — alleged that the city’s billboard deal “siphons” money from its property. In its second lawsuit, Hollywood Park sought about $400 million from Inglewood that it said it was owed for public infrastructure upgrades and other improvements, arguing the city was required to reimburse those costs once certain tax revenue thresholds were met.

But the city countered that the development agreement cited by Hollywood Park was unenforceable because it was adopted through an initiative approved by voters — and not a legislative body, the far more common route. Now, Inglewood has prevailed. A Los Angeles Superior Court judge upheld, in a ruling dated Tuesday, the long-term agreement between the billboard company and the city, affirming that it did not violate the law.

In an interview with POLITICO, Inglewood Mayor James T. Butts sought to lower the temperature, extolling Hollywood Park as a “great partner,” adding that “there is no animosity between” the city and the property owner. FIRST IN POLITICO: TRACKED CHANGES — Gov. Gavin Newsom is coming out with a new AI policy project that he’s framing as America’s first public early warning system for mass job displacement.

The tool, shared first with our colleague Tyler Katzenberger ahead of its unveiling today, aims to measure AI’s labor impacts by examining whether unemployment rates rise after companies like OpenAI and Anthropic roll out new AI models.

It’s part of Newsom’s broader response to AI’s impacts on California’s labor force, which he laid out in an executive order last month. And it’s the latest example of the likely 2028 presidential contender using his final months in office to road-test policy ideas while honing his vision for overhauling America’s economy in the AI age.

“We’re shaping the future — and charting the course for the nation,” Newsom said in a statement. “As AI advances, we aren’t just watching from the sidelines; we’re reimagining how we prepare California through strong governance and innovative policy.”

How it works: The online dashboard, dubbed the “AI-Unemployment Tracker” and published as a partnership between the state Employment Development Department and the UC’s California Policy Lab, breaks down new unemployment claims each month to look for trends that point toward AI-fueled job losses.

So far, it hasn’t found evidence of a statewide surge in AI-driven layoffs. But a deeper look at the data shows that unemployment claims have risen for college graduates with professional degrees and in the San Francisco Bay Area in the last three years, suggesting that white-collar workers closest to the tech industry are most vulnerable to AI-related job cuts.

BRING ON THE BUDGET— Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel told reporters Wednesday that he anticipates the Legislature and governor’s office will announce a final deal on the state’s budget “within the next couple of days,” predicting the agreement could be in print by Friday for a final vote Monday.

As for things that still need to be ironed out, he said that the three parties are “all trying to row in the same direction,” but there are “reasonable disagreements” over health care-related spending amid massive cuts from the federal government through H.R. 1.

“How do we do a constellation of investments in health care and in the safety net that are best going to protect people, and just think through how those pieces fit together,” he said. — Nicole Norman

BRIN STRIKES BACK — A second billionaire-backed anti-tax measure qualified for the November ballot Wednesday, complicating an already fraught debate in California over whether to slap new taxes on the state’s ultra wealthy.

The latest initiative, backed by Google co-founder Sergey Brin and other industry titans, would prohibit the state from imposing taxes that apply retroactively and restrict new taxes on retirement holdings, individually-owned assets and personal savings.

It is the second anti-wealth tax measure to qualify. Both would undercut a proposed one-time, 5 percent tax on billionaire assets, which qualified for the ballot last week.

The first Brin-backed countermeasure, which would require audits of state spending and invalidate taxes that don’t comply with state spending limits, qualified on Tuesday. The new ballot qualification is the latest blow to wealth tax proponents. This month, San Francisco voters rejected the so-called “overpaid CEO tax,” a ballot measure that was also opposed by tech donors and introduced by labor leaders during the primary. — Marisa Guerra Echeverria

DEVELOPERS FLIP — The eleventh hour drama over a tax ballot measure deal took another twist Wednesday as the initiative’s funders threatened to fight — ratcheting up the pressure on proponent Jon Coupal, who’s renounced a negotiated compromise and said he’s still pursuing his initiative to make it harder to pass local taxes.

Developers who have pumped millions into qualifying the initiative said they’d assembled roughly $10 million to “advance the negotiated compromise as an alternative” to Coupal’s measure.

Will it be enough to compel Coupal to back down? We’ll know by the end of today. — Jeremy B. White
