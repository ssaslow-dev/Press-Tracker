‘Nothing about us without us!’ | Disability rights advocates, seniors send a message to the next governor amid annual threats to their healthcare

Disability rights advocates welcomed the decision to avoid major health care cuts but said they are disappointed that some hard-won protections were weakened.

Author: Becca Habegger
Published: 4:50 PM PDT July 2, 2026
Updated: 4:50 PM PDT July 2, 2026

SACRAMENTO, Calif. — Big cuts and changes to Medi-Cal and In-Home Supportive Services in earlier budget proposals this year were largely avoided in the final version, which lawmakers passed and the Governor signed on Monday.

Still—seniors, Californians with disabilities and chronic conditions, and those who support them - have had to come to the Capitol repeatedly in recent years to fight for funding and eligibility around their healthcare.

With a new Governor taking office next year, advocates have a message for state leaders.

“Nothing about us without us!” shouted Brittanie Hernandez-Wilson, leading others in a chant.

She is a member of a coalition called Long Term Supports and Services for All (LTSS4All), which gathered outside the Capitol Wednesday morning for a rally reacting to the final budget – and looking ahead.

“This is what communities refusing to be invisible looks like, and the Legislature heard us.” said Hernandez-Wilson, who is also lead California homecare organizer at Hand in Hand: The Domestic Employers Network. “The Legislature rejected every single proposed cut to In-Home Supportive Services.”

In his January budget draft, Governor Gavin Newsom proposed three changes to In-Home Supportive Services (IHSS): Cutting the IHSS Backup Provider System, ending a temporary care-stopgap practice when someone loses Medi-Cal eligibility, and shifting more costs to counties, which advocates worried would result in reduced hours of care someone can get.

All of these cuts were avoided in the final budget.

Not every accomplishment this legislative session, however, is seen as a win.

“Sure, yes, they’ve done better than the worst, but that’s not the same as doing right by us,” said Elizabette Guéçamburu, a disability rights advocate from Stanislaus County, who was born with a severe neuro-muscular disability and said she relies upon Medi-Cal and IHSS.

She points to something called the asset limit, also known as the asset test, which is a financial cap that seniors and Californians with disabilities must stay under in order to remain eligible for Medi-Cal and IHSS. The lower the cap, the harder to stay eligible.

It has fluctuated in recent years – and this latest budget dealt a blow to seniors and disabled Californians on Medi-Cal, though less of a blow than proposed earlier this year.

For decades, California’s asset test mirrored the federal resource limit for SSI (Supplemental Security Income) eligibility. That federal figure was last raised in 1989 to a cap of $2,000 for an individual, $3,000 for a couple – and has not been raised since then, not even for inflation.

What counts as one’s ‘assets’ includes how much money someone has in their checking and savings accounts at any given moment - though it does not include a retirement fund. It does not include someone’s primary vehicle but does count a secondary vehicle. Advocates say – the limit prevents people from saving for things like emergencies and medical expenses not covered by insurance and keeps families from having a second vehicle, which can impact employment and getting kids to school.

Advocates call the asset test an outdated limit that forces seniors and Californians with disabilities to live in a state of poverty just to keep their medical care. They point out the asset test is not applied to – and therefore is not a determinant of Medi-Cal eligibility – for people without disabilities under the age of 65.

The $2,000/$3,000 asset test was in place in California for years, and disability rights advocates fought it for years.

Then, Gov. Newsom agreed to raise the limit to $130,000 for an individual, $195,000 for a couple in 2022, as a transition to 2024, when the limit was erased completely.

But that win was short-lived. The move cost the government money, according to administration officials, who told state lawmakers last year the asset test’s elimination led to many more people enrolling in Medi-Cal than anticipated, which contributed to the state’s growing costs. Between 112,000 and 115,000 people newly enrolled, compared to early estimates of just 40,000, according to officials.

So in 2025, to save money, Newsom proposed fully reinstating the test to its $2,000/$3,000 level once again. Advocates and lawmakers pushed back on that, and a compromise was reached, with a return to the 2022 limit of $130,000/$195,000 starting in 2026.

Facing another looming budget deficit for Fiscal Year 2026-27, Gov. Newsom - in the May 2026 revision of his budget proposal – once again recommended a full return to the $2,000/$3,000 asset test, saying it would save the state an estimated $278.3 million in the 2026-27 budget and $495.6 million ongoing.

Advocates appealed to lawmakers to reject this proposal, which they did – in part.

The final budget landed on $21,000 for an individual, $31,000 for a couple, to become effective July 1, 2027 - or whenever after that date someone currently on Medi-Cal has their annual redetermination. The law also says the state will track, quarterly, "the number of Medi-Cal enrollees who lost eligibility due to the asset limit."

Guéçamburu calls the compromise “very bittersweet.”

“We’ve still lost here,” Guéçamburu said. “We’ve had to give up practically all of the gains we’ve made in the last two, three years, to get the asset limit completely eliminated.”

She said her services are now at risk because of the newly lowered asset limit, set to go into effect in a year, barring changes by lawmakers during 2027’s budget negotiations.

"A wheelchair van itself is – for a used van, $75,000, and new is $90,000. Let’s say you need to do a bathroom modification to make a bathroom accessible. You’re talking at least $15,000,” Guéçamburu said. “And these are disability-related expenses; this is not even talking about other stuff like a new washer and dryer. So how are we supposed to save for everyday disability-related expenses on only $21,000 in assets? It’s impossible—especially in California, with the cost of living here.”

This is the third time she has come to the Capitol in the last six weeks. She said disability rights advocates find themselves having to do this year after year, asking lawmakers not to cut their life-sustaining care.

“Repeated attacks on the programs that allow us to live and have freedom - are repeated attacks on us,” said Kate Laddish of Yolo County, who is president of the California In-Home Supportive Services Consumer Alliance.

Laddish said this advocacy comes at the cost of many people’s health. 

“And yet we cannot afford to stay silent. We have sustained injuries using the Capitol’s ramps, had flare-ups due to fatigue and so much more," Laddish said. "For others, coming to the Capitol is not even an option.”

The rally was held on July 1, which Laddish pointed out is the first day of Disability Pride Month.

Serving as emcee of Wednesday’s rally, she also thanked the many lawmakers who partnered with them to advocate for their healthcare, “for meeting with us, for hearing us and for a budget that protects our essential programs.”

She named Senate President pro Tem Monique Limón (D-Santa Barbara), Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas (D-Salinas), Senate Budget Chair John Laird (D-Monterey), Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), subcommittee leaders and members Sen. Caroline Menjivar (D-San Fernando Valley), Asm. Dawn Addis (D-Morro Bay), Asm. Dr. Corey Jackson (D-Moreno Valley), Asm. Mia Bonta (D-Oakland) and Asm. Pilar Schiavo (D-Santa Clarita) and Assembly Majority Leader Cecilia Aguiar-Curry (D-Woodland).

Her bipartisan list also included Asm. Jeff Gonzalez (R-Indio), who attended Wednesday’s rally and said this fight is personal; he has a son with severe disabilities.

“This asset limit is forced poverty,” Gonzalez said. “We cannot balance our budget on the backs of California's most vulnerable. Don’t be fooled, lowering the Medi-Cal asset limit to $21,000 tells seniors and Californians with disabilities they must spend nearly everything they've worked for before they can access the care they need. In California, $21,000 isn't even enough to purchase many reliable vehicles, yet we're telling families that's too much to have.”

Asm. Gonzalez said he plans on introducing legislation next year to completely do away with that asset limit.
