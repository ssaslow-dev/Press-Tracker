State funds awarded for museum expansion - Beverly Press & Park Labrea News
By Edwin Folven / October 28, 2021

State funds awarded for museum expansion

Survivor Eva Nathanson stressed the importance of educating future generations about the Holocaust during a ceremony that was also attended by state Assemblyman Richard Bloom, left, Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, center, and state Sen. Ben Allen, right, as well as members of the Holocaust Museum L.A.’s board. (photo by Edwin Folven)

The Holocaust Museum L.A. received a significant boost in its capital campaign when California Sen. Ben Allen and Assemblymen Richard Bloom and Jesse Gabriel awarded $2.5 million in state funding for the museum’s upcoming expansion project on Oct. 21.

Holocaust Museum L.A. is planning a transformational project that will create the Jona Goldrich Campus in Pan Pacific Park, at the site of the current museum. The new campus will feature outdoor reflective spaces; large galleries and classrooms; a theater for survivor talks, film screenings, concerts, conferences and public programs; a new pavilion that will house an authentic World War II-era train boxcar; and a dedicated theater for USC Shoah Foundation’s “Dimensions in Testimony” exhibit, which allows visitors to have a virtual conversation with a Holocaust survivor using holographic and voice recognition software.

The $2.5 million will be added to $6 million in state funding already committed for the project. Holocaust Museum L.A. has launched a $45-million capital campaign to fund the expansion, and has already raised $22 million, Holocaust Museum L.A. CEO Beth Kean said.

“Holocaust Museum L.A. is the only museum in California that focuses solely on the enormity of the Holocaust and provides free admittance for all students and California residents. For the last 60 years, we have remained steadfast in our mission to commemorate those who perished, honor those who survived, educate about the Holocaust and inspire a more dignified and humane world,” Kean added. “With a shrinking community of Holocaust survivors, it remains more vital than ever to keep their memories alive and to teach next generations to think critically about the lessons of the Holocaust and its social relevance.”

Kean said the number of visitors has increased 400% since the museum first opened in 2010 in Pan Pacific Park, and it currently welcomes 60,000 people per year, including 20,000 students. The museum’s education programs are key to ensuring the lessons of the Holocaust are not forgotten so that something similar never happens again, Kean said. The new Jona Goldrich Campus will enable the museum to increase capacity to 500,000 annual visitors by 2030, including 150,000 students.

Allen (D-Santa Monica) said it is imperative that students and young people learn the stories of the Holocaust, adding that once they become informed, they are less likely to engage in anti-Semitic behavior. The museum establishes an understanding that young people carry with them long after they visit, he added.

“That’s what this museum does, it opens hearts,” Allen said. “And that’s why we have worked so hard at the Jewish [Legislative] Caucus, together, to prioritize this place, to get the resources here so that we can continue to educate the next generation, not with a closed heart, but with an incredibly open heart.”

Gabriel (D-Woodland Hills), chair of the California Legislative Jewish Caucus, also stressed the importance of Holocaust education.

“The Jewish Caucus is proud that the state budget includes funding to expand and enhance the critical work done by Holocaust Museum L.A.,” Gabriel said. “In too many places, and especially for too many young people, the Holocaust is fading from view. At the same time, acts of anti-Semitism, and other forms of hatred and bigotry, are rising at an alarming rate. Education is a powerful tool, and one of the strongest antidotes to anti-Semitism, hatred and bigotry of all forms.”

Bloom (D-Santa Monica) said Holocaust Museum L.A. has a special meaning for the local Jewish community. Having been started by Holocaust survivors in the early 1960s, it symbolizes connectivity among generations.

“This museum is not just special in the way all museums are special, this museum is personal,” Bloom said. “Personal to me, personal to tens of thousands of people, probably hundreds of thousands of people who live in this district because there are so many people who were affected by the Holocaust. This is a place that is hallowed, is a place of importance, and it’s a great pleasure and indeed a privilege for me to help make this museum an even better place.”

The new campus is named after the late Goldrich, a Holocaust survivor who settled in Los Angeles and became a successful real estate developer and home builder. Goldrich was instrumental in supporting the museum over the years, and funded the Los Angeles Holocaust Monument adjacent to the museum in Pan Pacific Park. His daughters Andrea Goldrich Cayton and Melinda Goldrich are lead donors in the museum project along with the Stanley and Joyce Black Family Foundation.

Holocaust survivor Eva Nathanson, 80, who lost most of her family at a young age in Hungary, said the museum is irreplaceable. Nathanson frequently speaks to visiting students, and said she hopes the new campus sustains the museum’s mission in perpetuity.

“The reason I tell my story is because there is so much hatred all over, not just against us, there is so much genocide all over,” Nathanson said. “When I speak, I always let people know that I am not only speaking as a Jew and a Holocaust survivor, I am also speaking for all the other minorities who are being persecuted. It’s important that we do everything in our power to guard against hatred and bigotry. We must say never again.”
