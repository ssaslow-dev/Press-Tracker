LEGISLATURE clears big hurdle — JENNER follows TRUMP path — UBER critic WEIL to DOL — NEWSOM preserves parklets, to-go cocktails

By Jeremy B White; Carla Marinucci; Richard Tzul

THE BUZZ: The state Legislature cleared the first major bottleneck of 2021on Thursday, and it offered a preview of what the ultimate showdown will bring.

Lawmakers wrapped up a week of votes forced by a Friday deadline to get bills out of their first houses. These biannual floor vote marathons tend to be clarifying exercises, both winnowing the ranks of policy proposals and showing which bills will face an uphill fight after barely squeaking out. A few of the lessons we took away from the action:

— NO VOTE: Authors effectively conceded defeat by not putting up some high-profile measures for floor votes. That included a contentious bill tightening charter school regulations in the latest clash between charters and unions and a measure offering thousands of dollars per worker, or billions overall, in “hero pay” for health care workers.

— ENERGY JOB JOCKEYING: When legislation to ban fracking and separate oil wells from homes and schools perished in its first committee earlier this year, it was largely because of warnings about job losses amplified by the powerful State Building and Construction Trade Council of California. But, yesterday, the Trades were on the other side as Democrats joined Republicans in objecting to the Trades’ energy workforce standards bill. While Senate Bill 419 got out on a second vote, the surprisingly vigorous debate laid bare Democratic divisions and came after the Trades’ aggressive tactics have alienated some lawmakers.

— SPEAKING OF THE ENVIRONMENT: Efforts to bolster California’s already-ambitious suite of climate policies are often a battle. A measure compelling California to achieve carbon neutrality by 2045 barely squeaked out after a long vote, and a bill to bar online retailers from using single-use plastic packaging failed twice — once on reconsideration — as legislators continue to struggle on plastic waste. On the other hand, environmentalists prevailed over utilities as the Assembly overwhelmingly rejected a measure to reduce consumer incentives to go solar. If you missed it, POLITICO’s Colby Bermel has an informative explanation of the complex clash.

— TAX TIMIDITY: Gun control tends to be a lighter lift in California than in other states. But a bill to impose excise taxes on gun sales underscored that raising taxes spooks plenty of Democrats. In a debate that surreally unfolded at the same time as a South Bay shooting one block from the San Jose massacre, which members invoked to justify the bill, Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel conceded to colleagues that “the word ‘taxes’” is “radioactive in our politics right now.” AB 1223’s author, Assemblyman Marc Levine, urged members to get over having been “conditioned never to vote for that dreaded T-word, with risk of our office.” But Levine could not get enough moderate Democrats off the fence to secure the needed two-thirds, and the measure fell five votes short — a reminder that a Democratic giga-majority is no guarantee on taxes.
