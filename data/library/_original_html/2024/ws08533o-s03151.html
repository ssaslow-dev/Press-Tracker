California’s Third Climate Law Requires New Carbon Offset Disclosures

By Andy Marks and Tammy Whitehouse, Deloitte Executive Perspectives

In October 2023, Gov. Gavin Newsom signed AB-1305, Voluntary Carbon Market Disclosures, into law, requiring disclosures regarding voluntary carbon offsets (VCOs). As detailed in a Deloitte Heads Up report, the law is intended to combat efforts by some companies to “greenwash” climate-related emissions claims.

Two more laws enacted in California—the Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act (SB-253) and Greenhouse Gases: Climate-Related Financial Risk (SB-261)—establish the first industry-agnostic U.S. regulations that mandate corporate reporting of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and climate risks. Separate climate disclosures for public companies proposed by the Securities and Exchange Commission have not yet been finalized.

The carbon offset disclosure details mandated by Assembly Bill No. 1305 (AB-1305), could draw the attention of buyers and sellers of VCOs, says Eric Knachel, an Audit & Assurance partner and senior consultation partner with the Professional Practice Network at Deloitte & Touche LLP. “This reporting requirement is different from anything we’ve seen before and could potentially expand on the current offset disclosures that we typically see in voluntary ESG reporting,” he says.

Although AB-1305 does not specify when initial disclosures are required, it does have an effective date of January 1, 2024. The bill’s lead sponsor, Jesse Gabriel, wrote to the state assembly’s chief clerk that his intent was for first annual disclosures to be posted by Jan. 1, 2025, to provide affected entities “with sufficient time to align their business practices with the stated objectives” of the law before being subject to potential penalties. This uncertainty has brought attention to AB-1305 because it is not clear when companies affected by the law will be required to begin making these new disclosures.

“It is not expected that Mr. Gabriel’s letter will result in changes to the text of AB1305, but it may affect enforcement decisions,” Knachel says.

Carbon Offset Disclosures: Required Details

The new carbon offset disclosure requirement applies to U.S. and international entities that market or sell VCOs within California as well as entities that operate in California and make certain climate-related emissions claims—whether or not they use or purchase VCOs. Entities that are subject to the new law are required to post disclosures on their websites and update them at least annually to describe their carbon offset projects.

“In SB-253 and SB-261, we see California relying on existing standards and frameworks, namely the GHG Protocol and TCFD. However, AB-1305 goes beyond the current standards and frameworks, creating new disclosure requirements that we haven’t seen in the market before,” says Brenna Vanderloop, an Audit & Assurance partner at Deloitte & Touche LLP. “For both buyer and sellers, this law is likely going to require that additional data is gathered and disclosed,” she says, adding, “Companies should think though their processes to gather and report on this data, and in many cases buyers will need to work with sellers to ensure that the information needed for disclosure is obtained.”

Required details regarding carbon offset projects include the protocol used to estimate emissions reductions or removal benefits, the location and type of the project, and its timeline. Entities are also required to say whether the project meets any standards established by law or a nonprofit entity, whether there is an independent or third-party validation or verification of the offset, and the annual emissions reduced or carbon removed. Further required details focus on accountability measures and calculation methods.

With respect to VCOs that are subject to the law, affected entities are required to disclose the name of the business entity selling the offset and the offset registry or program. They are also required to disclose the offset project location and type, including whether the offsets purchased were derived from a carbon removal, an avoided emission, or a combination of both. Entities must also disclose the protocol used to estimate emissions reductions and whether there is independent third-party verification of company data and claims listed.

For entities whose climate-related emission claims are subject to disclosure, the law requires information to establish how claims such as carbon neutral, net zero emission, or similar terms are substantiated or accomplished, how progress toward the goal is measured, and whether it is independently verified.

Under the law, a VCO is defined as any product sold or marketed in the state that claims to be a GHG emissions offset, a voluntary emissions reduction, a retail offset, or similar terms that suggest a reduction in or prevention of GHG emissions. Climate-related emissions claims focus on claims regarding the achievement of net zero emissions and carbon neutrality. The law also applies to claims that a product does not add net carbon dioxide or GHG to the climate or has made significant reductions to carbon or GHG emissions.

Several terms in the law are not defined, such as what it means to operate in California, make claims within the state, market or sell VCOs in the state, or verification of the offset being claimed. The law also does not define what constitutes significant reductions. As an example, an entity that makes climate-related emission claims on the internet, such as on its website, and operates within California may meet the criteria for making claims within the state.

Voluntary Carbon Market Headwinds

The voluntary carbon market (VCM) could enable companies to offset carbon emissions using carbon credits, and it may facilitate investment in projects that can help mitigate climate change. The VCM is decentralized and largely unregulated, however, with many third-party organizations providing standards, verification, and rating services in the absence of authoritative rules.

Growth of the VCM as a way to help mitigate the effects of climate change has been constrained, primarily resulting from undermined trust in the authenticity of carbon credits. The VCM market has been criticized as lacking adequate transparency, with difficulty for prospective buyers to establish credibility of underlying projects.

“This rule is really focused on reducing greenwashing and addressing the concerns around the validity of emissions offsets,” observes Sarah Stiff, a manager in the Audit & Assurance Sustainability and ESG Services team at Deloitte & Touche LLP. “These incremental requirements may drive companies to take a deeper look at the quality of offsets being purchased and the availability of the data required to be disclosed.”

Preparing for Disclosure

With wording in AB-1305 that can be broadly interpreted, entities that may be affected by the law might consider consulting legal counsel for assistance in assessing the applicability of the law to their own facts and circumstances. Entities that operate in California and have made public climate-related emissions claims, including assertions about emissions reductions, carbon neutrality, or other similar claims, could be subject to the disclosure requirements. Entities that market or sell VCOs in California could also be affected.

“There is a lot of grey area in AB-1305, especially regarding the companies that will be in scope and the date at which disclosures are required,” remarks Knachel. “So, it’s important that company management talk with their legal advisors as soon as possible.”
