California Lawmakers Considering Ban on Foods With Synthetic Dyes in Schools

Dan McCue

SACRAMENTO — The California Senate appears poised to vote within the next few days on a bill that would bar public schools from offering foods containing six synthetic dyes to their students.

The bill, which has already passed the state Assembly, does not ban any particular food or products.

Instead, if it advances, it would prohibit the state’s public schools from serving foods containing the dyes Blue No. 1, Blue No. 2, Green No. 3, Red No. 40, Yellow No. 5 and Yellow. No. 6, as well as titanium dioxide, a coloring agent that is often used in sunscreens, cosmetics, paints, and plastics.

The latter chemical has been banned for use in foods in the European Union since 2022; similarly the EU requires products containing certain food dyes to carry a warning label about their potential harm to children.

The new rules would go into effect in California on Jan. 1.

The lawmaker behind the legislation, State Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, has said the additives have been linked to a myriad of health issues in children including Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder and other neurobehavioral problems, damaged immune systems and even the onset of cancer.

Gabriel based these assertions on a 2021 report published by the California Environmental Protection Agency which found that the levels for safe intake of synthetic food dyes currently endorsed by the federal Food and Drug Administration “are based on 35- to 70-year-old studies that were not designed to detect the types of behavioral effects that have been observed in children.”

The report went on to say “newer studies indicate that the [FDA guidelines] may not adequately protect children.”

In a press release earlier this year, the Encino Democrat said “California has a responsibility to protect our students from chemicals that harm children and that can interfere with their ability to learn.”

“As a lawmaker, a parent, and someone who struggled with ADHD, I find it unacceptable that we allow schools to serve foods with additives that are linked to cancer, hyperactivity and neurobehavioral harms,” Gabriel continued.

“This bill will empower schools to better protect the health and wellbeing of our kids and encourage manufacturers to stop using these dangerous additives,” he said.

Gabriel’s introduction of the bill in March followed the successful passage of another of his food safety related bills last year.

That bill, the California Food Safety Act, banned the use of four potentially harmful chemicals — potassium bromate, propylparaben, brominated vegetable oil and Red No. 3 — in any foods sold in the state.

After swift action in the Assembly, Gabriel’s bill cleared the California Senate Education Committee in June and advanced through the chamber’s Appropriations Committee last week.

“Many children rely on school meals as a source of their daily nutrition and calorie intake,” said Tasha Stoiber, Ph.D., a senior scientist with the Environmental Working Group, a team of scientists, policy experts, lawyers and data experts who work to reform chemical safety and agricultural laws.

“Kids deserve wholesome foods that don’t negatively impact their ability to learn, and parents deserve the confidence that the schools they’re sending their kids to aren’t serving them food that may harm them,” Stoiber said in a written statement.

The legislation has even received the endorsement of two Hollywood heavyweights, Academy Award-winning actor Morgan Freeman and Emmy-nominated producer Lori McCreary.

“When we found out our schools were serving our kids foods containing chemicals linked to serious health problems, we were shocked,” said the duo, who are partners in the Revelations Entertainment production company.

“If the European Union and countries around the world have banned or require products with these chemicals to have a warning label, we should not be serving them in our schools,” Freeman and McCreary said.
