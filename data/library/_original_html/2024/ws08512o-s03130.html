New committee drills down on Newsom administration's housing spending

Updated: 6:33 PM PDT Mar 11, 2024Editorial Standards ⓘAshley ZavalaPolitical DirectorSACRAMENTO, Calif. —

A new committee at the state capitol has been tasked with scrutinizing state spending, and how effectively Governor Gavin Newsom's administration is using taxpayer dollars on key programs.

The committee, Assembly Budget Subcommittee Number 7, spent Monday drilling down on the billions of dollars spent on California's housing crisis. For years, the state has grappled with growing demand and a lack of production. The issue faces even greater challenges as California faces a significant budget shortfall this upcoming year and years to come.

"With this looming housing cliff and ongoing crisis, we really need to course correct," said the committee's leader, Assemblyman Avelino Valencia, D-Anaheim.

Members of Newsom's administration told the committee that the $18.5 billion earmarked over the last five years has resulted in 57,000 new units of housing. Zachary Olmstead with the California Department of Housing and Community Development told lawmakers 22,000 of those units are finished, while the rest are either shovel-ready or under construction.

Newsom's administration has also spent the last several years converting hotels and motels into affordable housing, a program known as Project HomeKey. Administration officials told lawmakers the average cost to taxpayers for each of those units is between $250,000 to $300,000. They said traditional affordable housing costs the state between $400,000 to $750,000 per unit, with coastal areas being more expensive. The program and other homelessness funds are at the center of a state audit set to be released later this spring.

"When you ask the question of effectiveness, I think they've all been effective in the sense that we've all been able to administer them," Olmstead told the committee.

"I'm glad to hear you thinking they're effective, what I'm looking for here is relative effectiveness," replied Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, D-Encino. "I think what we’re trying to figure out in a time of tremendously scarce resources is how do we get more targeted resources and where do we get our most bang for our buck?"

While lawmakers have approved state laws recently to try to fast track projects, assemblymembers noted bond money from previously approved ballot measures for affordable housing projects is running out, as the state faces a budget shortfall of up to $73 billion.

"We’ve done all this streamlining, we’ve done all this work and now are about to be out of money for affordable housing, is now the time to pull back on that?" said Assemblymember Buffy Wicks, D-Oakland.

Expert witnesses who testified said in order to make the programs more efficient, the state needs to release awarded project funds faster. They said one potential change to state law that could help would make new affordable housing projects "ballot proof" to prevent residents in typically wealthier areas from constantly fighting the construction.

The hearing comes as the vote count on Gov. Gavin Newsom's Proposition 1 remains too close to call. Among many things, the $6.4 billion bond initiative would help build shelter units and mental health campuses for people who are unhoused and severely mentally ill.
