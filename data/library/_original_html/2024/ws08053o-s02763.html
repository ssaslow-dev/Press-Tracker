Politics quickly seeps into kickoff of California legislative session

In addition to starting the regular session, Golden State lawmakers also set the stage for a special session to focus on Trump-proofing California.

Alan Riquelmy / December 2, 2024Link copied

SACRAMENTO, Calif. (CN) — More Republicans make up the California Legislature after November’s election, a change that gives the state’s superminority some optimism.

The new numbers don’t change the balance of power. Democrats still run the table at the Capitol, maintaining a two-thirds supermajority in both legislative chambers.

But Republicans see hope in gaining a seat in the state Senate and two in the Assembly.

Newly installed state Senator Steven Choi, an Orange County Republican, brings the number of Republicans in that 40-member chamber to 10. He defeated Democratic state Senator Josh Newman.

Two Republicans flipped seats in the 80-chamber Assembly: Jeff Gonzalez, who took the seat of retiring Democratic Assemblymember Eduardo Garcia; and Leticia Castillo, taking the seat of Democratic Assemblymember Sabrina Cervantes, who successfully ran for state Senate. Republicans now have almost 20 members in that chamber.

“I think it means Californians are starting to shift and they’re voting for Republicans,” said Assemblymember James Gallagher, a Yuba City Republican and minority leader, in an interview Monday. “Republicans have real answers for the issues.”

The Legislature convened the 2025-26 legislative session on Monday for the first time, focusing mostly on organization. Members took their oaths of office, voted in their chambers’ respective leaders and passed operational rules.

Politics quickly became part of the process.

State senators returned President Pro Tempore Mike McGuire, a North Coast Democrat, to his leadership post, while the Assembly returned Assemblymember Robert Rivas, a Salinas Democrat, to his role as speaker. Both houses followed those moves by passing rules for the upcoming regular and special sessions.

Governor Gavin Newsom called the special session days after Donald Trump won the presidency, saying the state needed to safeguard its civil rights protections, reproductive freedom and immigrants. Democrats intend to set aside funds to both legally defend California laws and fight against federal moves they oppose.

The special session drew some rebukes Monday from Senate Republicans, who opposed setting money aside for litigation against the Trump administration.

“We do not want to pass rules into place for something that shouldn’t exist,” said state Senator Kelly Seyarto, a Murrieta Republican.

State Senator Scott Wiener, a San Francisco Democrat, said the special session existed to ensure Trump follows the law. He argued it’s necessary to stop the Trump administration from, for example, using the military to deport people living in California.

In the Assembly, the passage of rules for the special session moved without interruption. However, Democrats set aside two procedural maneuvers from Republicans.

Assemblymember Bill Essayli, a Corona Republican, failed in his effort to pass a minority bill of rights. It would have, among other things, ensured every bill receives a hearing at the author’s request and that all members get five minutes to speak on the floor without interruption.

Essayli raged on the floor on the last night of the previous session in August, minutes before the Assembly was forced to adjourn at midnight. Cut off from speaking after being accused of trying to delay the process, Essayli called it “a dictatorship” and left the chambers.

The Assembly on Monday set aside his resolution in a 57-to-19 vote.

The chamber also declined a motion Monday from Assemblymember Carl DeMaio, a San Diego Republican, who sought to recess for an hour to give members time to read the body’s proposed rules.

While Monday set the stage for the upcoming session set to begin in January, lawmakers already are introducing bills.

State Senator and Brian Jones, a San Diego Republican and the minority leader, introduced a bill that would repeal the recent action by the California Air Resources Board that detractors say will increase the price of gas at the pump.

“Californians already pay the highest gas prices in the nation, and Gavin Newsom’s political agenda is about to make them even higher,” Jones said in a statement.

Wiener introduced a bill for the special session that includes $25 million in funding for the state Department of Justice, with another $10 million for county counsels and city attorneys. An additional $25 million is earmarked for legal services in local communities.

Trump’s election as president has spurred Democrats into action. On Monday, Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, an Encino Democrat and chair of his chamber’s Budget Committee, said in a release that he’d introduce two bills during the special session.

The first would allocate $25 million for possible litigation. The other bill would immediately designate $500,000 for case preparation.

Gabriel wants both bills passed into law by Jan. 20, the day Trump will become president.

“We will work with the incoming administration and we want President Trump to succeed in serving all Americans,” Newsom said in a statement. “But when there is overreach, when lives are threatened, when rights and freedoms and targeted, we will take action.”

Gallagher called the special session “tone deaf,” saying lawmakers should instead focus on issues important to Californians like public safety and the cost of living.

“In fact, we should be working with Donald Trump,” he added, pointing to issues like wildfire prevention.
