California throws in the towel

With help from Blanca Begert, Camille von Kaenel and Mike Lee

SOME WAIVERS LEFT BEHIND: California officials were hoping to have eight waivers in hand to enforce clean air rules before President-elect Donald Trump takes office. Instead, two of the biggest rules failed to cross the finish line.

The California Air Resources Board told President Joe Biden’s EPA on Monday that it was withdrawing requests for permission to enforce stricter-than-federal rules aimed at phasing out diesel burning trucks and trains — a whole week before Trump’s inauguration.

The agency framed the move as a way to insulate the rules from a hostile incoming administration poised to dismantle them.

“While we are disappointed that U.S. EPA was unable to act on all the requests in time, withdrawal is an important step given the uncertainty presented by the incoming administration,” CARB Chair Liane Randolph said in a statement today.

But the reality remains that California has lost the ability to implement a big chunk of its climate and clean air agenda — and to lead other states.

“I’m disappointed, to be honest,” said Ann Carlson, former head of the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration under Biden.

California had been waiting on the Biden administration to grant permission for it to implement eight rules, covering everything from passenger cars to lawn equipment. It ultimately got six of them.

The rules withdrawn Monday — Advanced Clean Fleets and the In-Use Locomotive Regulation — were the last two pending in front of EPA, which under the Clean Air Act has to sign off on California’s pollution rules that go further than the feds’.

They were big ones, as we’ve said: The American Lung Association estimated in a February 2024 report that ACF and the locomotive rule could provide more than $58 billion in health benefits like reduced heart attacks and asthma complications by 2050 — more than four times the impact of the car mandate.

No other states have signed up to follow California’s lead on either of the rules, as they have for the state’s tailpipe emissions rules and zero-emission sales targets. But the fleet rule, which would require all new commercial truck sales to be zero-emission by 2036, carried particular clout.

“California needs it to meet air pollution standards that are set by the federal government, and it’s important as part of a long-range climate strategy where California really is the leader, but other states follow,” Carlson said. “And not just other states, but as technology advances and becomes cheaper, that technology diffuses around the world, and that’s a really important rule for California to play.”

It’s not entirely clear whether CARB withdrew its requests because EPA was running out of time (the waivers needed to be issued this week to be published in the Federal Register by Jan. 20) or because EPA wasn’t going to approve them.

Given that they were already under heavy legal fire from industry and red-state AGs, it might have been the latter.

“Of all the waivers that were pushed out in the last couple of weeks, these were the ones that were the most controversial in terms of legal authority,” said former CARB Chair Mary Nichols. “Therefore, rather than test them in a hostile administration and likely hostile court, strategically, I think EPA was being helpful to California in this regard.”

Gov. Gavin Newsom, at least for now, isn’t making a big deal out of the loss, even though he made the waivers a top priority in recent months, including on a trip to Washington shortly after the election where he lobbied EPA officials to approve the full suite. A planned return trip was nixed last week as wildfires burned in Los Angeles.

Newsom spokesperson Daniel Villaseñor said in a statement Tuesday that the governor “is 100% focused on the Los Angeles fires.”

“We’re grateful to the Biden Administration for approving our six requests and we’ll continue to fight for clean air and water for all,” he said. — AN

FIRE ON ALL SIDES: LA’s wildfire aid is splitting along partisan lines on both sides, with Democrats and Republicans both attacking each other for proposing to tie it to other policies.

Democrats in Washington immediately rejected a tentative pitch by House Republicans to attach wildfire aid to a federal debt limit increase, as Nicholas Wu and Meredith Lee Hill report. GOP lawmakers had floated the idea in meetings over the weekend. Speaker Mike Johnson also said he’s interested in putting “conditions” on federal disaster aid to California, though he didn’t cite any specific policies.

“That’s just one of the most cruel and ignorant things you could possibly say, especially in this moment,” retorted California Democratic Rep. Jared Huffman. “You don’t condition it. We didn’t do that for Louisiana; we didn’t do that for Florida. I’m hoping that that was just a dumb thing that he said, which on reflection won’t go any further.”

Meanwhile in Sacramento, state Sen. Scott Wiener told reporters Monday night he would amend $2.5 billion in state spending on the Los Angeles wildfires into the same bill containing $50 million for legal defenses against Trump, causing an immediate fracas.

Assembly Minority Leader James Gallagher, who said Monday he was open to certain conditions on federal disaster aid to California to improve recovery and preparedness, immediately hit back on Wiener’s suggestion. Senate Minority Leader Brian Jones told Playbook’s Lindsey Holden the move was “insulting to those fire victims.”

Newsom was also forced to weigh in, writing on X on Tuesday, “I am not conditioning disaster aid on any other priorities.” The Senate canceled a scheduled special session budget hearing Tuesday, and neither Wiener, the Senate budget chair, nor Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, the Assembly budget chair, had introduced the wildfire spending bills as of Tuesday evening. — CvK, LH

LA TRIP: Johnson said he thinks that Trump will be visiting the site of the wildfire destruction in Southern California, POLITICO’s Mia McCarthy reports. He didn’t specify expected timing. Newsom and the congressional Democrats who represent parts of Los Angeles hit by the wildfires have sent Trump letters inviting him to visit the disaster area. A Newsom spokesperson said the governor hadn’t heard back and wasn’t sure if Newsom and Trump would meet. — CvK

TUNNEL MARCHES ON: The main water agency in the southern part of the Bay Area voted today to send $9.6 million towards the next round of planning and permitting of the Delta Conveyance Project, a version of which has been floating around — first as a canal, then a pair of tunnels — for more than half a century.

Santa Clara Valley Water’s contribution represents roughly 3 percent of the cost for this round. It was one of the last remaining large water agencies that would benefit from the project to vote on the latest round of funding. Agencies have yet to vote on the full construction costs, which could total $20 billion.

The project would route more water to Southern California through a single 45-mile-long tunnel under the crumbling Sacramento-San Joaquin River Delta. Environmental groups and Northern California Democrats oppose it because it could harm the Delta’s ecosystem, but Newsom has called it a key climate adaptation project as the state stares down an expected 10 percent reduction in water supplies by 2040. — CvK
