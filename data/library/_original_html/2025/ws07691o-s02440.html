How California Dems bluffed their way into a gerrymandering showdown

THEY DREW THE MAP — Before Gov. Gavin Newsom and California Democrats got serious about their plan to gerrymander the state’s congressional map, they hoped to bluff Texas out of a standoff.

But Texas Republicans and President Donald Trump only doubled down. And, in a matter of weeks, Golden State leaders followed suit, thrusting themselves into the center of a national fight to shape the outcome of the 2026 midterms and the remaining years of Trump’s presidency.

POLITICO’s Melanie Mason, Jeremy B. White, Shia Kapos, Aaron Pellish and Dustin released an in-depth report over the weekend sharing closely-guarded details of the process, including how the map was drawn and who led the charge. Read it here

They spoke with nearly 50 people involved with the California effort, including lawmakers, political operatives, staffers and redistricting wonks. Many were granted anonymity to share details of private deliberations, which spanned multiple states and levels of government. Five takeaways from their exclusive reporting:

Newsom had rejected the idea before: Several years before the current redistricting standoff began, then-Democratic National Committee chair Jaime Harrison had broached with Newsom an idea of pausing the state’s independent redistricting commission. His worry was that Democrats were sacrificing political advantage, while Republican-led legislatures were continuing to draw partisan gerrymanders. At the time, Newsom demurred, telling Harrison he did not think Californians would want to undermine the state’s independent commission they voted to put in place. But by this summer, that had changed entirely.

Newsom, in an interview with Melanie, noted that Texas Gov. Greg Abbott initially seemed hesitant about mid-decade redistricting until Trump weighed in. “Even he was unsure, because he knows deep down, as they all do, it’s the wrong thing to do,” Newsom said. “(Abbott) has to do it because, again, Donald Trump and the Republican Party is a T-Rex: You either mate with him or he devours you.”

The congressional delegation was caught by surprise: By late June, Newsom and his top aides were batting around options of how California could respond to Texas’ effort to draw five new red House seats. But his threat to respond to Texas, which came after Trump demanded a map with five new seats, came as a surprise to California’s Democratic delegation in Congress. Initially, they thought it was just chest thumping. Members of the caucus were hardly enthused at the prospect of rejiggering their district maps, a process that is notoriously sharp-elbowed in the best of circumstances.

Pelosi gave an early blessing: While some members of the caucus remained skeptical, the delegation held a series of meetings in late July. Former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi was among the party leaders who threw their muscle behind the idea. Her message to her California colleagues was firm: “California was not going to unilaterally disarm” if Texas crossed what she saw as a line in the sand, said a person familiar with Pelosi’s involvement.

Dems launched parallel efforts: By late July, Newsom’s political team was building a campaign on the fly, to sell the effort to voters. The crafting of the new maps began in earnest in a separate, parallel effort led by Democratic members of Congress. Redistricting consultant Paul Mitchell had been a consultant for the delegation during past redistricting cycles. He had an especially long-standing relationship with Rep. Zoe Lofgren, who called him with a task: Find five more Democratic seats to offset the Texas GOP gains.

Leaders used Zoom sessions to build consensus: Lofgren, chair of the California Democratic caucus; Rep. Pete Aguilar, the third-ranking Democrat in the House; and Mitchell held 30-minute Zoom meetings with each delegation member, talking through the proposed changes and soliciting feedback. Some members in safe blue seats were set to take in substantial numbers of conservative voters, and others were not thrilled about losing territory they had long represented. But none of those lawmakers aired their grievances publicly, enabling the delegation to project a united front.

“Once the governor signaled very clearly that he was serious, we all got serious about it,” said one California representative, who was granted anonymity. “I don’t think this took a lot of convincing, honestly. People got it.”

Aguilar, Lofgren, Pelosi and Mitchell later held similar sessions with the Democratic Caucus in the Legislature — and the maps were officially submitted to the Legislature by the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee. From there, Democratic leadership and staff continued to make small adjustments to the proposed boundaries.

MORE ON REDISTRICTING

YOU’VE GOT MAIL — Opponents of Prop 50 have already hit California voters with multiple mailers objecting to the Legislature’s redistricting proposal.

The early and swift barrage of opposition ads comes as Charles Munger Jr., a Palo Alto physicist and architect of California’s independent redistricting commission, contributed $10 million to the opposition effort.

As Jeremy writes, the campaign over California’s proposed gerrymandered map has already drawn more than $20 million in contributions from both sides of the issue as powerful and wealthy players dig in for a bruising three-month sprint until Nov. 4. Unlike candidate races, ballot initiatives do not have contribution limits, allowing supporters to instantly infuse millions of dollars.

Millions more are likely on their way. Former House Speaker Kevin McCarthy and former California Republican Party Chair Jessica Millan Patterson are spearheading a committee to defeat the measure, and former Gov. Arnold Schwarzenegger has promised to “terminate” it.

Democratic mainstays have also thrown down. The House Majority PAC, Newsom and the powerhouse union group SEIU California have already reported donating $6 million. Wealthy individuals like Bill Bloomfield and Andrew Hauptman have sent another $1.3 million.

WHERE NEXT? Now that Texas and California have moved forward, the redistricting war is officially on. The nation’s two largest states fired the opening salvo in what is likely to become an intense and protracted redistricting campaign by both parties to grasp power in Washington.

As POLITICO’s Liz Crampton, Nick Reisman and Dustin write, now other red and blue state governors face pressure to follow their lead and aggressively gerrymander their congressional maps.

Republicans hold a clear advantage: The GOP is poised to move forward with redistricting in Florida, Ohio, Missouri and Indiana, which could yield at least half a dozen more seats. Democrats, meanwhile, have struggled to get gerrymandering efforts moving in blue states beyond California, though leaders in New York, Illinois and Maryland say they are weighing options.

“Right now, these other states need to step up,” California Rep. Robert Garcia told Playbook. “I know it’s hard, I know it’s complicated … But, if you’re a blue state governor, the time is now to step up and get it done.”

CAMPAIGN YEAR(S)

REDISTRICTING RIPPLES — The proposed redrawing of the state’s congressional boundaries is having downstream effects across California as candidates assess their prospects in an evolving political landscape. Here’s an example from San Diego County: Democrat Suzanne Till, a member of the board of directors of the Padre Dam Municipal Water District in Santee, is withdrawing from the race to take on Rep. Darrell Issa. The new lines for the 48th Congressional District would make it much friendlier territory for Democrats, but Till says she wants to use her expertise in water issues to run for the 40th state Senate seat now held by the termed-out Minority Leader Brian Jones.

That leaves a handful of registered candidates for the seat held by Issa. They include Ammar Campa-Najjar, who filed papers on Friday for a rematch against Issa. Also in the race is Curtis Morrison, an immigration attorney in Fallbrook. He filed to run as a Democrat under the existing, heavily Republican district lines and says he’s staying in the race even if voters don’t approve new borders that stretch all the way up to Palm Springs. “I don’t know if I would have gotten in if it wasn’t hard,” he said. “I like challenges.” — Ben Fox

HOUSING

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: HOUSE DIVIDED — Just when it looked like the Legislature’s housing wars had subsided for the year, a bill that would allow taller apartment and condo buildings near transit stops is polarizing elected officials in Los Angeles.

Opposition to SB 79, by state Sen. Scott Wiener, has amplified in recent days after LA Mayor Karen Bass and the City Council approved a resolution objecting to the bill unless it’s amended to completely exclude cities like LA that have housing plans that have been approved by the state.

But Wiener expects to unveil two prominent endorsements today as he tries to shore up support to get the bill over the finish line at the state Capitol.

The new backers are Oakland Mayor Barbara Lee and the Los Angeles Area Chamber of Commerce, as Wiener’s office exclusively told Playbook. While business groups have rallied behind the bill, the nod from Lee, a well-known progressive Democrat, is sure to draw attention.

Opposition in LA has been stoked by former reality TV star Spencer Pratt. He claims the bill could lead to denser housing in areas ravaged by the January wildfires, which would “further endanger lives” during evacuations.

Wiener said that’s untrue: SB 79 would only allow for denser housing construction in areas with major transit stops, including within a half-mile of rail stations and bus-rapid-transit hubs. The Palisades, an affluent coastal community, has neither transit system.

“The amount of misinformation being spread in LA around SB79 is extraordinary,” Wiener told Playbook.

Pratt’s videos, which have been promoted by conservative media outlets, have increased pressure on Bass and LA officials. The resolution Bass signed last week, however, takes a different approach, extolling the benefits of local government control.

Meanwhile, the California Democratic Party voted over the weekend to support SB 79. And the issue seeped into the race for governor, as former Rep. Katie Porter, the Democratic front-runner, chided developer Rick Caruso, who has been considering a run for governor or mayor, for opposing the bill.

“Workers, students, seniors and other Californians will all benefit from more housing — especially near transit,” Porter wrote in an X post on Sunday, subtweeting a Caruso post.

TOP TALKERS

MENENDEZ SAGA TWIST — The state parole board denied Erik and Lyle Menendez’s petitions seeking early release, assuring that the brothers will remain in prison for the 1989 murders of their parents. But, as the Los Angeles Times reports, the process was thrown into chaos after a recording of Erik’s hearing was inadvertently released to the media. Emotional relatives of the brothers sharply criticized the parole board over the incident, but the board chose to keep both behind bars.

The brothers can try for parole again in three years. Newsom, meanwhile, has been weighing how to respond to a clemency request for the brothers, who allege they were sexually abused by their parents.

TRUMP’S SF THREAT — After deploying the military to LA and Washington, D.C., Trump has vowed that San Francisco and other deep-blue cities will be next as he cracks down on crime. “We’ll clean that one up, too,” Trump said of the City by the Bay — where crime has been on a steady decline since the fall of 2023.

Until now, Trump has been less focused on San Francisco than other major Democratic-led cities like LA and Chicago (though he did seek to turn Alcatraz back into a federal prison and to dissolve the Presidio). As The New York Times writes, several local leaders balked at Trump’s recent threat to send in troops. “It’s a lot of bluster and insanity,” said Nancy Tung, chair of the county Democratic Party. “If he wants to try to roll a tank down Lombard Street, go ahead. We’re ready.”

Mayor Daniel Lurie, who has refused to say Trump’s name for months, was the exception. He avoided directly addressing the president’s threat in a statement: “My administration has made safe and clean streets our top priority, and the results are clear: crime is at its lowest point in decades, visitors are coming back, and San Francisco is on the rise.”
