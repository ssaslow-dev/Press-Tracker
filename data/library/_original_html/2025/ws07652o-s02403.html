Can Calderon ride crypto to contention?

CRYPTO KING — Ian Calderon is making one of the most direct plays for crypto support ever in a California political campaign.

It’s unclear how much he can pull out of them, or from whom. But he name-checked Bitcoin in his first campaign ad and talked with prospective industry donors before announcing his long-shot gubernatorial campaign Tuesday. Then, asked what donor base he might lean on in the race, he named California’s deep-pocketed cryptocurrency world and emphasized his work on crypto legislation when he was in the state Legislature.

“I have a real, long history with the digital asset community,” he told Playbook on Wednesday. “I believe that’s a huge base in California, and nobody else is better positioned than me to talk to them, because I understand it, and I know what it’s like, and it’s something that is important to me.”

BIG PICTURE: Calderon has little cash on hand and is lesser known across the state than some of his competitors. And there’s no guarantee that industry players will come on board for him. But if he can generate a surge of crypto money, he could, at a minimum, become a factor. And it’s his clearest path.

Calderon, the millennial former Assembly majority leader, has taken on crypto and other tech clients since leaving the Legislature in 2020 and starting a consulting business. He told Playbook he had conversations with industry players ahead of his entry into the race, though he didn’t name them.

The Porter factor … The last time we saw crypto play heavily in a California statewide race, it was with them carpetbombing Katie Porter in last year’s Senate race.

But it remains to be seen if this is a longstanding grudge or a one-off. A New Yorker piece last year reported that the crypto-backed Fairshake super PAC was animated more in the Senate race by making a show of their political muscle, not necessarily animus against Porter. Since then, Porter has seemingly mended at least one fence in the industry, pulling maximum donations from Ripple co-founder Chris Larsen and his wife. She has also been reaching out to the industry, where her calls have been answered, according to a person in the industry familiar with the outreach who was granted anonymity to discuss private conversations.

But, but, but … There’s a widespread assumption among some political insiders that crypto still has it out for Porter.

A strategist working for one of Calderon’s Democratic opponents, who was granted anonymity to discuss fundraising strategy, said “the big question” is “will crypto come in and spend $10 million more in the race against her? … Will they just go against a candidate, or seek to lift another candidate up?”

Calderon said he hasn’t talked with representatives for Fairshake, but said his “hope is that if they do decide to play in California, that they’re not doing it in opposition to someone, that they do it in support of someone.”

Don’t take it to the bank yet … Whether major tech players boost Calderon could hinge on whether they see him as viable.

He doesn’t have the experience or name identification of having run before for statewide office, unlike Porter, former Attorney General Xavier Becerra, former gubernatorial candidate Antonio Villaraigosa (who last year joined the trading platform Coinbase’s global advisory council), former Controller Betty Yee and state Superintendent Tony Thurmond. Calderon also is starting with just a reported $68,000 on hand between his accounts, far less than the millions that polling front-runners reported having at the most recent disclosure deadline.

“He’s one of the longer shots, for sure. Obviously, he’s getting in a little bit late,” said former Assembly Speaker Anthony Rendon, who considers Calderon a friend and had him on his leadership team in the Capitol. “You start going down the line, you have people who’ve been on the statewide ballot. It’s all tied to name ID. Turning around name ID is the hardest thing you can possibly do.”

CAMPAIGN YEAR(S)

GOOGLE FAMOUS — Another long-shot contender to officially enter the race: former software engineer Thunder Parley, who became known for in-house food reviews at Google, where he worked for 18 years. He was laid off in 2024, when Google made several rounds of cuts as it redirected resources toward AI.

Parley kicked off his campaign Wednesday and told our own Christine Mui in his first interview since the launch that he’s pitching himself as a “professional problem solver,” centering his candidacy around affordability.

Parley fits a similar mold of politician to San Jose’s Matt Mahan and San Francisco’s Daniel Lurie: moderates concerned with reducing street homelessness, boosting public safety and encouraging new business.

Like Mahan, he knocked the current governor’s Trump-parodying social media tactics, telling the state to focus less on “petty, combative politics” and questioning, “at the end of the day, is it doing anything for my bills?”

The role tech plays in Parley’s vision is more supportive than central.

“My position isn’t to be ‘I’m bringing all of tech to Sacramento,’” he said. “I’m going to be talking to lots of people. Obviously, in tech, where I have connections, but my message is about attacking the affordability crisis, and not everybody in tech feels it the same way.”

KAMALA WATCH

TV TAKEDOWN — Kamala Harris on Wednesday ripped entertainment executives for their “level of capitulation” in Jimmy Kimmel’s suspension following his comments about Charlie Kirk’s assassination, our colleague Nick Reisman reports.

“I have always believed that when push comes to shove the titans of industry would at some level be the guardians of our democracy,” the former vice president said. “Yet we have seen a meal at the foot of a tyrant. We’ve seen fear of retribution or maybe the need to flatter.”

She credited people for acting with “their pocketbooks” — alluding to canceled Disney streaming subscriptions in the wake of Kimmel being taken off the air, before his return on Tuesday. She slammed company leaders for buckling under pressure from federal regulators.

“Yielding — what, because they want a merger approved? Because they want to avoid an investigation?” Harris said. “Because they’re worried about losing their yacht or their house in the Hamptons?”

IN THE COURTS

VISA CHECK — California is weighing its legal options after President Donald Trump slapped a new $100,000 fee on certain high-skilled worker visas often used by the state’s tech industry, Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office confirmed Wednesday.

“President Trump continues to push forward an anti-immigrant agenda,” Bonta spokesperson Elissa Perez said in a statement to POLITICO. “This latest move will harm our businesses and make our country less competitive in science, technology, engineering, and medicine — at a time when we can’t afford to fall behind. The California Department of Justice is closely monitoring the implementation of this EO and its effects, which will determine any potential next steps.”

Bonta’s review comes after the president’s surprise announcement Friday left tech companies scrambling to assess the impact. Our Tyler Katzenberger, Christine Mui and Juliann Ventura have more.

ON THE HILL

GIMME SHELTER — Oakland Rep. Lateefah Simon and Texas Rep. Al Green are pushing the Trump administration to respond to complaints that it has rolled back enforcement of fair housing laws. This week, the Democratic lawmakers led 50 of their colleagues in signing onto a letter scolding Housing and Urban Development Secretary Scott Turner.

“These actions will lead to devastating outcomes for our nation’s most vulnerable populations, including veterans, seniors, people with disabilities, domestic violence survivors, families with children, people of color and more,” the lawmakers wrote.

They added, “We demand that HUD reverse course and ensure fair housing opportunity for all.”

The letter from Simon and Green follows a New York Times report that detailed instances where HUD officials have dismissed aspects of the Fair Housing Act — a decades-old law that prohibits discrimination by landlords — as ideologically motivated or rooted in DEI culture.

Spokesperson Kasey Lovett dismissed the allegations in a statement, arguing any notion that HUD is trying to blunt enforcement is “patently false.” She added, “The department’s actions will be to enforce compliance with all laws and real harms, not those based on ideology or illegal forms of discrimination.”

TAKING A PASS — Democratic Rep. Adam Gray has pointedly said he opposes Republicans’ resolution to keep the federal government funded because it doesn’t do enough to protect consumers from health insurance premium spikes.

But Gray was absent when the resolution came up for a vote in the House last week. Now, Republicans are dunking on him for skipping the vote.

The NRCC, which is angling to flip his Central Valley seat red in 2026, accused Gray of trying to “play both sides” by not casting a vote (though the GOP’s funding resolution has since stalled in the Senate).

“Out of touch Adam Gray put politics over people, risking veterans’ healthcare, delaying paychecks to our servicemembers and slashing resources for law enforcement,” said NRCC spokesperson Christian Martinez.

Ben Rodriguez, Gray’s chief of staff, said he abstained on the funding vote because he favors a compromise that would reverse Republicans’ cuts to federal health care programs and permanently extend tax credits for insurance premiums.

“Congressman Gray doesn’t support a government shutdown,” Rodriguez said in an email. “He remains open to a bipartisan solution.”
