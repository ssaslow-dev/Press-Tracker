How lawmakers are riding the Prop 50 bandwagon

By JEREMY B. WHITE and WILL MCCARTHY

THE PROP 50 UNDERSTUDIES — Gov. Gavin Newsom may be the main character of the campaign to pass Proposition 50, but behind him is a growing cast of supporting actors jostling for their moment on stage. Nearly 40 different yes committees have emerged or rebranded to join Newsom’s fight, with a slew of Democratic state lawmakers — including Speaker Robert Rivas — using their ballot committees to run Yes on 50 ads putting them in the spotlight before constituents.

This isn’t quite the same as billionaire donor Tom Steyer casting himself in primetime Prop 50 spots or mall magnate and perennial rumor-mill supplier Rick Caruso plastering his face on Prop 50 mailers. While team Caruso gave team Newsom a heads up, both spurred cynical reactions from other Democrats who accused them of belatedly riding the initiative bandwagon toward their own potential candidacies for governor or mayor/governor, respectively.

Incumbent state lawmakers coordinated with the Newsom mothership and are hewing to its messaging.

“Love the support,” senior adviser Jim DeBoo said in a text message. “They actually voted for this. They took a risk when this didn’t look easy so if they want to get behind their vote we are all for it. And someone like Rivas who has name ID in his district it’s smart and helpful.”

But that doesn’t make the ad campaigns any less opportunistic. Through ballot measure committees, politicians can raise unlimited funds from Sacramento interest groups to raise their profiles between election cycles. A few of the lawmakers now putting committees to work are rumored to have an eye on political promotions.

That includes Rivas, whose Central Coast district overlaps with that of Rep. Zoe Lofgren. The speaker last week began showing his constituents a television ad in which he characterizes Prop 50 as a way for Californians to push back on Trump’s “tyrant”-like approach to immigration enforcement. Rivas spokeswoman Elizabeth Ashford said a future House run was “not a scenario we are thinking about,” noting Rivas had endorsed his longtime ally Lofgren for re-election and insisting Rivas was “100% focused on being speaker.”

Ashford said Rivas’ ad would bolster the broader Yes on 50 turnout push because Rivas is “uniquely positioned” to talk to his rural district, where ICE raids have taken a heavy toll on farmworkers like Rivas’ late grandfather, whom he mentions in the spot. Gonzalez had a similar argument for casting himself in an ad that links to a website depicting a clash between him and Trump, saying he was a “validator” for his Los Angeles constituents.

“There’s a familiarity in my district,” Gonzalez said.

There are many ways for lawmakers to lend their committees to the effort. Senate President Pro Tem Mike McGuire sent a six-figure check from his insurance commissioner to Yes on 50, which would birth a new House district in McGuire’s part of the state. State Sen. Tim Grayson and Assemblymembers Jesse Gabriel and Blanca Pacheco have been running pro-Prop 50 ads in which they appear. Republican Assembly member Carl DeMaio has revved up his Reform California machine, hosting No on 50 rallies across the state.

Democratic Assembly member Tina McKinnor has worked with fellow Black electeds to channel her ballot committee bucks — largely from organized labor — toward turning out Black voters in multiple Los Angeles districts, including her own.

“It’s about letting Black people know this affects us if we don’t get Congress back in the midterms,” McKinnor said. “I was listening to a lot of the messaging and I have to admit, some of it did not resonate with the Black community.”

But images have a special resonance, conveying to disillusioned Democratic voters that their representatives understand a profound desire for the party to take on Trump.

“It’s an opportunity for state leaders to send a message to their constituents that ‘I’m not sitting on the sidelines. I’m fighting back,’” said Dave Jacobson, a political consultant whose clients appear in several ads. “It’s a win-win for these California lawmakers.”

A highly subjective ranking of the ballot measures — past and future, certain and possible — getting our attention this week.

1. Chatbot child safety (2026): Online-safety advocates have filed an initiative aimed at protecting young people from AI chatbots and holding tech companies accountable for any harm they cause to kids, POLITICO’s Chase DiFeliciantonio reports. Former U.S. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy and Common Sense Media CEO Jim Steyer spearheaded the measure after Newsom vetoed a Steyer-backed bill focused on kids’ chatbot safety.

2. Ride-hailing tax (San Francisco, 2026): Connie Chan and three other San Francisco supervisors unveiled a city initiative Friday that would raise taxes on ride-hailing companies, including autonomous vehicle services like Waymo, and reinstate the previous way the city taxed highly paid executives, Chase is also reporting. The measure will appear on the November 2026 ballot.

3. CEQA overhaul (2026): The California Chamber of Commerce is proposing an initiative to overhaul the state’s broadest environmental law. The proposal, filed last week, would build on anti-CEQA momentum to streamline its process for “essential projects” by removing red tape and blocking NIMBY lawsuits.

4. Vacancy tax (San Diego city, 2026?): San Diego City Council is considering a tax on second and vacant homes, voting to draft a version of a measure that would boost the city’s general fund. The proposal is likely to pick a fight with real estate agents across the state, who have successfully organized to defeat similar measures in other cities in recent years.

5. Billionaire tax (2026): SEIU-UHW is back with its third initiative of the cycle, now proposing to plug holes in the state’s health care budget shortfall by imposing a one-time, 5 percent excise tax on the wealthiest Californians. The so-called “billionaire tax” is intended to restore Medi-Cal funding and other health programs, with the remaining funds allocated for the public education system. Other SEIU-UHW initiatives to place restrictions on hospital executive pay and clinic funding have been cleared for circulation.

6. Voter ID (2026?): DeMaio is touting a “record-breaking haul” of 300,000 signatures in the first 20 days of petition-gathering for a constitutional amendment to require government-issued identification when voting, putting his Reform California organization one-third of the way to the 2026 ballot with months left to go.

7. Prop 2 (2024): An education bond approved by voters last year is the target of a lawsuit by Public Advocates, arguing that its construction projects are discriminating against poor communities, reports POLITICO’s Eric He. The lawsuit aims to halt certain funding for school facilities until lawmakers rethink the way money is allocated so that it “delivers education opportunity equitably,” according to Public Advocates managing attorney John Affeldt.

PROP 67 (2016): Ten years after voters approved a ballot measure to ban single-use plastic bags in the state, Californians in the new year will finally bid adieu to the once ubiquitous shopping bag.

In 2016, voters chose to uphold a statewide bag ban established two years earlier by Gov. Jerry Brown, shooting down industry-backed referendum Prop 67 by a 6 percentage point margin. Although many grocers, including Trader Joe’s and Whole Foods Markets, have since switched permanently to paper, some turned to a loophole that allowed them to sell slightly thicker, but still not very recyclable, bags to shoppers. A statewide investigation initiated by Attorney General Rob Bonta in 2022 questioned certain companies’ usage of the recycling symbol and recyclable claims, leading to a settlement last week in which four companies paid $1.7 million to the state and were forced to stop selling plastic bags in California.

In 2024, after years of jousting with plastics producers, lawmakers passed SB 1053, which closes the loophole that still allowed for the thicker plastic bags that proliferated during the pandemic. The law will take effect on Jan. 1.

… LONG BEACH: Voters across California are returning their ballots for an election that countless advertisements have warned them could decide the fate of democracy. In Long Beach, a minor-league baseball team is following the same process to determine whether they should be named after the colorful parrots that fly through town.

The seaside Los Angeles-area city has long been a big baseball town. The Long Beach State Dirtbags, of the local Cal State campus, has a storied history. Major League legends the late Tony Gwynn and Jason Giambi are known for their roots in the city.

But it’s also a city that has an equally rich history of letting citizens participate in the lawmaking process. In addition to voting on numerous ballot measures over the years, Long Beach recently established a participatory budgeting process to encourage public engagement in fiscal policy.

“I love all that direct-democracy stuff,” said Mayor Rex Richardson.

The city will now get a new minor-league team, playing in the Pioneer Baseball League, that will begin playing in the Pioneer Baseball League, an MLB partner, next May. Now known simply as Long Beach Baseball Club, the team initially brought a series of naming ideas to city hall. Richardson said he was not particularly excited about any of them (although he declined to say what they were), suggesting instead that the community weigh in.

And so they have been, in a mock ballot-measure experiment in which residents of Long Beach — or, honestly, anyone passing through town, as there’s no voter-registration process — can cast a ballot. Nearly 5,000 ballots were cast in the first round of voting this fall, which narrowed possible names to the Coast, the Parrots, or the Regulators.

According to team president Ena Patel, there was some initial concern about the direct democracy experiment getting away from the team (think, Boaty McBoatface or NASA Space Station Colbert). The team reserved the right to diverge from the majority vote based on how the name “is brand aligned with the parent companies portfolio” and other practicalities like trademarks — but she pledged that it would be one of the three finalists.

Voting ends on Wednesday at midnight, and voters can participate online or make their way to polling places in breweries, gyms, or the city’s airport to cast their ballot. A winner will be announced in November. According to Richardson, some low-stakes democracy is just what his city could use right now.

“In general, fear is a big reason that people vote for things — it’s to prevent something from happening, or it’s because they’re afraid of something happening,” he said. “But optimism is also a great way to get public participation.”
