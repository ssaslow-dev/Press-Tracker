California Doubles Down in Legal Battle Over Nazi-Looted Pissarro Painting

Richard Whiddington

One of the longest running Nazi restitution cases has taken another turn. On November 17, California’s Attorney General, Rob Bonta, filed a motion to intervene in the Cassirer family’s 20-year pursuit of a Camille Pissarro painting that was stolen by the Nazis and is now owned by Spain’s Thyssen-Bornemisza Collection Foundation.

The family, the heirs of Jewish collector Lilly Cassirer Neubauer, have long been battling the Spanish museum for the return of Rue Saint Honore, Apres Midi, Effet De Pluie (1897), which was taken during World War II. Last year, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled that the Madrid museum could keep the canvas, deciding that Spanish law applied over California law.

With his motion this week, Bonta said California will defend the validity of Assembly Bill 2867 in court, which protects the rights of victims of art theft and requires courts to apply California law to the claims of victims. The Thyssen-Bornemisza has previously claimed that the law is “constitutionally indefensible” and should be ignored. The measure was signed into law by California Governor Gavin Newson in September last year in response to the Ninth Circuit’s ruling.

Whereas California law would have protected the plaintiff as it holds that a thief cannot transfer the right to sell property, Spanish law states that an institution can retain an artwork as long as it has been held for three years in good faith and in the absence of ownership disputes. The Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum bought the painting from Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza in 1993; the first contact from the Cassirers came in the early 2000s.

The case received a new wind of life after the passage of Assembly Bill 2867, which prompted the U.S. Supreme Court to vacate the Ninth Circuit’s decision and ask for the case to be reconsidered. Since that decision in March, the case has rested with the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California.

“There is nothing that can undo the horrors and loss experienced by individuals during the Holocaust. But there is something we can do—that California has done—to return what was stolen back to survivors and their families,” Bonta said in a statement. “My office has supported the Cassirers’ quest for justice for two decades, and we will continue to fight.”

The Cassirers’ attorneys, Sam Dubbin and David Barrett, were pleased to see the Bonta’s office intervene. “It defends California’s interests in protecting the integrity of the art market and the rights of stolen-property victims,” they said over email. “California law has always provided strong protections for the victims of stolen property and stolen art in particular, which the Legislature has consistently reinforced.”

The Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum did not respond to a request for comment.

The Camille Pissarro painting hanging in the Berlin apartment of Lilly Cassirer (ca. 1930). Photo courtesy of David Cassirer.

The atmospheric Pissarro painting was sold in 1939 by Lilly Cassirer Neubauer and her husband Otto Neubauer in exchange for exit visas and $360 (roughly $8,000 today when adjusted for inflation), a sum she never received. After living out the war in England, Cassirer Neubauer sought the painting’s return. In 1958, the West German government paid her compensation of 120,000 Deutschmarks (more than $300,000 today), but the painting itself remained elusive.

According to records, the Pissarro was sold at a Nazi government auction in 1943 and then disappeared from view. It passed through the hands of art dealers in Beverly Hills and Missouri before being purchased by Baron Thyssen-Bornemisza in 1976, whose collection of more than 700 works was acquired by the Spanish government for $350 million in 1993.

In 2000, Claude Cassirer, Cassirer Neubauer’s grandson whose family had moved to California, discovered that the painting was on display at the Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum and petitioned Spain for its return. When this failed, the Cassirer family filed a lawsuit in California in 2005 claiming that the museum should return the painting to its original owners. Claude Cassirer died in 2010 and the case is now brought by his son, David Cassirer, Lilly’s great-grandson.

California Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who introduced the Assembly Bill 2867, has praised Bonta for pursuing the case. “AB 2867 is part of a decades long quest for justice and is rooted in the belief that California must stand on the right side of history,” Gabriel said in a statement. “I am proud that our state will continue to fight for justice and morality.”
