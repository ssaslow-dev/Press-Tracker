The Republicans suddenly into climate adaptation

With help from Emily Schultheis, Nicole Norman, Annie Snider, Alex Guillén, Kate Hapgood and Zack Colman

A MAGIC EYE MOMENT: If you set aside the partisan finger-pointing (a big if) and squint hard enough, it looks like politicians from both sides of the aisle agree on climate-proofing California against future natural disasters.

Congressional Republicans inspired by President-elect Donald Trump’s threats to withhold disaster aid over policy clashes are converging on the idea that federal relief funds should come with conditions. Rep. Byron Donalds, a Republican from Florida, cited “forestry management” and “building out reservoir systems that they should have built 60 years ago” as two examples.

Democrats — and some Republicans from swing districts — are panning the idea of conditioning disaster aid on policy changes.

“I will push back forcefully against linkage and against anything that looks like California is being sort of blackmailed into getting its disaster relief,” Rep. Jared Huffman said. “We don’t do that to other states. They shouldn’t do that to us.”

But he acknowledged the momentum to legislate on more disaster-resistant development, in California and elsewhere. “I’m not saying you don’t talk about improving vegetation management or the hardening of buildings or any number of other things that we should continue to push,” he said. “Just like we should push smarter building standards in hurricane zones and floodplains and earthquake areas.”

The Los Angeles fires have already juiced up support for at least one bipartisan proposal: Rep. Bruce Westerman, a Republican from Arkansas, and Rep. Scott Peters, a Democrat from San Diego, reintroduced their Fix Our Forests Act focused on forest management and wildfire prevention today.

Westerman told reporters Wednesday that the bill would be the first to go through the House Rules Committee next week and that he expects more support in the Senate than late last year, when the bill passed the House but ran out the clock in the Senate.

“The political rhetoric is high, but under the surface, there’s potential here for real, bipartisan, good-faith negotiations to move forward,” said Matt Weiner, a former Democratic aide in both Sacramento and Washington who is now leading the advocacy group Megafire Action, which supports Westerman and Peters’ bill.

In Sacramento, lawmakers have already introduced or promised to introduce dozens of bills on boosting recovery in Los Angeles, fighting wildfires and stabilizing housing insurance. So far, the bills are falling mostly along party lines, with Republicans — who’ve long griped about fires burning through overgrown forests in their rural districts — broadly focusing on forest management. Democrats — who are reckoning with cost-of-living concerns that helped fuel Trump’s rise — are all about housing and rebuilding.

But both Democrats and Republicans have hit on similar policy themes over the past week, like a desire to streamline permitting for housing and the clearing of flammable vegetation.

“There is this awareness and an agreement, really, within a lot of folks in the Legislature and in government right now that we have to use this, as we do the rebuild, to do it smarter and to do it better and to do it in a more thoughtful way,” Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel said today. “It should be an issue of bipartisanship, an issue of people coming together — and frankly, I hope that’s something we’re going to demonstrate here in the state of California.”

As evidence, he cited several calls he’s already had with his Republican counterparts in the Legislature.

Granted, it’s not all kumbaya: Assembly Minority Leader James Gallagher has said he’d be open to conditions on federal disaster aid, breaking from Senate Minority Leader Brian Jones who said today it shouldn’t happen, and Republican firebrand Assemblymembers Carl DeMaio and Bill Essayli are having a field day lambasting their chief antagonist, Gov. Gavin Newsom.

“It is abundantly clear that California’s insane laws and regulations hampered our ability to prepare for, mitigate, and respond to this fire emergency,” DeMaio wrote to GOP congressional leaders Wednesday asking them to place conditions on disaster aid. Among his ideas: funding and speeding brush removal, working with insurers to lower risk and boosting firefighter staffing. — CvK

ZELDIN ON FIRES: Trump’s pick for EPA administrator, Lee Zeldin, took a bipartisan tone in today’s confirmation hearings, assuring Sen. Adam Schiff that he would do anything in his power to help California recover from devastating wildfires.

The former New York representative told Schiff that he would like to visit the state to see the damages and vowed to keep politics out of the EPA’s response — a marked contrast to other Republicans’ approaches.

“It does not matter what kind of a state you’re from. All 50 states are going to be of the highest importance, regardless of where disaster strikes,” he said.

AND ON TJ RIVER: Zeldin also signaled that he would at least reconsider EPA’s decision to withhold federal resources from the polluted Tijuana River by denying it a Superfund designation. The designation would unlock federal resources to clean up the pollution that Schiff said poses “serious environmental and public health challenges to San Diego and Imperial County.” — AS, NN

GASSED UP: California and the federal government announced late Wednesday what could be their last collaboration on vehicle emissions for a while: a settlement with Toyota subsidiary Hino Motors to resolve criminal and civil allegations of emissions fraud.

Per the settlement, Hino will pay $1.6 billion to the federal government and California in fines and mitigation and refrain from importing engines to the U.S. for five years.

The company submitted false applications for engine certification approvals allowing it to import and sell over 110,000 diesel engines for installation in heavy-duty trucks between 2010 and 2022, according to authorities. The applications included fraudulent emissions data.

The amount marks one of the largest criminal and civil penalties in the history of EPA’s mobile source program. — AG, CvK

IN CASE YOU DIDN’T ALREADY KNOW: The Treasury Department’s Federal Insurance Office released a long-awaited report Thursday with new data and analysis showing just how much insurance coverage has become less available and more expensive in areas with the highest expected losses from climate-related perils.

The report includes ZIP-code-level information aggregated from 2018 through 2022, which covers 330 insurers and more than 246 million policies, write POLITICO’s Kate Hapgood and Zack Colman.

Average homeowners insurance premiums per policy increased 8.7 percent faster than the rate of inflation in 2018-2022, with some consumers facing substantially larger premium increases than the national average, according to the report.

The report also found a clear climate-related footprint in insurance costs: Claims averaged $24,000 in the highest-risk ZIP codes, compared with $19,000 in the lowest. People in ZIP codes with the highest expected annual losses due to climate risk paid $2,321 in premiums on average, 82 percent more than the lowest-risk quintile. Policy nonrenewals in those highest climate-risk ZIP codes were 80 percent higher than the lower-risk ones. — CvK
