How Newsom just changed chatbots, housing and social media

DIE IS CAST — Gavin Newsom wrapped the year’s bill-signing season Monday with a raft of decisions that will likely drive the behavior of California housing developers, chatbots and schools for years.

The highlights from the final four days of desk clearing:

Some local officials will have to allow apartment construction near transit. The hard-fought legislation Newsom signed Friday is geared at developing denser housing around rail stops, Nicole Norman reports. Though its backers concede changes to development will be gradual, the signing of SB 79 is a landmark victory for YIMBY groups that also this year scored environmental law exemptions that were previously a third rail in Capitol politics.

Californians can keep consuming Happy Meals and forever chemicals. Fast food restaurants that sell kids’ meals won’t have to offer a healthy option following one Newsom veto. People will also still be able to buy nonstick cookware and other products containing “forever chemicals,” or PFAS, due to another veto, Nicole reports.

California will start a reparations agency. Newsom signed a bill to make that so, in addition to a proposal to raise incarcerated firefighters’ pay, writes Lindsey Holden.

But the governor also killed more contentious pushes to offer descendants of slaves preference in college admissions and home-buying assistance, Eric He and Lindsey report — as well as a bill to help victims of racially motivated eminent domain get compensation.

Newsom gave the tech industry, to which he has deep ties, a few nudges. Social media companies will have to display warning labels on their platforms under one new law. Device makers including Apple and Google will have to check child users’ ages when a parent sets up their devices under another (though, as Tyler Katzenberger reports, the industry supported the bill, which was less stringent than age checks in other states).

Tech companies will also have to identify and address users’ suicidal ideation, and chatbots won’t be allowed to represent themselves as doctors, per Chase DiFeliciantonio and Tyler.

But the governor delivered Silicon Valley major wins with his veto pen. Newsom blocked the threat of massive fines against companies that feed users violent content, prevented the state from requiring a human to oversee workplace firings and discipline when using AI and sank sweeping rules preventing AI companies from making chatbots accessible to children, Tyler and Chase report.

Schools won’t recoup attendance-based funding lost due to Donald Trump’s immigration raids. As Eric reports, Newsom raised concerns that the measure wouldn’t ensure kids staying home from school due to federal crackdowns are able to study elsewhere — among other issues.

But the governor did sign a bill aiming to help families caught up in the raids. AB 495 will allow parents to seek joint guardianship for their children if they get deported and expand the definition of a “relative” who is authorized to serve as a child’s caretaker, Eric writes.

Utilities will be banned from passing on lobbying and advertising costs to consumers. As Alex Nieves and Noah Baustin report, Newsom’s signing of the ban was a win for energy affordability advocates in a year in which the governor tried to press down California energy prices — surely sensing the political liability that comes with high costs.

Diesel trains on the brink of retirement can rest their axles. As Alex writes, Newsom signed legislation to ban public rail agencies from selling their oldest locomotives after they’re retired.

REDISTRICTING WARS

THE ULTIMATE BACKUP — Newsom is calling in Barack Obama as Democrats’ closer to help sell voters on the California governor’s redistricting campaign, Melanie Mason reports this morning.

The former president, who consistently ranks as the party’s most popular figure, stars in the newest ad for Proposition 50, as Newsom and his allies work to juice Democratic turnout ahead of next month’s special election.

“California, the whole nation is counting on you,” Obama says in a direct-to-camera spot, leaning into the nationalization of the race that has been central to Newsom’s strategy. “Democracy is on the ballot November 4th.”

Obama never mentions Trump by name. But as he accuses Republicans of “trying to steal enough seats in Congress to rig the next election,” images of the current president flash on-screen.

The ad begins airing Tuesday on broadcast, cable and digital platforms. A spokesperson for the campaign did not provide details on the size of the buy.

SAN FRANCISCO

CODE SWITCH — Tech billionaire Marc Benioff’s new show of support for Trump and call for National Guard troops to patrol San Francisco rattled local Democrats who long regarded the Salesforce CEO as a close ally, Tyler writes.

Benioff, speaking to The New York Times in an interview published Friday, said he was “all for” deploying Guard troops with the goal of fighting crime in the city. In the same interview, Benioff said Trump is “doing a great job” as president.

His comments cut against his years of lavish giving and full-throated support for Democratic causes, even as other tech titans shifted toward Trump. “This is a slap in the face to San Francisco,” Matt Dorsey, who represents parts of downtown on the city’s Board of Supervisors, said in an X post.

Before Benioff’s bombshell comments … he changed his voter registration from California to Hawaii and began acquiring more and more property there, the San Francisco Standard reports. Benioff in a text said, “It’s not a secret I live in Hawaii,” adding, “I have been living there on and off for decades.” But his time spent away from his home town San Francisco is part of what local political insiders see as a broader withdrawal from the city.

After he made them … Benioff wrote a long clean-up post on social media Sunday, praising Mayor Daniel Lurie and claiming that San Francisco is “moving in the right direction” ahead of Salesforce’s Dreamforce event in the city this week.

“When I was recently asked about federal resources, my point was this: each year, to make Dreamforce as safe as possible for 50,000 attendees, we add 200 additional law-enforcement professionals — coordinated across city, state, and other partners,” Benioff wrote. “It’s proof that collaboration works and a reminder that the city needs more resources to keep San Franciscans safe year-round.”

Still, a Monday press event featuring Benioff and Lurie was canceled, KQED reported, with Salesforce representatives blaming an unexpected rainstorm. They did not explain why the event wasn’t simply moved indoors.

CAMPAIGN YEAR(S)

SAC SHOWDOWN — Richard Pan, a physician who pushed some of the nation’s most sweeping vaccine legislation as a state senator during the pandemic, is running for the Sacramento-area House seat currently held by his former statehouse colleague Kevin Kiley.

After running unsuccessfully for Sacramento mayor last year, Pan is betting that frustration with Medicaid cuts passed by Republicans in Congress — paired with his medical background — will help him flip the seat for Democrats.

“Health care is going to be a very important issue of the campaign,” Pan said in an interview on Monday, in which he recalled tangling with Robert F. Kennedy Jr. when the future Health and Human Services secretary traveled to Sacramento to oppose Pan’s vaccination policy efforts. “We need representation from people who will hold the administration accountable when they try to take away people’s health care.”

If California’s House maps are redrawn this fall, the 3rd District will flip from a 6-point Republican voter registration advantage to a roughly 8-point Democratic edge. Kiley is one of five incumbents that Democrats are seeking to oust with Prop 50, though he hasn’t announced what he’ll do if the measure passes.

Pan will run in the 3rd District regardless of whether it’s redrawn, a spokesperson for him said.

Hanging over Pan’s candidacy is Rep. Ami Bera, who currently occupies the nearby 6th District but has not said whether he’ll run in that district or the 3rd if Prop 50 passes. Bera is still considering running in either if the measure succeeds, a person familiar with his deliberations who was granted anonymity to describe the decision-making process told Playbook.

Others floated for one of the Sacramento-area seats include state Sen. Angelique Ashby, Sacramento County District Attorney Thien Ho, and Bera’s spouse, Janine Bera, though none has announced plans to run for Congress.

Pan told Playbook he has spoken with several of the potential candidates, but none has told him definitively that they plan to run.

LOS ANGELES

BEUTNER’S RETURN — Austin Beutner, the former investment banker who led Los Angeles schools through the beginning of the coronavirus pandemic, announced Monday he will challenge fellow Democrat Karen Bass in the Los Angeles mayor’s race, casting the city under her leadership as “adrift.”

“It seems every day our city is becoming a more expensive, less safe and more difficult place to live,” the former Los Angeles Unified School District superintendent said in a launch video. “The city has spent billions to solve problems that have just become bigger problems: homelessness, the cost of housing, the loss of jobs and opportunity.”

Beutner previously considered running for state superintendent of public instruction but instead became the first serious challenger to Bass’ reelection bid. The mayor was politically weakened by criticism of her response to this year’s deadly fires in Los Angeles County, even if she has rebounded while sparring with Trump over his deployment of immigration agents and military members to her city.

The race would be further shaken up if mall magnate Rick Caruso, who ran against Bass in 2022, stages a rematch. He has weighed running for mayor again, or, for governor. Read the full story here.

FLYING OVER L.A. SOON — The Trump administration is moving to fortify U.S. skies against potential drone threats ahead of next summer’s World Cup and other mega-events, casting the technology as necessary both to bolster security and to help outpace foreign competition for aviation technology, writes Sophia Cai.

The administration plans to launch a $500 million effort to support state and local governments as they develop anti-drone security strategies ahead of the soccer tournament being played across the U.S., Canada and Mexico, along with the United States’ 250th anniversary and 2028 Olympics in Los Angeles, according to Andrew Giuliani, director of the White House’s FIFA World Cup 2026 Task Force.

The White House says the money will be available to all 50 states but put to particular use to secure the 104 soccer matches that will be played in American stadiums.

ON THE AIRWAVES

TAPES ON TAPES — Republican Steve Hilton got in on the spreading trend of gubernatorial candidates splicing their own messaging with Katie Porter’s viral videos berating a staffer and threatening to walk out of an interview. Hilton, playing on Porter’s complaint during the interview that she was asked too many follow-up questions, inserted some “follow-ups” of his own bashing Democratic policy in a campaign video set to “Curb Your Enthusiasm” theme music.

And Antonio Villaraigosa released at least his third ad seeking to capitalize on the videos, this one showing clips of media commentators aghast at Porter’s conduct. (He also picked up the endorsement of the California State Association of Electrical Workers, his campaign announced on Monday.)

Meanwhile … Alex Padilla’s boosters see last week’s video controversy as an opportunity for the senior California senator to enter the race. The New York Times reports that “several prominent interest groups are now in talks about commissioning a poll to gauge how Mr. Padilla would fare if he ran.” A prominent California political entity previously conducted a survey on the governor’s race that included Padilla’s name as the senator weighed a bid, Playbook reported in August.

Further reading: Aaron Pellish looked back at seven other times politicians flubbed TV interviews — from Ted Kennedy’s listless response to a question about challenging Jimmy Carter to Sarah Palin’s confusing remarks about her news diet and the supposed foreign policy knowledge she gained from Alaska’s proximity to Russia.

CASH DASH

KIM Q3 — Republican Rep. Young Kim raised more than $1.5 million during the third quarter, according to her campaign. That’s not a big drop from her massive $2 million, race-leading second quarter — which is notable, because Prop 50 could turn her Orange County district from a battleground to a safe red seat. Several of her current competitors have said they’ll instead run against Rep. Darrell Issa if California’s House maps are redrawn.

GERRYMONEYING — The campaign fighting California Democrats’ gerrymandering measure is not only being walloped in fundraising but in TV spending. Punchbowl News reports that Yes On Prop 50’s broadcast spending edge is approaching $40 million.

Top Talkers

KAMALA COMMENDS — Kamala Harris joined Newsom in congratulating the Trump administration on the Israel-Hamas ceasefire deal, a rare instance of bipartisan backpatting. “I commend the leaders and partners whose efforts made this agreement possible, including the president and his team,” Harris wrote in a statement.

ALTADENA COMEBACK — The winner of a $2 billion Powerball is one of the biggest-spending investors scooping up lots in Altadena that were burned by the Eaton Fire earlier this year, the Wall Street Journal reports. Edwin Castro, 33, told the outlet he’s planning to build mostly single family homes and sell them at market value — bringing back the small-town feel of the community along the way.

“I want it to feel like the old neighborhood,” he said. “Like if you put all those houses pre-fire in a time bubble.”
