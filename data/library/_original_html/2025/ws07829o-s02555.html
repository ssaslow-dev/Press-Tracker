Is Newsom ready to take California’s budget rules to the ballot?

By EMILY SCHULTHEIS and WILL MCCARTHY

BUDGET BALLOT MOMENTUM? — A year ago, after his political operation polled voters on whether they supported amending the state constitution to loosen budgeting constraints, Gov. Gavin Newsom declared that “we’ve got work to do” to convince them those changes are necessary.

Newsom may find his on-again-off-again nemesis Donald Trump is doing the work for him. The federal-level economic turmoil caused by Trump’s multifront trade war will likely lead the governor to update his budget proposal later this month to show a projected shortfall. That could give a boost to a pair of potential constitutional amendments that Newsom and other supporters say would give the state new tools to weather financial storms.

“There’s been more energy and momentum around thinking about some structural changes that could help to address those challenges,” said Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who chairs the Budget Committee.

The first proposal, making its way through the Legislature with Democratic sponsors as ACA 1, would allow state officials to allocate more money to the state’s rainy-day fund in years when the state has a budget surplus, while lifting the so-called Gann limit on government spending.

As part of the small-government drive that inspired the passage of Proposition 13 in 1978, voters the next year approved Proposition 4, which caps spending in perpetuity at then-current levels (while adjusting for inflation and population). If revenue exceeds that spending level, half of the extra funds must be returned to taxpayers and the other half put toward education. In 2014, voters approved Prop 2, which set the maximum amount that can be allocated to the rainy-day fund as 10 percent of the general fund proceeds of taxes.

Newsom and other Democrats have argued that raising the rainy-day fund cap and amending the Gann limit will help cushion California from the massive fluctuations in revenue that result from the state’s progressive tax laws. (As revisions of previous constitutional amendments, these budget- and revenue-related questions need to be resolved at the ballot box.)

“It’s very suboptimal, from a public policy perspective and from a budgetary perspective, to have these wild swings in revenue,” said Gabriel.

Others are looking to bolster the state’s finances by focusing on revenue. Backers of an existing tax on high earners, established by 2012’s Proposition 30 and extended by 2016’s Prop 55, are considering putting it before voters for another extension in 2026. The tax is set to expire in 2030, meaning its supporters are deciding whether to pull the trigger this year or hold off for a (potentially) more favorable electorate in 2028.

“Proponents seeking additional revenue face an incredibly high bar in 2026 to demonstrate need, efficacy and that their measures won’t hit voters’ wallets,” veteran ballot-measure consultant Ned Wigglesworth told Playbook. “A ballot measure’s impact on family finances is always a concern for voters, but the current anxiety and stress around cost of living and economic issues make this cycle more fraught.”

The Assembly Budget Committee is set to hear proposals related to the rainy-day fund in a hearing on Wednesday, which could give an early indication as to lawmakers’ interest in pushing the issue to the ballot. Trump’s trade policy and the attendant market convulsions are making it hard for anyone to anticipate where the economy is likely to be next November.

“Part of the challenge for everybody,” said Gabriel, “is that it feels like things can change in Washington four times before noon.”

Spotlighting the legislative wrangling over bills that could lead to 2026 ballot measures.

BONDS AWAY (AB 48): The state borrowed $10 billion to fund K-12 school construction and upgrades with last November’s Prop 2. Assemblymember David Alvarez wants to do something similar for public colleges and universities, with one major difference: His AB 48 would also include provisions for building student housing.

In a hearing of the Assembly Housing and Community Development Committee, Alvarez argued it’s a crucial need in a state where many college kids lack stable housing and some are proposing that students be allowed to sleep in their cars. The bill passed the committee by a vote of 9-1, but even some of those who support the proposal’s aims asked for more specificity about the bond’s total cost and how that would be allocated.

Assemblymember Ash Kalra asked “how much of it will go to actual housing versus other kinds of infrastructure needs,” noting that California’s public universities have “almost unlimited” needs for infrastructure investments. Alvarez said he is speaking with University of California and California State University officials about their needs and balancing those with how much bond borrowing the state can afford to take on.

AB 48 heads to the Appropriations Committee next.

CANNABIS ALIGNED: An effort to overhaul California’s decade-old laws regulating marijuana sales appear to be dead for 2026.

“We’re going to extend our timeline,” Nina Parks, a veteran industry consultant who had organized the coalition of small business owners, said last week. “Trying to push it for next year was very ambitious.”

Her group, Cannabis Aligned, had intended to ask voters to repeal and replace Proposition 64, a 2016 initiative that legalized recreational marijuana in the state for the first time while imposing taxes on retail sales and cultivation. Parks spent months leading Zoom-based strategy sessions with business owners that she promoted with can-do Instagram posts, but never filed paperwork with the secretary of state.

The growing coalition reached a consensus that it should delay its deliberations, according to Parks, to let key regulatory issues play out in the coming months before reassessing its path to the ballot. On July 1, the tax rate for cannabis will rise from 15 percent to 19 percent, raising prices in a way that drives buyers toward the illegal market. In Los Angeles, industry groups are pushing the city council to pass a “cannabis equity and market stabilization act” that would shut down illegal shops and provide retroactive tax credits to businesses.

Parks believes those developments could further incentivize legal cannabis businesses to fund a campaign to cut taxes and streamline regulations via the ballot. While she says the coalition plans to continue with regular coalition meetings and viability polling, it is likely that Cannabis Aligned will file an initiative in the coming months.

“This was to get the industry excited and galvanized again,” Parks said. “The realistic timeline is 2028.”

Cannabis Aligned is survived by other groups that hope to see California voters change the way marijuana is bought and sold, including a veterans’ group known as Weed for Warriors.

DIETARY ACRYLAMIDE: Proposition 65 may be California’s most-ubiquitous ballot measure, thanks to the labels the 1986 initiative placed on everyday products warning of potential carcinogens. But the presence of one chemical should no longer trigger warnings, a federal court ruled last week.

Acrylamide is a chemical used in drinking water treatment, and can also form naturally in certain foods — including potatoes and grains — during the cooking process. It was added to the list of approximately 900 chemicals covered by Prop 65 after studies showed it produced cancer in laboratory mice.

The California Chamber of Commerce first filed suit against the state in 2019, arguing it violated the First Amendment to require that businesses disseminate “false or misleading speech” about a chemical never clearly linked to cancer in human beings. (The FDA says “it’s not clear exactly what risk” it poses, although the agency recommends trying to reduce exposure.)

A federal district court granted a preliminary injunction for Prop 65 warnings on acrylamide in 2021, but the nonprofit Council for Education and Research on Toxics quickly appealed the decision. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals upheld the injunction in 2022, and a judge made it permanent on Friday after concluding that the warnings were “misleading and controversial.” (The state has given no indication about whether it intends to appeal.)

CalChamber President and CEO Jennifer Barrera said in a statement that the ruling “not only protects businesses from enforcement actions based on these warnings, but also upholds the principle that government-mandated disclosures must be factually accurate and not misleading.”

… GROVER BEACH: The former mayor of an upper-middle-class beach community believes its government is rife with corruption. The best way to fix it, according to her, is to have more Grover Beach officials owe their jobs to voters than to politicians.

Next year, the approximately 12,000 residents of this Central Coast city will likely be asked to convert the jobs of city clerk and city attorney from appointed positions, chosen by city council, to elected offices. The measures’ top booster argues that the current arrangement — which prioritizes having government staff aligned with small-town leadership — has created something of a deep state that opposes the will of voters.

“I’m not a conspiracy theorist, I never have been,” said Debbie Peterson, a self-described lifelong Democrat who resigned from office in 2019 to (quite literally) write the book on her city’s corruption. “It has just been going on so long that people don’t see it.”

Last year, Peterson led an effort to recall two council members and her mayoral successor due to their support of a water project that she believed was evidence of their corruption and disconnect with voters. Only one of her recall petitions actually led to a vote — the council member, Daniel Rushing, was ultimately recalled — but Peterson clashed with the city clerk during the process.

Soon after, Peterson began another petition drive, this time to turn the clerk’s office into an elected post. Just last month, she decided to broaden her ambitions, drafting a parallel initiative that would make the city attorney position elected, too.

The current mayor, Kassi Dee, says both of the ballot measures are unnecessary and will prove costly for a city that usually hires its attorney on a contract basis and would pay more to fill an elected position in an ongoing way.

Besides, she argues, her predecessor’s allegations just promote conflict between staff and city council. Dee says she has never observed any corruption within city government, let alone of the kind that would require revising the charter.

“I don’t doubt any of the ethics or morals of any of our city staff,” Dee said.
