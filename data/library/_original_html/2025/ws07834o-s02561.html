Nobody puts Maggy Krell in a corner

LOSE-LOSE: A freshman Democrat today threw gasoline on an already smoldering sex-trafficking dumpster fire — forcing Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas to very publicly put it out on the body’s floor.

After Maggy Krell couldn’t get her Public Safety Committee colleagues on board with a proposal to add 16- and 17-year-olds to a child solicitation law, the state lawmaker joined with Republicans to take the provision to the Assembly floor and force the entire body to consider it.

Her move — defying Rivas and Public Safety Chair Nick Schultz — sent Democrats into crisis-management mode and handed GOP lawmakers the chance to assert the moral high ground (and gather plenty of campaign ad fodder) while slamming Dems as lacking character.

“It’s our chance to choose the kids of California, to save them,” said Assemblymember David Tangipa, a Fresno Republican.

After a leadership huddle that lasted more than an hour, Rivas responded by taking Krell’s name off the bill, giving it to Schultz and moderate Sacramento Democrat Stephanie Nguyen. Democratic leaders also added language stating their “intent” to draft the “strongest laws to protect 16- and 17-year-old victims.”

Lawmakers approved the changes on a mostly party-line vote, and the bill now heads to the Assembly Appropriations Committee for further amendments.

But Democrats made their legislative moves from a defensive crouch, once again pressured into explaining that, yes, they are against trafficking and, yes, there are existing laws to protect minors approached for sex. Those who object to including older teens in the soliciting law have expressed hesitations about enacting harsh penalties in a blanket manner, especially as the state moves in a conservative direction on crime.

Schultz told reporters his concern is that Krell’s proposal would treat “an 18-year-old exactly the same as a 50-year-old trafficker of children.”

“I’m not saying that one should have a consequence and the other should not,” he said. “But it’s imperative that the law be just and that we account for differences in circumstance for each case.”

Krell — a former prosecutor who’s dedicated much of her professional life to fighting sex trafficking — said she was fine with the bill getting taken away from her, as long as it takes older teens into consideration. She ended up voting against the amendments.

“I don’t care whether my name is on the bill, but I would happily support it if it includes protections for 16- and 17-year-olds, if it treats them as victims, because they truly are,” Krell said on the floor. “If it recognizes the fights that survivors have led for decades for the basic understanding that if you’re 17 years old on a street corner, and an old man comes up and purchases you for sex, that’s rape.”

Following the floor session, Rivas and Schultz blamed Krell for the blowup, saying they offered her the choice of shelving her bill for a year or accepting the older teen carveout. When Krell took the latter option, they said, the two of them believed she had accepted a deal — and then went on to shred it.

Now, the leaders’ plan is to craft what they see as a more nuanced piece of legislation. Rivas said he had not spoken to Gov. Gavin Newsom about the situation. The governor’s office earlier in the week issued a statement supporting Krell’s efforts.

When asked whether Krell will face consequences for her legislative maneuvering, Rivas dodged the question.

“I respect all of our colleagues,” he said, “and we’re all very passionate about the work we have to do.”

WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW TODAY

DON’T BRING YOUR KID TO WORK DAY: Perhaps the day when every assemblymember was getting up to speak about the evils of child sexual abuse and prostitution was not the best day to bring a child to the floor of the chamber, as Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel found out the hard way.

According to Gabriel, there had been a longstanding plan to take his son (who was thrilled to be up playing video games in the wee hours to make the early flight to Sacramento with his father) to the Capitol today before he knew the topic of the day’s debate. Gabriel quickly ushered him to the members’ lounge before the sex trafficking talk started.

“We’re like, right on the verge of having a conversation with him about what sex even is,” Gabriel told reporters. Evidently, that was not a talk he wanted to start on the floor of the Assembly. — Rachel Bluth

ON THE BEATS

TICKET TO RIDE: A sweeping proposal from Assemblymember Isaac Bryan to impose new rules on ticket resellers such as StubHub and SeatGeek is moving on.

The proposal attempts to stop “speculative ticketing,” in which third parties sell tickets to consumers before they’ve acquired them. It would also ban resellers from saying shows are “sold out” if a primary seller still has tickets available, among several other provisions.

It cleared Assembly Judiciary after a successful stop in the chamber’s Privacy and Consumer Protection Committee and has also been referred to Arts and Entertainment.

A rival bill from Assemblymember Alex Lee got through Privacy today. It would require venues to accept a consumer’s proof of purchasing a ticket (such as a receipt) in lieu of a ticket under certain circumstances. Both bills will likely be amended further, their authors and committee chairs have acknowledged, in an attempt to reach a compromise on complicated ticketing issues that have drawn interest from deep-pocketed industry groups on both sides. — Blake Jones

DATA PRIVACY DELAY: Assemblymember Carl DeMaio will likely delay his push to help Californians shield their personal information from foreign governments like China by another year, our Tyler Katzenberger reports for POLITICO Pro subscribers.

Seizing on bipartisan concerns about TikTok and Chinese companies’ data-sharing practices, DeMaio proposed AB 364 as one of his first bills in office, seeking to require companies to obtain consent annually before maintaining a California-based user’s data outside of the United States.

But DeMaio told POLITICO he’s still working on amendments ahead of a key committee deadline on Friday: The San Diego Republican’s bill is currently listed as a fiscal measure, meaning it must clear the Assembly Privacy and Consumer Protection Committee to advance this year.

DeMaio in a text message said it’s “unsurprising” his bill hasn’t been scheduled for a committee hearing yet, given its focus on international data-sharing covers “unchartered territory” in the Legislature.
