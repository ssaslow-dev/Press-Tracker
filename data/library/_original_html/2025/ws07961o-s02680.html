IT’S HAPPENING: Donald Trump knows what he’s doing this time.

That’s what we would have said Monday had Trump issued his executive order on “Unleashing American Energy” more than 25 minutes before publication time.

None of the substance of what Trump’s doing is surprising. Monday’s orders hew closely to Project 2025’s recommendations, which included revoking California’s waiver to set its tailpipe emissions standards, exiting the Paris Agreement, expanding oil drilling in Alaska, kneecapping offshore wind and freezing tens of billions of dollars in renewable energy and electric vehicle subsidies.

“Despite what he said about not endorsing Project 2025, it seems to be kind of the administration’s driver’s manual,” said Dan Farber, faculty director at the Center for Law, Energy and the Environment at the University of California, Berkeley.

Some California climate types are clinging to agenda-slowing realities like the pace of court proceedings and automakers’ production schedules. Others are acknowledging Trump’s amped-up policy chops, as underscored by his ability to get government workers to put out 46 executive orders, proclamations and other decrees — on a federal holiday, no less.

“He’s been surrounding himself with people that are capable politically and capable with resources, and they have an agenda,” said state Sen. Jerry McNerney, an eight-term House veteran who started in the Legislature this month. “He’s got all kinds of intellectual firepower that’s ready to move forward in making sure that we step back and allow climate change to further destroy our nation and our resources.”

There are some signs of pressure on Gov. Gavin Newsom — whose sole verbal response to Trump’s climate moves since Monday has been a one-sentence statement (“If you don’t believe in science, believe your own damn eyes”) — to play more-active defense.

McNerney introduced a bill today to beef up the state’s tax incentives for renewable energy, in part to make up for anticipated federal cuts.

And 65 environmental groups today sent Newsom’s transportation appointees a letter citing Trump’s rollbacks in pushing for more spending on public transit and less on highway expansions.

“In the next four years of the Trump Administration our state’s ability to regulate electric vehicle and truck technology may be greatly diminished and necessitate that we make even more progress in reducing driving to achieve our climate goals, a policy area that is entirely within state and local government control,” they said.

More moves are on the way. Trump told reporters Monday that Mexico and Canada have to address his concerns about immigration and fentanyl shipments or else face 25 percent tariffs on Feb. 1, according to POLITICO’s Doug Palmer.

The tariffs could raise costs on everything from electric vehicle components to lumber that Los Angeles residents need to rebuild from the fires. And Trump’s deadline for a decision is sooner than the April 1 date specified in Monday’s White House trade memo.

QUOTE OF THE DAY: “When you are a rich person, you like to take a shower,” Trump said today in reiterating his desire for Newsom to “turn the valve” and send more water from Northern California to central and Southern California (even though it doesn’t really work that way.)

Trump said he’s going to issue an order “demanding that they immediately let that water come down through California,” but he’s already made one move with the goal of boosting deliveries: A Day One memorandum orders the federal government to review and restart his previous administration’s stalled efforts to rewrite the endangered species rules guiding how much water can be sent south from the Sacramento-San Joaquin River Delta. We’ll see what else unfolds ahead of his trip Friday to tour LA’s fire-devastated areas.

FIRE SPENDING: The Assembly and Senate budget committees are both scheduled to hold hearings tomorrow on sending $2.5 billion to help Los Angeles recover from the recent wildfires.

Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel and Senate Budget Chair Scott Wiener introduced the early action budget legislation Monday (which, after some chaotic back-and-forth, will indeed be separate from the legislation setting aside $50 million for legal defenses against the Trump administration.)

Lawmakers and Newsom expect to get most or all of the $2.5 billion reimbursed by insurance or the Federal Emergency Management Agency, though that may take years — and could be subject to Trump’s push (reiterated today) to condition or slow aid to California.

The money would be available in several rounds, with $1 billion available for emergency response like debris removal and mitigation of any debris flows as soon as Newsom signs the bill, and the rest within days. Four million dollars would be set aside specifically for local governments to fast-track rebuilding permits and $1 million to rebuild and recover local public and charter schools.

Lawmakers tacked on new reporting requirements for the state agencies spending the money in anticipation of informing future wildfire funding negotiations, including possibly sending more disaster aid to Los Angeles later this year and appropriating the $10 billion climate bond voters passed last November.

The hearings are scheduled to start at 9 a.m. in the Assembly and 2 p.m. in the Senate. A floor vote is expected Thursday, in time for Newsom to sign the spending before Trump’s scheduled visit to Los Angeles on Friday. — CvK

THE OTHER LA WATER PROBLEM: Southern California’s biggest water supplier again punted today on what to do with its general manager, Adel Hagekhalil, after the completion of an investigation into claims of harassment and a toxic workplace.

The Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, which supplies some 19 million Californians in Los Angeles and surrounding areas, is set to continue today’s closed session meeting Thursday. The 38-member board had been set to vote to either reinstate or terminate Hagekhalil after putting him on administrative leave six months ago.

Met didn’t release the findings from the completed independent investigative report, but Hagekhalil’s lawyer, Kerry Garvis Wright, claimed that it didn’t substantiate claims of retaliation, harassment or a hostile work environment. She said it did show that Hagekhalil treated certain male colleagues preferentially but didn’t conclude that it was because of gender.

Hagekhalil’s allies include environmental and tribal groups, who saw in his focus on recycled water and climate resilience a departure from Southern California’s previous reliance on imported water from sensitive Northern California ecosystems (yes, the same imported water Trump wants to increase). They also, at least historically, were the cities of San Diego and Los Angeles, who flexed their political muscles over rivals in Orange County to appoint him as general manager in 2021 in a close vote. Hagekhalil’s fate now may be determined by whether Los Angeles will back him again — and the fires may have given him a helping hand.

Exhibit A: The majority of the Los Angeles City Council wrote to Met’s board of directors Monday urging them to reinstate Hagekhalil and citing the recent fires and scrutiny of Los Angeles’ water supply as a reason.

“The region, including the City and County of Los Angeles, has just gone through the most disastrous fire event in its history,” they wrote. “Today, more than ever, ensuring robust water supplies and infrastructure, with proper leadership, is needed. … Adel is uniquely qualified to lead the agency at this moment of great need.” — CvK

THE INTERIM FEDERAL WATER CREW: The Interior Department’s temporary leadership until Trump’s nominees clear Congress is taking shape under an order today by acting Interior Secretary Walter Cruickshank.

Most notably, Sacramento’s own Paul Souza, the longtime director of the Fish and Wildlife Service’s Pacific Southwest Region, will serve as acting director of FWS. And David Palumbo will temporarily lead the Bureau of Reclamation, where he’s also long served and most recently was deputy commissioner. — CvK

BERNHARDT’S BACK: Relatedly, lobbying firm Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck announced today that it has rehired David Bernhardt, one of its longtime energy and water lobbyists who served as Interior secretary during Trump’s first term and had taken a break from the firm in the fall of 2024 to focus on “nonprofit activities.” Bernhardt will provide “strategic counsel” in Washington. — CvK
