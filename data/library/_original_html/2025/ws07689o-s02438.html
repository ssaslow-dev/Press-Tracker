Glock handguns could be banned in California

Jamie Kennedy

SACRAMENTO, Calif. — As the owner of the gun shop Gun Worx in Sacramento, Justin Davis said there’s a reason so many people buy Glock and Glock-style handguns.

What You Need To Know

  • Proposed state legislation would ban all Glock-style guns that use a striker-fired system

  • Glock and Glock-style guns make up a large portion of gun sales for many gun shop owners

  • Lawmakers said switches that are easily procurable too easily turn them into automatic guns, making them very dangerous

  • From 2012 to 2016, federal authorities seized 814 machine-gun conversion parts; that jumped to 5,454 between 2017 and 2021

“They’re so reliable,” Davis said. “They will go bang. You cannot clean them, you can run any ammo through them, and they will always, always, go bang. And I don’t think I can say that about any other gun.”

Because of this, these types of guns, which use a striker-fired system versus a hammer-firing system, also make up the bulk of Davis’s handgun sales at his small gun and ammo shop.

“I’m going to say 75% at least,” Davis said. “Maybe more; could be 80%. I mean, it’s tremendous.”

He also said handgun sales make up around 60% of his total business, which is the reason Davis said he’s very alarmed with legislation that could ban all striker-fired gun sales, because of the ease lawmakers say in turning them from semi-automatic, to fully automatic.

“They’re banning them from people that don’t break the law,” Davis said. “Criminals are criminals because they break the law, and they don’t care what Glock ban or this gun’s banned they don’t care. That’s why they’re criminals.”

A so-called Glock switch converts the gun into an automatic weapon.

A small add-on inserted into the rear of the gun allowed it to fire up to 30 rounds in two seconds. 

From 2012 to 2016, federal authorities seized 814 machine-gun conversion parts; that jumped to 5,454 between 2017 and 2021.

The ease of procuring the switches and what they do to certain handguns is something that needs to be addressed said Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, the bill’s author.

“The idea that people could actually be making them at home with something that they could 3D print or buy online for $25 is a really scary thing,” Gabriel said.

He said he’s asking for a simple solution.

“There’s a lot of folks out there that have put pieces in place in terms of the engineering of the firearms, that you can’t use a switch, that you can’t easily convert it into a machine gun,” Gabriel said. “So, we’re not asking the industry to do something that’s impossible. We’re not even asking them to do something that’s difficult. We’re just asking a few manufacturers out there to do what all of their competitors are doing, which is to make these very minor modifications.”

The law would not require anyone who still owns a Glock-style gun to surrender it.

The bill has made it out of the Assembly and is currently in a Senate committee suspense file, which could mean it doesn’t get a full Senate vote this year.

Little comfort, Davis said, if it doesn’t make it this year.

“99.9% sure it will be back next year,” Davis said.

And something he is trying to prepare for, to fight against, and how to figure out how his business might endure.
