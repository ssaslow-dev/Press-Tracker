Predictions we got right (or wrong)

Programming note: We’ll be off starting next week for the holidays but will be back to our normal schedule on Monday, Jan. 5.

PAGING MISS CLEO — Around this time last year, we gazed into the crystal ball and made a series of big predictions about California politics in the year ahead — from the tenor of Trump resistance 2.0 to major policy fights on AI safety and housing.

How did our prognostications fare in the roller coaster ride that was 2025? What did we get right and what missed the mark?

Our POLITICO California team huddled to revisit our biggest predictions — and what the outcomes say about how those political and policy fights could evolve in the year to come:

CALIFORNIA RESISTANCE, PART 2: Some argue Democrats are too tired or bloodied to resist Trump, but those fights will begin immediately once he takes office.

Gov. Gavin Newsom and other prominent Democrats did initially downplay the idea of resistance 2.0, but that lasted about five minutes. California filed dozens of lawsuits challenging Trump’s executive orders within his first few months in office, aiming to protect the state’s policies on everything from immigration to the environment. And by the early summer, Newsom’s office embraced a more combative tone and trolling social media persona. The pivot came as Trump stretched political norms and sent the National Guard into Los Angeles and a host of other blue cities. Heading into 2026, Newsom and other prominent pols have a strong political incentive to keep the resistance alive and well in the Golden State.

AFFORDABILITY IS THE WORD: 2025 will be the year of the “affordability” buzzword.

Nailed it (forgive the brag). Democrats so relentlessly leaned into the affordability messaging that the word itself seemed to get under Trump’s skin, prompting him to dismiss it as a “con job.” But California’s ruling Democrats, who vowed to make affordability a centerpiece of their focus in 2025, learned that actually tackling the high cost of living is a lot harder than just identifying the problem. Democrats passed measures to address the state’s housing (more on that below) and energy prices, but California is still one of the most expensive states to live in. Nationally, we can expect Democrats to continue to hang the affordability albatross around the GOP’s neck in the midterms. But the issue will undoubtedly be a theme in next year’s governor’s race too, and the many Democrats hoping to succeed Newsom will need to walk a tightrope, acknowledging voters’ worries about high costs without laying the blame on the incumbent governor and their own party.

OPINION SHIFT ON IMMIGRATION: California won’t take substantial action to impede ICE agents.

This prediction looked good at first. Some California House Democrats opened the year voting for the GOP’s Laken Riley Act, and Newsom for months barely spoke publicly about immigration.

But everything changed when Trump ramped up deportations and, in June, sent the National Guard and Marines to Los Angeles. Democrats at all levels of the state’s politics — especially Newsom — ripped the deployments and raids. By September, Newsom had signed legislation attempting to ban ICE agents from wearing masks used to conceal their identity, alongside laws meant to shield immigrants’ health care information and require families be notified of federal agents’ presence at schools.

We’ll take a shred of credit, though, for pointing out that the federal government’s sweeping immigration authority would limit the effectiveness of any California response. The mask ban is tied up in a court fight, and the Trump administration has vowed not to follow it, anyway.

HOUSING WILL BE KING: Democrats in the Legislature are expected to go BIG on housing this year.

On the money. There could hardly have been a bigger year for housing in Sacramento. Legislators and the governor, after a monthslong brawl with unions and environmentalists, enacted the most sweeping reforms to state housing law in decades. They took a major bite out of environmental reviews for urban infill housing. Another landmark measure, Senate Bill 79, will require large cities to allow taller apartment buildings near rail stations.

Now, the question remains: Will these big swings actually lead to more housing construction? Newsom and YIMBY-aligned state lawmakers are under pressure to show these laws can bear fruit. There’s also expected to be a host of “clean-up” bills on SB79, which could potentially remove its teeth and revive a heated debate over the state’s role in mandating housing. Don’t forget that these major housing laws narrowly passed. It’s not inconceivable that the Legislature could move the other direction next session.

REPRO FUNDING WANTED: Expect reproductive health care to be a major budget issue this year.

Last year we thought the reproductive health question was whether Newsom would reup the $200 million in new abortion funding he spent in response to 2022’s Dobbs decision. Turns out the issues were much more existential. Federal family planning grants have been pulled, Planned Parenthood clinics around the state have closed and Congressional Republicans are debating further restrictions. Instead of the expansive new programs from a few years ago, expect the repro world to be lobbying for money to keep clinic doors open.

TECH BATTLES INCOMING: The fight of artificial intelligence regulation is far from over.

If anything, we underestimated AI’s impact, judging by the amount of late nights our new California Decoded team pulled this year. Sen. Scott Wiener brought (most) tech giants together to pass his internationally-watched AI transparency bill. Washington was watching, too, and it helped spark Trump’s ongoing fight to preempt state AI rules. Back home, the political debate around AI chatbots exploded amid a series of high-profile wrongful death lawsuits implicating OpenAI’s flagship model ChatGPT. OpenAI and kids’ safety groups might even take chatbot controls to the ballot in 2026 — a mark of how quickly the issue went mainstream. Don’t even get us started on data centers, which are shaping up to be a serious issue in next year’s midterms.

EXPECT MORE RECALLS: After a series of successful recall campaigns, voters across the state will try to take their frustrations with elected officials to the ballot.

Although there were a handful of recalls across California last year (notably, successful efforts to remove San Francisco Supervisor Joel Engardio and the sheriff of San Mateo County, as well as a failed attempt in Fairfax), no serious statewide campaigns materialized. Instead, it was Proposition 50’s redistricting that ultimately gave voters the power to target politicians, installing new congressional maps that will leave Republican representatives fighting for their political lives.

TOP READS

FROM MARIN TO MODOC COUNTY — We sent Will McCarthy to drive 15 hours across the state, traversing mountain ranges and rock slides, dirt roads and agricultural checkpoints, from Marin to Modoc County — a nearly 700-mile ramble through California’s most egregiously gerrymandered new district.

Perhaps the most extreme example of the newly enshrined gerrymander is CA-02, Will writes, a massive, hatchet or flag-shaped district that links Marin, one of the wealthiest counties in the country, with the remote, isolated high desert of northeastern California. It is million-dollar mansions in Tiburon, empty beaches in Sonoma, struggling lumber mills in Siskiyou, healing rocks in Mt. Shasta, and simply sand and sky and silence. There is little uniting these communities politically, economically, climactically, culturally, historically — or even symbolically. But as a result of Prop 50, they will all share the same representative in Congress. And that representative, in all likelihood, will be a Democrat.

While Prop 50 has been trumpeted as a victory for Democrats and democracy, it was also, fundamentally, a decision to further distance vast stretches of the state from political representation. Far-flung places that couldn’t feel more different from urban centers like Marin will now, at least geographically, make up the vast majority of the district.

CAMPAIGN YEAR(S)

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: WAITING IN THE WINGS — Santa Clara County District Attorney Jeff Rosen is considering running for attorney general if incumbent Rob Bonta enters the governor’s race, according to an ally granted anonymity to discuss Rosen’s planning.

Rosen could fill a progressive lane, with his history criticizing the tough-on-crime Proposition 36 and working with Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas on competing retail theft proposals. Part of California’s criminal justice reform movement, Rosen has also sought resentencing for death row inmates and expungement of some low level drug crimes since he took office in 2011.

Rosen is one of more than a dozen Democrats who’ve been floated to replace Bonta, meaning a succession campaign would likely be crowded.

CAN HE SEE SOME I.D.? — Prominent conservative donor Richard Uihlein made a massive donation to a California voter I.D. ballot initiative, buoying the chances the measure makes the 2026 ballot and outlining the national stakes. Midwestern shipping supply magnate, Uihlein reported a $4 million donation, by far the campaign’s largest to date. Uihlein is a prolific donor to Trump and Republicans. — Jeremy B. White

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: SPEAKER’S BLESSING — Former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi endorsed Lt. Gov. Eleni Kounalakis for state treasurer in a video released this morning. Pelosi’s nod comes as little surprise given she previously backed Kounalakis for governor (before she pivoted races) and the two are longtime friends from the well-heeled world of Pacific Heights. But it could help Kounalakis, who’s raised close to $9 million, solidify her dominance in the treasurer’s race.

“Eleni has really been a leader all her life,” Pelosi said in the video. “People know that when you hear the name ‘Eleni,’ it is synonymous with great values, vision and love of the people.”

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: IN THE TRENCHES — Josh Fryday, a former Naval officer and Democrat running for lieutenant governor, is leaning into his military record in the race, arguing Democrats have a self-inflicted liability due to the party overlooking the needs of veterans and the military.

“Too often Democrats lack credibility on military and national security issues,” Fryday told Playbook. “As President Trump continues to abuse the military to intimidate the public and for political gain, we need to elect veterans with the authority to stand up and push back.”

Fryday — who served at Guantanamo Bay (and testified to the Senate about abuses there) and in Japan during the Fukushima nuclear accident — will today unveil the endorsement of VetVotes. The group one is one of the nation’s largest progressive PACs focused on the needs of veterans.

Fryday, who works as the chief service officer in Newsom’s administration, said he would leverage the lieutenant governor’s office to expand a state jobs corps program that helps veterans and military families find jobs when they return to civilian life. Another proposal would expand an initiative to help veterans get college credit for their service.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: EARLY MONEY — Meanwhile, a person familiar with Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel’s fundraising operation said he has raised almost $900,000 in the week since he opened a campaign finance account for an attorney general race that has not yet started.

Gabriel, chair of the Assembly Budget Committee, also has more than $2.9 million in his legislative campaign account, which he could tap into if he runs to be the state’s top cop. — Nicole Norman

GOVERNOR'S RACE

SWALWELL THE PRODUCER — Rep. Eric Swalwell is coming under fire from some labor unions over his connection to a film production where the crew has gone on strike over workplace conditions and compensation disputes.

Swalwell, a Democratic gubernatorial contender, is listed as the executive producer of the film, “The Gun on Second Street,” an independent crime drama filming in West Virginia. The crew went on strike Thursday, and the International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees called upon its union members not to cross the picket line.

The representative, in a statement, downplayed his involvement. He said he shared details of his “family’s law enforcement experiences with the writer” and was offered an executive producer credit in exchange.

“I never personally invested,” Swalwell said. “I have also been a lifelong supporter of labor. While I am prohibited by Congressional ethics rules from participating in the negotiations between production and labor, I joined the film expecting that labor standards would be adhered to and urge the production team to seek a fair outcome for the crew. Should that not be achieved, I will remove my name from the film’s screen credits.”

But Lindsay Dougherty, the head of Teamsters Local 399, which represents more than 6,000 workers in Hollywood, suggested Swalwell should pay a price. Teamsters California has endorsed former Rep. Katie Porter in the race.

“We want candidates that are going to protect working people, and this is not an example of that,” Dougherty told Playbook. “This is not helping our Hollywood industry in California, either. I don’t see how this is a positive for him at all.”

REPUBLICANLAND

TRUMP BUMP — Trump has intervened in Republicans’ bid to oust frontline Rep. Adam Gray, endorsing former Stockton Mayor Kevin Lincoln in a TruthSocial post.

“As your next Congressman, Kevin will work tirelessly to Grow our Economy, Cut Taxes and Regulations, Promote MADE IN THE U.S.A., Champion American Energy DOMINANCE, Keep our now very Secure Border, SECURE, Stop Migrant Crime, Strengthen our Great Military/Veterans, and Protect our always under siege Second Amendment,” Trump wrote on Thursday.

House Speaker Mike Johnson followed the president’s lead, endorsing Lincoln in a move poised to consolidate the Republican base in a GOP priority race. Gray won the current version of his district by fewer than 200 votes last cycle, and though it will swing from a district Trump carried to one Kamala Harris won under Democrats’ gerrymander, she carried it by less than a percentage point.

Both parties amplified the Trump endorsement. A spokesperson for the National Republican Campaign Committee celebrated the nod as evidence of Trump’s faith in Lincoln the “fighter who puts California families first,” while a Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee spokesperson accused Lincoln of “proving he answers to his Washington party bosses over the Central Valley.”

HEALTHCARE

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: HOLDING THE LINE — A coalition of LGBTQ+ advocacy groups will send a letter to California hospitals and health care providers today, urging them to not comply with the Trump administration’s demands that they stop providing gender-affirming care to transgender minors or face massive funding cuts.

The groups argue that California law requires hospitals to provide health care access to transgender people, asserting that Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s proposed rules to ban such care cannot override the state’s policies.

“Even if the proposed … rules were finalized, medical care for transgender young people would remain legal and protected in California, and healthcare providers would continue to be bound by California’s nondiscrimination protections,” the letter states.

Signatories on the letter include Equality California, the Los Angeles LGBT Center, ACLU California Action and TransFamily Support Services.
