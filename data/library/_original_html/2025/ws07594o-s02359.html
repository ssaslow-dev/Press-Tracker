California Glock Ban Explained: What AB 1127 Really Does

Casey Hartwell

The Bottom Line

California’s AB 1127, signed by Governor Gavin Newsom on October 10, 2025 and effective July 1, 2026, bars licensed firearm dealers from selling Glock-style “machinegun-convertible pistols.” It does not ban owning a Glock you already have, and it does not legalize the illegal switches it was written to fight. California’s own legislative analysis admitted the bill’s practical effect is to prohibit the sale of Glock handguns in the state.

You won’t find the word “Glock” anywhere in AB 1127. The people who wrote it were careful about that. What they put in instead is a definition built so tightly around the Glock pistol that California’s own Senate analysts admitted, in writing, that the practical effect of the bill is to prohibit the sale of Glock handguns in California.

It didn’t come from a gun-lobby press release. It came from the Legislature’s own committee staff, describing what their bill would do, before they voted to pass it.

Gun owners just call it the Glock ban. Fair enough. But the nickname buries the part that should bother you, and it should bother you whether you carry a Glock or have never picked one up. California didn’t go after the illegal little gadget that turns a pistol into a machine gun. That gadget has been a felony for years. The state went after the ordinary, legal pistol underneath it.

That choice is the whole story. It’s also why this fight is nowhere near finished.

The Short Version

  • What passed: AB 1127, signed by Gov. Gavin Newsom on October 10, 2025 (Chapter 572 of the 2025 statutes). It cleared the Legislature on a party-line vote, every Republican against it.
  • When it bites: The dealer-sale ban kicks in July 1, 2026.
  • Who it hits: California gun dealers and anyone trying to buy a new affected pistol from one. It does not reach into your safe.
  • What it does not do: It doesn’t ban owning an unmodified Glock you already have, it doesn’t legalize switches, and it doesn’t make anybody surrender a gun.
  • Why it matters: California is restricting a common, legal pistol based on what a criminal might illegally bolt onto it. That logic is the real headline.
  • What’s in court: Gun-rights groups sued in Jaymes v. Bonta, then dropped the case in April 2026. Now the U.S. Department of Justice is threatening its own federal lawsuit.
  • Watch this: A June 30 federal deadline, a possible DOJ suit, dealer guidance from the state, and whether Glock ever redesigns for California.

What AB 1127 Actually Says

Starting July 1, 2026, a licensed California dealer cannot sell, trade, give, transfer, or hand over any “semiautomatic machinegun-convertible pistol.” That ban gets written into the Penal Code at section 27595, and breaking it costs dealers real money.

So what fits the phrase? The law says a semiautomatic pistol with a cruciform trigger bar that someone could turn into a machine gun by swapping a small converter onto the back of the slide, no gunsmithing required. In plain terms, it targets the internal part that a Glock switch happens to grab. We’re not going to walk anybody through how that conversion actually works, and you don’t need to know it to follow the law.

Definition

Cruciform trigger bar

The cross-shaped internal part inside Glock and Glock-pattern pistols that connects the trigger to the firing mechanism. AB 1127 uses the presence of this part as its legal trigger. Striker-fired pistols built without it, like the Sig P320 and Smith & Wesson M&P, are not covered.

The sleight of hand is in that definition. The state pinned the everyday pistol to an illegal part it could theoretically accept, then restricted the everyday pistol.

And the whole thing runs on dealers to enforce it. First slip, a fine. Second, your license can get suspended. Third, you’re looking at a misdemeanor and losing the license for good. For a small-shop FFL, the federally licensed dealer who runs your local gun store, that’s the difference between keeping the lights on and locking the doors.

Is This Really a Glock Ban?

Not by brand name, but in practice, yes. The bill never types out “Glock,” and on its face it covers any pistol that meets the cruciform definition. But the Senate’s own analysis didn’t bother dancing around it. Because the convertible pistols in question are Glocks, the staff wrote, the practical effect is to stop Glock sales in California.

They even did the math. 54 Glock models were sitting on the state’s approved handgun roster at the time, and every one of them would fall off the menu for regular buyers.

It catches the knockoffs too. Glock-pattern clones built on the same guts, like Shadow Systems pistols, get swept up right alongside the real thing.

What it does not touch is just as telling. Striker-fired pistols that skip the cruciform trigger bar are in the clear. Your Sig P320, your S&W M&P, at least the rostered versions, sail through untouched. So this isn’t “California banned plastic pistols,” and it isn’t “California banned one brand.” It’s a law written around a single internal part that just so happens to live inside the most popular handgun in the country.

What the Law Does NOT Do

Here’s the part the internet keeps butchering, so slow down. The quick version:

What AB 1127 Does

  • Bans dealer sale of “machinegun-convertible pistols” starting July 1, 2026
  • Targets pistols with a cruciform trigger bar (most Glocks and Glock clones)
  • Puts escalating penalties on dealers, up to losing the license
  • Stacks a new restriction on top of California’s handgun roster
  • Offers a redesign-and-recertify path back onto the roster

What It Does NOT Do

  • Ban possession of a Glock you already own
  • Legalize switches or converters (still illegal)
  • Force anybody to surrender a pistol
  • Stop private-party transfers through a dealer
  • Apply to non-cruciform striker pistols (Sig P320, S&W M&P)

A few of those deserve a sentence more. Switches were a serious crime before this bill and they’re a serious crime after it. AB 1127 stacked a restriction on the gun on top of that. It didn’t trade one for the other. And the exceptions list is long: private-party sales run through a dealer, repairs at a gunsmith, transfers to other dealers or out-of-state makers, guns coming back to you after storage or a temporary hold, forensic-lab transfers, police and military sales, peace-officer purchases, and a narrow allowance for inventory a dealer already had before January 1, 2026. For a normal person walking up to a counter, the only date that matters is July 1, 2026.

Definition

California handgun roster

The state’s list of handguns approved for new commercial sale. If a pistol isn’t on the roster, dealers can’t sell it new. AB 1127 strikes affected Glock-pattern models off that roster, which is why the practical effect lands on dealer sales rather than on ownership.

Who Actually Gets Hit

If you already own one, your week doesn’t change. Shoot it, clean it, take it to a gunsmith, buy a case of ammo for it. None of that gets touched.

If you’re trying to buy one new, that’s where the wall goes up. Come July 1, 2026, you can’t walk into a California shop and buy a new affected Glock-pattern pistol off the rack, unless a court has stepped in first.

Carry a Glock on your CCW? The one on your hip is fine. The squeeze is on the next gun. A brand-new permit holder, or somebody who wants to swap to a different carry pistol a few years down the road, loses easy access to the platform half the instructors in this state train on.

Dealers eat the risk and the paperwork. They have to know which guns are affected, move what they can before the deadline, and watch a real chunk of their handgun business get walled off. The penalties land on them, not on Sacramento.

Cops are exempt. Sworn officers and certain agencies can still get these pistols, so duty carry rolls right along. The Senate analysis even pointed out that the exemption list left off some agencies whose officers carry Glocks, which tells you how clean the line-drawing really was.

Glock and the other makers get a choice. Redesign for California, or sit out new dealer sales in the third-biggest gun market in the country.

The used market stays open. Person-to-person transfers through a dealer are an exception, so private sales keep happening. Just expect the price tags to climb as the new-gun supply dries up. Off-roster Glocks already trade at a stiff premium out here. This pours gas on that.

And that brings us to hunters, the part you might be tempted to scroll past.

The State’s Case

Give California’s side a real hearing, because it isn’t nothing.

Switches are ugly business. A little illegal part, some of them printed on a cheap 3D printer, can turn a pistol into something that dumps a magazine in a couple of seconds. That’s not a hypothetical. It has shown up in shooting after shooting, with real people dead. Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who carried the bill, was blunt about his goal. He wants this law to force Glock into changing the design so the switch has nothing to grab. He has said the company has known about the weak point for years and could save lives by fixing it.

The pitch is prevention. If this is the platform criminals keep exploiting, lean on the company to redesign the platform. And for the families who have buried somebody killed by a converted Glock, none of this is a policy abstraction. A mother in Sacramento lost her son when a gunman opened up with a switched pistol in a downtown nightclub, eleven rounds in a few seconds, and she has spent her grief at the capitol pushing for exactly this kind of law. Pretending that loss isn’t part of the engine here would be dishonest.

The Other Side

Now the opposition, and their argument is almost insultingly simple.

The switch is already illegal. Making one, selling one, owning one, all of it was already a felony before AB 1127 added a single comma. So the law doesn’t criminalize anything new about the dangerous part. What it restricts is the boring part, the everyday pistol that millions of people who’ve never broken a law in their lives happen to own.

From there it snowballs. You’re punishing a common, legal firearm for what some criminal might do to it. You’re squeezing ordinary buyers and the dealers who serve them, while the guy who actually wants a switched pistol is going to find one on the street no matter what the roster says. And you’re setting a precedent with teeth: a gun can be restricted not for what it is, but for what somebody could illegally turn it into.

That last point is the one worth losing sleep over, and not because of Glocks. Once “this gun could theoretically be modified into something illegal” is enough to justify banning its sale, that reasoning doesn’t stay parked on one pistol. It can be aimed at the next gun, and the one after that.

A common, legal gun, restricted not for what it is but for what a criminal might turn it into. That’s the precedent, and precedents get reused.

The gun-rights crowd figures the Constitution is on its side, too. The Supreme Court has said arms in common use by regular people for legal reasons get strong protection, and that handguns in particular can’t be banned. A Glock is about as “common use” as a firearm gets in America. That’s the backbone of the legal challenge. It’s an advocacy position, and I’m flagging it as one, but it’s built on actual Supreme Court rulings, not wishful thinking.

Why Hunters and Outdoorsmen Should Care

Nobody’s jump-shooting mallards with a 9mm. A Glock isn’t a duck gun, an elk gun, or an upland gun, and I’m not going to pretend it is. So why should a guy who lives for the teal opener care about a handgun law out of Sacramento?

Because this was never really about one pistol. It’s about who gets to decide which legal guns you’re allowed to buy, in the biggest and most copied gun market in the country.

Start with plain access. Hunters live and die by a healthy, legal market for the guns and gear we use. A law that bans a common firearm over a crime that hasn’t even happened yet is not a principle that knows how to stay in its lane. Today the trigger is a cruciform bar. The logic is the part that travels.

Then there’s the sidearm question, which is a lot more real for sportsmen than the duck-gun line lets on. Plenty of us carry a handgun in the field. Bears and hogs, two-legged trouble at a dark trailhead, or just the basic fact of being armed where you live and hunt. Glock-pattern pistols are a default backcountry and carry choice, and for good reason. Choke off that option through the front door and you shrink what’s practical for anybody who actually packs a sidearm into the marsh or up the mountain.

There’s your local shop, too. Pile legal risk and red tape onto handgun sales and you’re leaning on the same FFL who stocks your nontox loads, your slugs, your glass, and your shotguns. A gun store bleeding margin on a third of its business is not a gun store that’s good for any of us.

And here’s the one that ought to hit home for every sportsman, because it’s not abstract at all. The whole conservation-funding machine in this country runs in large part on Pittman-Robertson, the excise tax on firearms, ammunition, and gear that quietly bankrolls habitat, wildlife management, and the access we all hunt on. The Congressional Sportsmen’s Foundation has been waving the flag about this exact thing. Kneecap firearm sales in a giant market like California and you drive a wedge straight into the money that pays for ducks, deer, and dirt. Fewer legal guns sold can mean fewer conservation dollars in the ground. That’s your wildlife areas, your boat ramps, your habitat projects, all of it downstream.

Last one, and it’s the soft kind that ends up mattering more than people admit. Hunting and lawful gun ownership grew up in the same house. Same roots, same critics, same fate. Once the culture decides common firearms are guilty until proven innocent, hunting is never far behind in that line.

You don’t have to own a Glock, like Glocks, or care one bit about Glocks to see the trap. A rule that lets the state wall off a legal gun over what someone might illegally do to it does not stop at Glocks. It has a long reach. Sooner or later it reaches the guns in your safe too.

Key Takeaways

The Five Things to Remember

  • It’s law, and it’s real. Signed October 10, 2025. The dealer-sale ban starts July 1, 2026.
  • It’s a sales ban, not a possession ban. Keep the Glock you already own. Private transfers through a dealer are still allowed.
  • It targets a part, not a brand. The trigger is the cruciform trigger bar, but the practical effect, by California’s own admission, is killing Glock sales in the state.
  • The fight leveled up. Private groups sued, then dropped it during talks. Now the DOJ is threatening a federal lawsuit, with a June 30 deadline for California to answer.
  • The precedent is the point. Restricting a common, legal gun over a hypothetical illegal modification is a template, and templates get reused.

The Legal Fight

The courtroom story comes in two acts, and act two is breaking right now.

Act one was the private lawsuit. Three days after Newsom signed, on October 13, 2025, a coalition of gun-rights heavyweights sued in federal court in San Diego. Jaymes v. Bonta put the NRA, the Firearms Policy Coalition, the Second Amendment Foundation, the San Diego retailer Poway Weapons and Gear, and a couple of individual gun owners on the same complaint. Their argument was the common-use one: Glock-pattern handguns are everywhere and legal, the Constitution protects common arms, and you can’t ban the sale of them.

It never reached a verdict. The two sides started talking, the judge paused the case on March 27, 2026, and then the plaintiffs themselves asked the court to toss it, which the judge did on April 13, 2026. A voluntary dismissal like that usually leaves the door cracked to refile, so it closed the round without closing the fight.

Worth knowing the backdrop here. California’s broader handgun roster, the list that decides which new pistols a dealer can sell at all, is already getting hammered in separate cases like Boland v. Bonta and Renna v. Bonta. In Boland, a federal judge actually struck down the roster’s feature mandates as unconstitutional, and then that ruling got stayed while the state appeals, so the roster is still standing for now. AB 1127 basically bolted a fresh restriction onto a system that was already in court.

Act two is the federal government, and this is the live wire. On June 24, 2026, Harmeet Dhillon, who runs the DOJ’s Civil Rights Division, sent Newsom and Attorney General Rob Bonta a formal notice of suit. She gave California until 5 p.m. Eastern on June 30 to say whether it wants to negotiate. If the state stays quiet, the letter says, the United States may just file its complaint with no further warning.

Definition

“Pattern or practice” (34 U.S.C. § 12601)

A federal civil-rights tool normally aimed at police departments accused of systemic misconduct. By framing California’s firearm enforcement this way, the DOJ is treating the state’s gun laws as a civil-rights violation against lawful gun owners, which is an unusually aggressive posture.

And the federal posture is not gentle. It goes after both AB 1127 and the handgun roster, and it frames California’s enforcement as a “pattern or practice” of misconduct. The demands reportedly run all the way to making California stop enforcing the laws, admit they’re unconstitutional, sign a court-enforceable agreement never to pass anything like them again, and preserve every related record. As of this writing, Bonta hasn’t said a word in public.

So the people lined up against this law have changed. Round one was advocacy groups with good lawyers. Round two, if it comes, is the United States of America.

What to Watch Next

  • The June 30 deadline. Whether California negotiates, ignores the DOJ, or gets sued is the single biggest near-term domino.
  • State dealer guidance. Watch for the California DOJ to hand FFLs compliance instructions as July 1 closes in. The fine print on inventory and exceptions matters enormously to anybody behind a counter.
  • Glock’s move. There’s been chatter about a possible California-compliant redesign, sometimes floated as a “V” series, but Glock hasn’t publicly committed to a clear plan and the details are thin. If the company does redesign and re-certify, the law has a built-in path to put modified models back on the roster, with a testing window running into early 2027.
  • Roster cases. Movement in Boland or Renna could reshape what’s even sellable in California, AB 1127 or not.
  • What dealers do with their stock. How shops handle remaining affected inventory before and after the deadline will drive both availability and price.
  • The private-transfer reading. Expect heavy use of the dealer-facilitated private-sale exception, and watch how it actually gets applied.
  • New lawsuits. A dismissed case can come back, and a federal suit would open a brand-new front.
  • Copycats. Lawmakers in Maryland, Connecticut, and New York have already run with similar measures. If California’s survives, more states pile on.

Final Takeaway

Let me be square about what this fight is, because the spin runs both directions.

It is not a fight over whether switches are dangerous. They are. Full stop. A converter turns a handgun into something no private citizen has any business firing, it’s already illegal six ways from Sunday, and nobody honest is arguing otherwise.

The fight is over a different and much bigger question. Can the government restrict common, legal pistols, owned by millions of people who’ve done nothing wrong, because some criminal might illegally modify them? California said yes. The gun-rights groups said no. And now the Department of Justice is saying no with the full weight of the federal government behind it.

July 1, 2026 was supposed to be the day this law settled in for good. It’s shaping up to be the day the real fight starts.
