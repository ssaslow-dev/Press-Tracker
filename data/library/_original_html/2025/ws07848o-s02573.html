California Dems get health care heartburn

By LINDSEY HOLDEN

SLASH AND BURN: California Democrats are stressed about funding health care for the state’s poorest residents — a growing crisis made worse by federal cuts that now seem all but certain.

Lawmakers today agreed to provide a $2.8 billion lifeline for Medi-Cal, the state’s version of the federal Medicaid program, which is running out of money because of high enrollment numbers, my colleague Eric He reports for Pro subscribers. Meanwhile, House Republicans approved a budget framework, setting the stage for safety-net program slashing.

Less federal money would intensify a state health care spending problem caused by increased pharmaceutical expenses, greater senior enrollment and an expansion of coverage for undocumented immigrants. With about a month until Gov. Gavin Newsom presents his revised May spending plan, lawmakers are already seeing the writing on the budgetary wall.

Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel seized on what he views as the unfairness of California residents sending their tax dollars to fund programs in other states.

“It is not right that money from California taxpayers can be taken and spent in other communities,” he said. “Then we’re going to have to be in a position to contemplate really terrible choices for health programs and services that we want to provide for Californians.”

Democrats today also defended their push to provide insurance for undocumented Californians against GOP pushback just before leaving for a weeklong spring recess. Republicans tried to hold their ground.

“All of us support Medi-Cal — it is the most important health care program for the neediest among us,” said Assemblymember Carl DeMaio, a San Diego Republican. “But all of their health coverage has been put at risk because Governor Gavin Newsom and this Legislature decided to grant a gift of public funds to illegal immigrants to cover their health insurance at taxpayer expense.”

An exasperated Gabriel was rubbing his face as soon as he walked onto the floor, later saying he just wanted to go home and see his kids.

But the assemblymember will likely come back from his break to even more financial woes. California could sustain disproportionate damage from President Donald Trump’s tariffs, which have proved to be unpredictable and appear dependent on the president’s whims.

And even if congressional Republicans preserve some federal health care dollars, California leaders will need to substantially rethink the state’s Medi-Cal system.

As Eric reports, Newsom’s administration today acknowledged the cost overruns in an Assembly Budget Committee hearing before the legislative vote.

Erika Li from the Department of Finance called the situation “unsustainable” and said her agency is “looking at ways to curb those costs into the budget year.”

DEM DISSENTION: A group of Democrats today opposed a resolution from Assemblymember Tri Ta honoring California civil rights hero Sylvia Mendez and called Republicans hypocritical for backing the measure while their party fights DEI initiatives.

Mendez was barred from attending a segregated school in Westminster in the 1940s, and her court case helped pave the way for future civil rights victories.

Ta, an Orange County Republican, previously served as mayor of Westminster. Last year, he authored a bill pushing for California schools to include her case in social studies curriculum.

But Assemblymember Corey Jackson and other Democrats couldn’t stomach Republicans bringing the resolution, asking members to hold off on voting for it. Some said they wanted to honor Mendez, but only with a new resolution.

“It’s time for us to send a message, colleagues,” Jackson said. “It’s time for us to understand that boundaries matter. And it’s time for us to make sure that we do not deem it acceptable to use people who sacrificed on their own — for their people, for their communities — for political points.”

Assemblymember Ash Kalra said GOP members were presenting a “contradiction” by “putting forth the resolution in recognition of a movement that they now want to erase from history.”

Jackson and Assemblymembers Mia Bonta and Alex Lee objected to a voice vote on the resolution, instead asking for a roll call that put members’ decisions on the record.

When the dust had settled, Bonta and Lee were the only lawmakers to register “no” votes, although Jackson and a handful of other Democrats declined to vote.

PALISADES DETENTE: Call it the Redick Accords. JJ Redick, head coach of the Los Angeles Lakers, orchestrated an unusual sight today: Mayor Karen Bass and her once (and possibly future) rival Rick Caruso, smiling side-by-side.

The occasion was the announcement of a new public-private partnership between the city, Caruso’s nonprofit group Steadfast LA and Redick’s own sports-focused initiative to rebuild the Palisades Recreation Center, which was destroyed in January’s historic wildfires.

It was the first time the political foes had been together in public since they faced off on the debate stage during their 2022 mayoral race and the vibes were mostly collegial, with the pair sharing multiple handshakes, a side hug and brief utterances of praise for each other.

After the unveiling of the ambitious rebuilding plans — which would be privately financed on city-owned lands — Bass said her appearance with Caruso, the billionaire developer who has been harshly critical of her leadership, could be summed up by a simple credo: Keeping her “eyes on the prize.”

“The goal is restoring and healing this community. There is nothing to me more important than that, and so I think it is appropriate for us to come together in a public-private partnership. That’s the best way to get things done,” Bass said, praising Caruso’s “outstanding” contribution to the recovery effort.

Caruso, for his part, said that he and Bass are “genuinely friendly” and there’s been no friction in his nonprofit working with the city on recovery efforts. But he still criticized the recovery effort for moving too slowly, regardless of the city hitting certain milestones — such as the clearing of toxic debris and restoring utilities — at a faster rate than what happened in the aftermath of previous major fires.

“Those are failures,” Caruso said of those past recovery efforts. “That would be like if your kid comes home and says, ‘hey Dad, I got a D but everyone else in school got an F.’ Well, that’s nothing to celebrate.” — Melanie Mason

SIX-FIGURE SLAP: The Fair Political Practices Commission quickly and unanimously voted to impose one of its largest penalties ever on a former elected official — ex-Assemblymember Evan Low. It approved the $106,000 fine this morning in response to several campaign finance violations that the enforcement division assistant chief Angela Brereton described as “egregious.” Low had already agreed to the settlement.

Chair Adam Silver recused himself from the 3-0 vote, following a previous promise to abstain from votes on issues regarding the Assembly — where he used to be chief council for the Ethics Committee. — Blake Jones

TARIFF TROUBLES: California has a lot riding on Trump’s ever-changing tariff moves, our Jeremy B. White, Christine Mui, Camille von Kaenel and Alex Nieves report today.

Major industries that power the nation’s largest economy — from almond growers counting on foreign buyers to Silicon Valley giants reliant on Chinese components — are still trembling from Trump’s shifting trade policies. The economic uncertainty also threatens to tank the stock market, drying up state tax revenue for schools and health care.

“It’s really bad,” said Lenny Mendonca, a former top economic adviser to Newsom. “The United States is the biggest beneficiary of global trade and California’s more dependent on international activity than the country. Take the country, multiply it by 20 or 25 percent, that’s what’s going to happen to California.”
