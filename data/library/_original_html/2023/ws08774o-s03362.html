REDDING, Calif. — A controversial proposed gun tax in California is headed for Governor Gavin Newsom’s desk.

Assembly Bill 28, spearheaded by Democratic Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, would impose an 11% excise tax on retailers and manufacturers for the sale of guns or ammunition within the state. The bill aims to further curb gun violence, with the proposed tax bringing in an estimated $160 million annually. That money would be spent on violence intervention programs, improving safety at schools, plus law enforcement efforts to take guns away from people who are prohibited from owning them.

In the last decade, California lawmakers have attempted several other sales or excise taxes on guns and ammunition; all of them failed, with some never getting a hearing. With AB-28 approved by state legislature, it appears destined to become law—much to the dismay of local gun shops, who are already taxed on both the state and federal level.

"It’ll be a burden," said Shasta County Supervisor Patrick Jones, whose family have operated 'Jones Fort' Gun Shop in Redding since 1962. "Small businesses already struggle with the taxes that we pay now. So, this will be a very large tax, obviously focused on gun dealers, which [there] are very few in California. It’ll make the cost of firearms go up and it [will] reduce, really, the number of sales, which obviously won’t be good for us or any other dealer."

"It’s, again, overreach from the state of California. This is a tyrannical law [and] the money isn’t going to do what they say it will do, it never does," Jones added.

KRCR also spoke with employees at Redding Guns in downtown Redding. They called AB-28 "simply wrong" and say it infringes on a person's ability to exercise their second amendment right.

"All they [Democrats] are doing is pandering to their base," said employee Moe Modin. "Why are gun owners responsible for all the school safety? Everybody should be responsible for that." Both Moe and his coworker, Randy, feel confident the bill will be challenged in court.

Governor Newsom has until Oct. 14 to sign this bill into law. Right now, it’s uncertain what he will do, and his office has declined to comment on the bill thus far. If the bill is passed, it would take effect on July 1, 2024.
