Assembly Public Safety committee faces pressure for blocking fentanyl, rape and other crime bills

SACRAMENTO, Calif. —

The Chairman of the California Assembly Public Safety Committee, Assemblymember Reggie Jones-Sawyer, is responding to the mounting pressure his committee is facing after his committee decided to block or stall several crime-related bills.

This week, both Republicans and Democrats have held or are scheduled to hold news conferences to speak out against the committee for not taking action on fentanyl.

Recently, Jones-Sawyer said he decided to hold fentanyl-related legislation from moving forward in order to have a hearing on the issue later this year, possibly in June.

"The bills aren't being held up, they weren't making it through and I wanted to make sure they didn't get killed or die," Jones-Sawyer told KCRA 3 on Wednesday in a sit-down interview. Jones-Sawyer has the power the make recommendations on legislation, and most of the fentanyl-related measures had received a NO recommendation from him prior to the effort to hold a special hearing on the issue.

The committee, which is made up of mostly progressive Democrats, has been known for blocking penalty enhancements broadly for fear of mass incarceration and the potential disproportionate impacts on Black and brown communities.

The committee has also recently been criticized for favoritism after approving a progressive Democrat’s bill that enhances penalties on high-dollar property crimes (AB 484), while rejecting numerous measures to boost penalties for domestic violence, rape of a developmentally disabled child, rape of an unconscious person, human trafficking, and the use of a gun during serious crimes, among other issues.

Jones-Sawyer said all of the measures are up for reconsideration but said the lawmakers who have written the rejected bills have not spoken to him about making any changes, a claim some of those bill authors rebuffed Wednesday afternoon.

The rejected measures have been written by Republicans and moderate Democrats, which, public committee hearings show, get different treatment than their progressive counterparts.

The chairman told KCRA 3 he approved AB 484 because the progressive Democratic lawmaker, Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, was willing to work with the committee prior to the bill's first hearing. He said Republicans were not willing to do the same.

A spokesman for Assemblymember Bill Essayli, R-Riverside, said the committee didn't provide feedback or guidance on AB 328, which would have restored harsher penalties for firing a gun in the process of a violent crime including murder and rape.

The same was the case with AB 1378, a bipartisan measure that would make the violation of a criminal protective order punishable as either a felony or misdemeanor, adding stricter punishments for violators armed with a firearm.

Jones-Sawyer's committee for years has also blocked numerous attempts to change or toss parts of Proposition 47, the voter-approved law that changed how property crimes and retail thefts are prosecuted in the state. The latest bill to stall in the committee on that issue, AB 1708 by Democratic Assemblymember Al Muratsuchi.

"They think we should go back to incarcerating," Jones-Sawyer said. "We've reduced the prison population, which is expensive and ruins lives, and the best thing to do is rehabilitation and the best way to deal with it is to stop crime at the beginning. Mr. Muratsuchi's ultimate goal was not that."

Jones-Sawyer said getting to the root of a lot of these issues will require everyone to work together, including law enforcement, advocates, and lawmakers from both sides of the aisle.

But committee hearings show that won't be easy, based on how some of the Democratic members of the committee have treated Republicans who present their bills.

For example, when Republican Assemblymember Devon Mathis presented AB 808, which would enhance penalties for people who rape a developmentally disabled child, Democratic Assemblymember Liz Ortega criticized him for not meeting with immigrant communities on a separate housing issue, which had nothing to do with the legislation.

Another example includes Democratic Assemblymember Mia Bonta refusing to grant reconsideration to Essayli's AB 328 because he included a San Francisco Chronicle article on gun violence in Oakland as part of his presentation. After this hearing, multiple sources told KCRA 3 Jones-Sawyer advised some legislators to avoid bringing up another member's district in presentations.

"Most importantly, we've got to take the temperature down," Jones-Sawyer said in response to these examples.
