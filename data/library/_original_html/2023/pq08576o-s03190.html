California’s less-ballyhooed corporate climate law

By Jordan Wolman; Blanca Begert

With help from Anne C. Mulkern and Heather Richards

SETTING UP THE FEDS — We’ve written a LOT about SB 253, the bill Gov. Gavin Newsom signed in October to require large companies to disclose their greenhouse gas emissions.

We haven’t focused as much on another first-in-the-nation bill he signed to improve the quality of companies’ existing voluntary climate disclosures.

AB 1305, by Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D-Encino), is going to require all companies operating in California to publish the number and type of voluntary carbon offsets they buy and sell. They’ll have to disclose whether the offsets are being used to meet corporate net-zero commitments and whether the offsets have been verified by a third party. Policy wonks and Gabriel alike are saying the bill could juice the Federal Trade Commission’s ability to take action against bogus environmental claims, much in the same way that Sen. Scott Wiener’s (D-San Francisco) SB 253 could make it easier for the Securities and Exchange Commission to require national disclosure of companies’ entire carbon footprints. Companies posting their carbon-neutrality claims online, as the law requires, will provide the feds with fodder for enforcement under their existing Green Guides rules, which cover corporate environmental marketing claims. “The FTC could go and look at that, and if it didn’t look like there were enough plans in place or they didn’t provide the protocol that they’re using to estimate carbon emissions, or something like that, that could be a reason for the FTC to open an investigation,” said Mary Engle, the executive vice president of policy at BBB National Programs and the former director of the FTC’s advertising practices division.

One of the reasons we haven’t written about this as much yet, though, is there’s not much in the way of enforcement on the state level.

The law generally puts the state attorney general and local district or city attorneys in charge, with fines of up to $500,000 for noncompliance or inaccurate information.

But it doesn’t even say when it would take effect. Gabriel took the unusual step of writing a clarifying letter to the Assembly clerk last month saying that he wants the law to kick in Jan. 1, 2025. (That’s not binding, though.)

Gabriel said it’s OK.

“I don’t think you need a particularly big stick here to induce compliance,” he told Jordan. “I think a lot of folks are just, because of the nature of their business, going to want to be in compliance with the law because the possibility of enforcement exists.”

The FTC declined to comment.

Another signal this might not go much further within California: While he signed AB 1305, Newsom vetoed another bill in October, SB 390, that would have applied California’s false advertising laws to the carbon offset industry, saying he didn’t want to risk “creating significant turmoil in the market for carbon offsets, potentially even beyond California.” A spokesperson for the author, Sen. Monique Limón (D-Santa Barbara), said she’s planning a new push next year. — JW

BLAME GAME: California Republicans are cheering the Biden administration’s move Tuesday to blame a ship for an oil spill in 2021.

The National Transportation Safety Board said the spill in San Pedro Bay might not have happened if ships responsible for initial damage to an Amplify Energy pipeline had warned the company of a potential strike, reports Heather Richards from POLITICO’s E&E News.

So the NTSB recommended that the U.S. Coast Guard require ships to anchor further away from oil pipelines than they currently have to.

In some ways, the recommendations are a coup for Amplify Energy, which has been pointing the finger at the shipping companies for the oil spill, which dumped 25,000 gallons of crude oil into California waters. The company was fined by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration and had to settle with residents and businesses of Huntington Beach after the spill.

“As the NTSB found in their investigation, had either international shipping company notified us of this anchor drag event, this event would not have occurred,” the company said in a statement.

But the oil company doesn’t have clean hands, either. The report also found that “controllers were not adequately trained to handle abnormal operating conditions, which delayed shutting down and isolating the pipeline,” according to NTSB Chair Jennifer Homendy. It also found that the company’s leak detection system initially identified the wrong location of the leak.

State Sen. Janet Nguyen (R-Huntington Beach) praised the determination and said she’s eager for new regulations on ships to reduce the likelihood of pipeline strikes. “It’s about time,” she told Blanca. “We’ve known what happened, but now we can at least have good regulations in place so that future oil spills like this will never occur again.” Environmentalists see it as a step in the right direction.

“We think it’s a good step, in terms of having more of a buffer,” said Pete Stauffer, the Surfrider Foundation’s ocean protection manager. “But it doesn’t solve the larger issue, which is that we have aging, oil and gas infrastructure in the ocean off California.”

Most of the oil drilling that occurs off the California coast happens in federal waters. New offshore leases are currently banned in state waters, and a 2022 bill by Dave Min (D-Irvine) to end offshore oil drilling under existing leases in the state failed to pass the legislature.

MYSTERY COMMERCIALS: Californians are witnessing a multimillion-dollar ad blitz blaming state climate policies for pushing up gasoline and electricity costs. But they don’t know who’s behind them, or what they’re in reference to specifically.

The ads come from Californians for Energy Independence, which has ties to fossil fuel interests, and Californians for Affordable and Reliable Energy, which is linked to a big business association. Neither group will provide specifics on their membership, reports Anne C. Mulkern from POLITICO’s E&E News. The two groups have spent a combined $4.3 million this year to put the ads on television and Facebook in both English and Spanish Officials affiliated with the groups insist that the commercials are NOT aimed at a 2024 oil industry ballot measure to overturn a state law passed last year to phase out neighborhood oil drilling.

“The commercials are discussing the importance of domestic energy production and the dangers of being overly dependent on foreign imports, as California currently is,” Rock Zierman, CEO of the California Independent Petroleum Association, said in an email.

But the timing of the ads just before the 2024 elections is raising eyebrows among activists and academics. “They are doing what we call issue advocacy, which is laying the groundwork for an argument that they are going to use once the referendum has a [ballot] number and can be targeted,” Jamie Court, president at Consumer Watchdog, wrote to Anne in an email. “This is soft money, does not have to be reported, so it can be hidden, and there does not have to be disclosure about the true source of the money on the ad.”

LESS LUXURY: Across the country, as in California, wealthier and whiter neighborhoods have more tree cover and are better insulated against the effects of global warming than poorer areas. It has to do with legacies of gentrification and resources to maintain vegetation.

But a new study out of UC Riverside shows that the protective effect of income against climate change, known as the “luxury effect,” is weakening. Landscape plants aren’t doing as much as they used to to shelter rich people from extreme heat.

The study, focused on L.A., looked at different neighborhoods, income levels, race, satellite data on vegetation and ground temperatures between 1985 and 2021, while the city’s surface temperature rose an average of 8.4 degrees Fahrenheit. It found that poor areas are still hotter than wealthy areas, but the gap has narrowed, and the luxury effect is 42 percent weaker than it was in the 90s.

That’s not because poorer areas are getting cooler, but rather richer regions are less able to insulate themselves. The author speculated it could be because it’s getting so hot that even though trees are transpiring more efficiently and becoming more effective at cooling, they’re not able to keep up with the pace of warming. Wealthy residents replacing leafy vegetation with drought tolerant species in their gardens could also play a role.

While it may look like equity is increasing, “I think it makes it harder to establish environmental equity in cities,” said Dion Kucera, a Riverside botany Ph.D. student and first author of the study. “The decrease in effectiveness of income will make it harder for cities with limited budgets to influence urban temperatures and urban greenness.” —BB
