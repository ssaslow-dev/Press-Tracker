Newsom, California lawmakers have a $68B budget hole to deal with, legislative analyst says

SACRAMENTO, Calif. —

Gov. Gavin Newsom and state lawmakers will need to figure out how to close a $68 billion budget hole when they begin crafting next year's state budget, California's Legislative Analyst Office announced Thursday.

Legislative Analyst Gabe Petek stopped short of calling it a crisis but said the governor should consider declaring a budget emergency to tap into the state's rainy-day fund. The outlook means California could also be facing deep budget cuts by next summer, when the state's updated spending plan is due.

The $68 billion figure would make it the largest deficit in the state's history by dollar amount,but ranks among deficits from the early 2000's based on the percentage of the state's total state budget. California's revenues and budget have grown significantly over the last couple of decades.

The LAO noted state leaders have several tools to address the problem, including the $37.8 billion the state has in reserves. The report suggested state leaders consider reducing billions for education and halting the use of funds it has earmarked but not yet spent on one-time programs including those related to the environment and transportation.

Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, who recently took the helm of the Assembly's budget committee, said he's hoping to avoid cuts to services.

"The goal here is to avoid cuts particularly to safety net services for our most vulnerable, that is the absolute last thing that anybody wants to do," Gabriel said. "We're unfortunately in a position this year where we're going to have to say no to a lot of things."

The non-partisan Legislative Analyst Office is the state agency that guides the Legislature in its financial decision-making. The office released its updated fiscal outlook about a week after releasing a report that showed the state's delayed tax revenues came in much lower than expected, which could result in deficits in the tens of billions of dollars spread out over the next three years.

The projected budget shortfall means state leaders will have another difficult year of negotiating the state's spending plan. Last year, the Legislature and governor haggled over how to close a $31.5 billion hole.

State officials have pointed to a variety of reasons for the revenue decline. California's top earners provide the majority of state revenues. Those earners have been impacted by an underperforming stock market, persistently high inflation and job losses in high-wage sectors.

Tax returns this year were also delayed seven months because of a series of storms that impacted most of the state. Because of that delay, the legislature and governor crafted a state spending plan this summer without having a full account of its financial picture. The LAO said last week those revenues came in $26 billion below expectations for this upcoming year.

The LAO said the revenue shortfall is unprecedented, noting typical budgets don't account for large changes in revenue from the year before. The office warned the economic weakness could persist into next year. "Whether the recent weakness will continue is difficult to say. However, the odds do not appear to be in the state's favor," the LAO said in the report.

“Despite all warnings that it was unsustainable, California’s tax-and-spend majority increased state spending by $116 billion over the last six years, nearly doubling the general fund budget in that short time," said Republican State Sen. Roger Niello, who is the vice chairman of the Senate's budget committee.

He said in part, "Republicans cautioned that this level of spending would lead to greater deficits, and it would be more prudent to show restraint. Unfortunately, the majority party ignored those warnings."

How exactly lawmakers and the governor close the gap is to be negotiated over the next several months. Newsom will formally begin those negotiations in January when he presents his first draft of the state spending plan.
