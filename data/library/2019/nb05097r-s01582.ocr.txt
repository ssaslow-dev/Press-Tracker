SPLIT DECISION FOR RENT CONTROL - CAPS ON INCREASES MOVES ON; MORE HOMES
BEING COVERED STALLS
April 26, 2019 | Mercury News, The (San Jose, CA)
Author: Katy Murphy and Marisa Kendall, Staff writers Section: Local News 864 Words
Page: 1B
OpenURL Link

There was something for each side of the heated rent control debate to celebrate Thursday: one closely watched
bill that would cap rent increases across the state moved forward, while a second bill that would have allowed more
homes to be covered by rent control stalled and likely died.
Assembly Bill 1482, which would prevent landlords from raising rents more than 5 percent a year after inflation,
advanced Thursday after members of the Assembly Housing and Community Development Committee voted 6-1 in
favor — even though they expressed several concerns with the measure.
The bill by Assemblyman David Chiu, D-San Francisco, now moves on to the Assembly Appropriations Committee.
"This is a really big step forward, and this has the potential to really move the needle in a very serious way and
help a lot of folks who need and deserve help," said Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, D- San Fernando Valley, who voted
for the bill. But, he added, "we have to be thoughtful because it could have unintended consequences."
Gabriel and his fellow committee members were scheduled to vote on another rent control measure, Assembly Bill
36 as well. But its author, Assemblyman Richard Bloom, D-Santa Monica, announced late Wednesday night that he
would pull the bill, making it highly unlikely that it will move this year.
AB 36 would have allowed cities to impose rent control on buildings 20 years old or older and on single-family
homes, as long as the landlords owned 10 or fewer residences in the same jurisdiction.
Currently, a state law known as Costa Hawkins restricts rent control to apartments built before 1995, or in some
cities, even earlier. Single-family homes my not be rent-controlled.
Last November voters shot down a ballot measure, dubbed Proposition 10, that would have repealed Costa
Hawkins. The same group that backed that ballot measure, the AIDS Healthcare Foundation, and its president,
Michael Weinstein, on Monday announced they're trying again — this time to get a similar but scaled-back measure
on the 2020 ballot.
Given legislative deadlines, AB 36 is unlikely to be revived this year. The bill was shy of the votes it needed to pass
out of the Thursday morning hearing, even though dozens of tenant activists staged a sit-in at Gov. Gavin
Newsom's office Wednesday afternoon, demanding he do more to champion the proposal.
Both AB 36 and AB 1482 are part of a renter-focused legislative package requested by Newsom.
Thursday's hearing was filled well beyond capacity. Supporters and opponents of AB 1482, the controversial rent
cap measure, packed the small hearing room and those who couldn't fit formed a line that wrapped around the
hallway outside, waiting their turn to voice their opinions. Supporters holding signs that read "Stand with the 17 MIL
Californians who rent" faced off against opponents with signs that read "Don't make a bad problem worse."
AB 1482 would cap rent increases at 5 percent per year plus any change in the cost of living, not to exceed a total
increase of 10 percent. Landlords would be prohibited from evicting a tenant in order to get out from under the cap,
and the Department of Housing and Community Development would be required to study the cap and submit a
report to the legislature on its effectiveness by 2033.

Chiu on Thursday said his bill isn't rent control — it's a protection against rent-gouging.
"What this cap does is prevent the most egregious increases in rent," he said. "For landlords, this bill would still
enable them not just a fair rate of return, but a healthy return on their investment."
Opponents said a rent cap isn't the solution to California's housing shortage. Debra Carlton, senior vice president of
state public affairs for the California Apartment Association, worried imposing a 5 percent rent cap would start
legislators down a slippery slope, and they could be tempted to cap rents even lower in later years.
Adam Regele, policy advocate for the California Chamber of Commerce, complained the bill doesn't exempt new
construction, nor does it allow for landlords to pass on costs of water, gas, electric and renovations to their tenants.
"The bill disincentivizes desperately needed new housing," he said.
Several committee members echoed the concerns opponents presented Thursday, and Chiu said he would take
those issues to heart.
"I'm not prepared to commit to any of these issues today," he said. "But certainly hearing them put on the table, I
very much appreciate that and I think we will certainly be having discussions in the coming days and weeks as we
hope to move this forward."
Elsa Stevens, who rents a one-bedroom apartment in a building for seniors in Richmond, spoke in favor of Chiu's bill
Thursday.
She told the crowd that she was hit with a 7 percent rent hike in 2017 and a 12 percent hike last year, which was
ultimately lowered to 3 percent. Now she faces another 5 percent hike.
"My husband and I were already spending 40 percent of our income on rent," Stevens said, later adding: "you gotta
move these bills."
