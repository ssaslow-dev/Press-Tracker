California should expand 'civil Gideon' in its courts; The state should provide lawyers to those who can't afford them in eviction, custody and other cases.

Tens of thousands of Californians who otherwise would have been at grave risk of losing their homes, their children, their ability to pursue a living or other basic human rights and needs had at least a fighting chance to defend themselves in court because of landmark 2009 legislation that gave them access to lawyers.

In a rare feat of foresight, the Sargent Shriver Civil Counsel Act that established the pilot program required and provided funding for evaluations. So we know, for example, that providing counsel for low-income tenants facing eviction in Los Angeles County increased the likelihood of tenants responding to eviction actions, of cases being settled and of tenants retaining their housing or finding new places to live with minimal costly disruption.

Similarly good outcomes in the few other counties involved in the pilot led the Legislature to make the program permanent in 2016. Now lawmakers are considering a bill that would increase court filing fees to provide funding to expand the number of people served by the program. It's a good move that deserves to proceed.

AB 330 by Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, a Democrat representing the west San Fernando Valley, would raise filing fees for some court services, such as issuing a writ to enforce a judgment or taking an affidavit.

Raising court fees is not something to be taken lightly; those fees can add up, and there is a tendency to look to them to pay for all kinds of services without raising taxes. Yet studies of the earlier legislation show that providing counsel benefits more than the litigants. It improves court efficiency and saves court time and money. When the parties have lawyers, fewer disputes return to court to be reworked following settlements. Court fees to pay for lawyers -- in limited numbers of cases, for parties who cannot afford to pay -- also, in the end, improve court for everyone.

Criminal defendants have a right to "assistance of counsel" under the 6th Amendment, and since the 1963 Supreme Court ruling in Gideon vs. Wainwright, that's meant a right to a public defender or court-appointed attorney if the defendant can't afford to hire a lawyer.

There is no similar constitutional right to an appointed attorney in civil matters.

Yet many lawsuits and other civil actions can put litigants in deep jeopardy of losing fundamental rights. Providing them attorneys has come to be referred to as "civil Gideon" -- in reference to the right recognized in criminal cases. More jurisdictions are employing civil Gideon programs around the nation, supplying lawyers to parties who don't have one when their opponents do.

New York City was first to adopt a so-called right to counsel for indigent tenants in eviction cases, and the program resulted in a sharp drop in wrongful evictions, according to a recent study.

Of course, the city couldn't actually make counsel in noncriminal cases a constitutional right. But just as the term "civil Gideon" underscores the importance of appointed counsel in civil cases, New York now provides it to some impoverished litigants in select cases.

Similar programs are being launched in landlord-tenant cases in Philadelphia, Washington, Detroit and Newark, N.J. Los Angeles city and county are studying such programs with an eye toward reducing homelessness.

Last year, San Francisco voters adopted a "right to counsel" but made it available to any tenant, even wealthy ones who could afford to hire their own lawyers.

Gabriel's bill is far more measured, and is based on the earlier legislation crafted by Mike Feuer, who is now L.A. city

attorney. Funding comes not from taxpayers but from court users. Use would not be limited to eviction cases, but could be applied, as it is under the existing state program, to child custody, domestic violence or other programs that are vetted and evaluated by the state Judicial Council.

One of the council's tasks will be to ensure that the funding does not duplicate the new programs being rolled out by San Francisco and other cities and counties. That shouldn't be difficult.

The state's existing program has produced a good record of success. The Legislature now has an opportunity to build on it. Lawmakers should move forward.
