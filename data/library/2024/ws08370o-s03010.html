California budget talks to ramp up this week

SACRAMENTO, Calif. —

California lawmakers have about two and a half weeks to pass a state spending plan that closes a $27.6 billion budget gap.

"We are trying to think this through very, very carefully to try to do the best job we can under difficult circumstances," said Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, the Democratic Party leader of the Assembly's Budget Committee.

On Thursday, the committee is set to adopt a budget plan crafted by Democrats in the Assembly, according to an agenda posted online. Details of that plan are set to be released soon.

Lawmakers have spent the last few weeks reviewing the governor's proposed spending plan, which includes cuts to numerous programs across state agencies.

Gov. Gavin Newsom's proposal pushed the state's powerful teacher's union, the California Teachers Association, to publicly pressure him with an ad over his proposed cuts to public schools. The group warned schools could lose billions over the next couple of years, which would mean lost teachers and resources.

Newsom's administration and the union came to a truce during the holiday weekend, Politico first reported. Lawmakers on both sides of the aisle said they were still reviewing the details as of Tuesday.

H.D. Palmer, a spokesman for Newsom's Department of Finance, confirmed the agreement. He said the deal avoids deep cuts by using a combination of delayed spending, the use of reserves, and a suspension of the guaranteed minimum spend amount under Proposition 98, which is the state's required funding formula for school spending. Palmer said Newsom's administration and the union spent the last couple of weeks sorting out the issue.

"The result of that is an agreement we can both support, and that advances two shared goals: avoid multi-billion-dollar cuts to schools in the near term and provide greater predictability for school budgets in the long term," Palmer said.

The agreement comes at a time when various other groups remain upset with proposed cuts to key services, including those related to health care.

"I understand perfectly, but tell me where else to cut," said Republican state Sen. Roger Niello. "Because once you get to a balance, if you're going to restore money to one party, you're going to have to take it away from another. And that's a tough thing to do."

"We're trying to do our homework, really dig in, figure out where we can make efficiencies, savings and do things that are going to allow us to balance the budget but not do it in a way that hurts Californians," Gabriel said.

The State Senate is set to continue holding budget hearings this week. Lawmakers face a June 15 deadline to pass a plan, or else they don't get paid. What's typically passed by the deadline serves as a placeholder for the actual agreement between the legislature and governor. The new fiscal year starts July 1.
