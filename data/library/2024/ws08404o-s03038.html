CHICO, Calif. – California’s first-of-its-kind Food Safety Act bans four chemicals found in candy, cereals, salad dressings and other processed foods. Starting in 2027, propylparaben, brominated vegetable oil, potassium bromate, and red dye Number 3 cannot be used in food products sold throughout California.

“(Red dye number 3) has been linked not only to cancer but also to behavioral issues," said Dr. Maria Giovanni with the Center for Regenerative Agriculture at Chico State. “Some of the concerns, for all of them, really, were around cancer or hormonal disruption."

Three out of four chemicals are already banned in 27 nations in the European Union.

“They look at it differently than we do; we look at it on a risk basis, so basically that means, what's the risk versus the benefit? What's the benefit of having behavioral issues from eating what the FDA considers from studies a reasonable amount of the food?" said Dr. Giovanni.

Many people have no idea what foods still contain them and will contain them until 2027, when the law takes effect.

“I can’t understand why anyone would want any of these heavily processed foods. They add so much to food that’s not food,” said Chico shopper Judy Kane.

“I certainly don’t know the names. They try to disguise them so we can’t even pronounce them, but I definitely do run through the ingredients list when I make any purchase,” said another local shopper, Marcy Reeves.

Red dye number 3 colors candies like Peeps. Propylparaben is a preservative used in baked goods and trail mixes. Brominated vegetable oil is added to citrus-flavored drinks. Potassium bromate is used to strengthen the dough.

The assemblyman who sponsored the bill says the FDA isn’t paying close enough attention.

"When I realized that 99% of these chemicals are not being reviewed by the FDA, that was my ah-ha moment,” said California Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel.

The non-profit Institute of Food Technologists told Action News Now that the state action takes things a step further than the Food and Drug Administration’s rules and said it may be an unnecessary step.

“We do have an incredibly strong regulatory framework coming from the FDA that’s outlined our food safety for years, and we have one of the safest food supplies in the world,” said Anna Rosales, Senior Director of Government Affairs and Nutrition for the IFT. “The FDA does have regulatory oversight over all food products and ingredients, and there are absolutely standards for what additives and ingredients are being included.”

Once the new rule takes effect, many food companies will have to change their recipes to sell in California, and the IFT says that may mean higher prices.

“When you talk about changing a formula that increases food costs or you’re talking about having different formulas in different states, that could increase food costs,” Rosales said.

But some say their family’s safety and their peace of mind are worth it.

“It’s definitely worth it when you think about all the things we ingest and the chemicals we’re exposed to every day. It’s definitely worth it to limit that exposure as much as possible,” said Reeves.

“The heavily processed foods should be very expensive because they use more manufacturing processes. These foods people need to eat that are not processed, that are organic—all those things can make a difference in health, and I think we’re going to be finding that out for years and years to come,” said Kane.

Skittles contain titanium dioxide, which brightens some candies and dairy products. The bill would have banned titanium dioxide, but it was removed by an amendment.
