California lawmakers brace for budget uncertainty now that President Trump is in office

Updated: 6:26 PM PST Feb 10, 2025Editorial Standards ⓘAshley ZavalaPolitical DirectorSACRAMENTO, Calif. —

California lawmakers on Monday attempted to begin the process of analyzing Gov. Gavin Newsom's proposed state spending plan but acknowledged now with President Donald Trump in office, the picture could look completely different in a matter of months.

The governor introduced what's considered the first draft of his proposed $322 billion budget days before the wildfires in Los Angeles broke out and weeks before President Trump took office. Newsom acknowledged the uncertainty in his presentation in January, which was more brief than usual.

"To put it mildly, it has been an uncertain start to the year," said Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel, D-Encino, the chairman of the Assembly's budget panel that held the first hearing on the plan. "The governor's budget is barely a month old and yet it feels like it was introduced in a different era."

The state is facing a handful of uncertainties beyond the Trump administration, state officials said. That includes the stock market, the impact of the Los Angeles wildfires and its delay on tax returns, and the economy.

California Legislative Analyst Gabe Petek said his office is tracking a relatively flat economy, noting state job growth is mainly centered around government and healthcare-related sectors and hardly anything else.

Federal dollars help the state with about a third of its expenses, and it's unclear how the Trump administration could change that. The President and his Department of Government Efficiency have pushed to freeze or slash federal funds. Some Democratic state lawmakers said they're bracing for deep cuts.

"This could be a very, very dire situation that we are faced with if this current President and his appointed people have their way," said Assemblymember Pilar Schiavo, D-Santa Clarita.

Erika Li, Chief Deputy of Budgets for Newsom's Department of Finance, told lawmakers that California's general fund has no way to backfill the federal dollars if they're lost.

When asked by Assemblyman Corey Jackson, D-Moreno Valley, if the administration could commit to preserving California's safety net services, Li responded, "everything is on the table."

Gov. Newsom's administration is not projecting a significant shortfall or surplus this year, but a roughly balanced budget. To cover costs, the governor is planning on pulling $7 billion out of the state's reserves, which appeared to frustrate lawmakers from both sides of the aisle at Monday's hearing.

"My concern would be dipping into the rainy-day fund when it's not even misty out, particularly in light of the potential risks," said Assemblymember Cottie Petrie-Norris, D-Irvine. "We need to actually have those funds for when the rain actually starts coming."

State officials noted California is spending more money than it actually has at this point, with the situation expected to worsen in the coming years. Petek told lawmakers he recommended they do more oversight and analysis into state programs to prepare.

"As we're saying the budget is roughly balanced at this time for this coming year, the bigger challenges will be in those future years," Petek said.

Because of the major immigration policy differences between the state and federal government, one major line item that will likely be under the microscope this year is California's program that provides healthcare to undocumented people.

During a line of questioning with Assemblyman Carl DeMaio, R-San Diego, Newsom's Department of Finance said the state is estimated to spend $9.5 billion on the expense, with $8.4 billion coming from the state's general fund. The number has grown from the $6 billion that was initially projected.

"What are the priorities of California taxpayers?" DeMaio asked. "I don't think covering 9.5 billion in illegal immigrant healthcare in the general fund would be at the top of their list."

Monday's hearing kicked off what will be dozens of hearings over the next several months to analyze the state's spending plan. The state spending plan is due by late June.
