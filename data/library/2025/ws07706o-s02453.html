California bill pushes for ban on harmful ultraprocessed foods in schools by 2032

Jackson Ellison

A Los Angeles lawmaker pushes a bill to remove harmful ultraprocessed foods from California schools by 2032.

SACRAMENTO, Calif. — A Los Angeles lawmaker is pushing for specific ultraprocessed foods to be removed from schools in an effort to make California students healthier.

Assembly Bill 1264, authored by Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel (D), would allow foods that contain fruits, vegetables, dairy, protein or whole grains. It defines “particularly harmful ultraprocessed foods” as those determined by experts based on scientific research, as they have been linked to causing adverse health effects.

Schools would need to begin phasing out the offering of particularly harmful ultraprocessed foods by 2028, with a complete ban on vendors providing the foods to schools by 2032. Any producer of products that are deemed "particularly harmful" would be able to petition the state to remain in schools.

“Our schools should not be serving students products that are filled with dangerous additives that can harm their physical and mental health or interfere with their ability to learn,” Gabriel said.

The bill has received support from medical professionals, such as those from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), who say ultraprocessed foods sold in schools have contributed to chronic health conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes in children.

”These aren’t just distant threats, they are showing up in exam rooms, they’re showing up in my exam room," said Dr. Ravinder Khaira with the AAP.

AB 1264 is opposed by a coalition of food and agricultural groups who feel the language is overly broad in scope and the timing for implementation is rushed, which could open the door for litigation.

“They have to make these scientific determinations by July 1, 2026, on what a particularly harmful [ultraprocessed food] is,” said Dennis Albiani with the coalition at a senate committee hearing for the bill.  

He argues that's not enough time to establish and implement the regulations for the new category.

Gabriel argues the focus is narrow, requiring experts to define precisely what constitutes a particularly harmful ultraprocessed food, with the ultimate goal of removing them from schools.

“California’s going to serve north of a billion school meals this year, so one of the most direct and impactful ways we can impact student health is to change what we serve in our public schools,” Gabriel said. 

AB 1264 has been placed on the Senate suspense file for a later vote.
