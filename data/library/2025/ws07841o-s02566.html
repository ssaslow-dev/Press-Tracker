‘California 49’: The Golden State’s most influential public officials in 2025

Brett Stover and Emily Hamann

SACRAMENTO — The legislative session is in full swing and speculation abounds about next year’s gubernatorial race to replace term-limited Gov. Gavin Newsom, all against the backdrop of a bleak budget outlook and a new administration in the White House poised to antagonize California’s Democratic supermajority.

With that in mind, welcome to the inaugural “California 49.” Brought to you by CapitolTrack/*State Affairs*, the 2025 list is modeled after the “Power 50” — a yearly feature at our sister outlets; but notably, the Golden State version highlights powerful elected officials and those who hold sway in their respective legislative or judicial office.

The 49 is an attempt at ranking the state’s most influential leaders in politics and policy. *State Affairs* will publish a new version of these rankings each year, and we want your feedback to refine each subsequent edition. So if you don’t agree with someone who’s on the list, not on the list or too high or low, let us know! Without further ado, the California 49:

**1. Gavin Newsom**

Newsom’s dominance in California politics is without question. Not surprising. Backed by major revenue growth, the Democratic governor has overseen a massive expansion in state spending, creating or expanding programs for housing, early childhood education, health care and climate change. But now, a murkier economic and fiscal outlook for the state has put some of those lofty goals in jeopardy. With just 20 months left for Newsom atop the state’s political pyramid, it’s time for him to cement his legacy — and set up his future, including a long-rumored leap into the 2028 presidential race.

**2. Robert Rivas**

The rise to the top in Sacramento has been swift for the Democratic Assembly speaker. It took Rivas just a few years to win enough support to supplant then-Speaker Anthony Rendon, though the change was one marked by a shift more in management style than in ideology. Rivas, who grew up in rural San Benito County, commands his caucus with a tighter grip than his predecessor. He has also become even more media-shy in recent months, drawing the ire of the Capitol Press Corps. The 2026 election — with a new governor and a likely favorable environment for Democrats nationwide — will provide an opportunity for Rivas to increase his influence.

**3. Mike McGuire**

The Democratic Senate president pro tempore is now in his second full year leading the upper house of the Legislature after taking over from the formidable Toni Atkins in a somewhat more peaceful transition than the power struggle that elevated his counterpart in the Assembly around the same time. As presiding officer of the chamber, McGuire, who was born and raised in Sonoma County, has brought his trademark boundless energy and positive attitude to the Senate’s top spot, although he has a small window to get things done before he is term-limited out of the Legislature at the end of next year. The Democrat is already eyeing a run for insurance commissioner as his potential next move.

**4. Nancy Pelosi**

On the national level, few politicians are as closely associated with California as the former Democratic House speaker, who has represented San Francisco in the U.S. House of Representatives for nearly four decades. When she ascended to the role of speaker of the House in 2007, she made history as not only the first woman but also the first Californian to hold the position. And although her formal leadership in the House is over, she remains an influential, albeit polarizing, figure and powerful fundraiser in Democratic politics — nationwide. Major questions remain about her future, however, as the 85-year-old powerhouse dodges questions about retirement and faces a challenge from the left for her House seat in 2026.

**5. Rob Bonta**

After nearly a decade in the Assembly, Bonta was appointed attorney general by Gov. Gavin Newsom to replace Xavier Becerra and won reelection shortly thereafter. But President Donald Trump’s victory in November has elevated Bonta’s profile, particularly on the national stage. He’s filed at least 13 lawsuits against the president’s administration in just three months — along with countless other statements — building up his “resistance” bona fides. It’s early, but Bonta’s ability to lead other blue states as they fight federal actions could help his political future — much like a similar approach helped the next entry on this list.

**6. Adam Schiff**

Becoming a prominent anti-Trump voice can pay dividends in a state like California. Just ask Democratic U.S. Sen. Adam Schiff, who became an overnight sensation in Trump’s first term, first by pushing for investigations into collusion between the president and Russia and later by serving as lead manager in Trump’s first impeachment trial. That popularity translated into a pocketbook boost last year, when Schiff’s financial advantage in the state’s most expensive Senate race allowed him to prop up Republican candidate Steve Garvey and box out fellow #resistance favorite Katie Porter, earning an easy victory and completing his journey from California senator in the late 1990s to U.S. senator. Schiff also served for several years as co-chair of the bipartisan Congressional Caucus for the Freedom of the Press, cementing his position internationally as a stalwart for press freedom and democracy.

**7. Alex Padilla**

Long before Trump took office, Democratic U.S. Sen. Alex Padilla stood out as one of the fiercest advocates in the Senate for immigration rights — even breaking with fellow Democrats over policies he saw as harmful to immigrants. He seems almost uniquely suited to the job. It was California’s 1994 anti-immigrant initiative, Proposition 187, that got Padilla, whose parents are immigrants from Mexico, involved in politics. Now that he’s in Congress and chairs the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration, Citizenship and Border Safety, Padilla could emerge as one of the loudest voices opposing Trump’s immigration policies.

**8. Karen Bass**

The Los Angeles mayor faces significant challenges in her first term leading the nation’s second-largest city, including a nearly $1 billion budget deficit and devastating L.A. wildfires that have sparked criticism of her handling. The rough few months in local leadership comes after a long career in state and national politics, during which time she made history as the first Black woman to lead the Assembly, was so well-regarded by congressional Democrats that she was once pushed to challenge then-U.S. House Speaker Nancy Pelosi for the speakership (Bass declined) and was under consideration to be Joe Biden’s running mate in 2020.

**9. Buffy Wicks**

As chair of the Assembly Appropriations Committee, Wicks, a Democrat, wields considerable power in carrying out the committee’s business of deciding which bills make it to the Assembly floor and which ones do not. Hundreds of bills die every year through the committee’s suspense file process without coming up for a public discussion or vote. It’s Wicks’ second year leading the powerful committee after being appointed by Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas in late 2023.

**10. Anna Caballero**

Caballero’s position as chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee makes her one of the most powerful members of her chamber. Caballero, a Democrat, wields significant influence over which bills move ahead during the committee’s suspense file process. Senate President Mike McGuire appointed her to the coveted position after he took over leadership of the House at the beginning of last year.

**11. Scott Wiener**

Wiener’s appointment as chair of the Budget and Fiscal Review Committee gives him significant influence over the state budget — a job that becomes much harder during years, such as this one, that require tough choices on state spending. But the San Francisco senator is no stranger to controversy; he routinely introduces some of the most far-reaching and attention-grabbing bills each session, tackling topics like housing, tech and AI, climate change and LGBTQ+ rights, and he has been one of the Legislature’s loudest and most ardent critics of the Trump administration. Although the Democrat can stay in his seat through 2028, he already has his sights set on higher office and has been quietly organizing for years to take Nancy Pelosi’s House seat once she decides to retire.

**12. Jesse Gabriel**

The Encino assemblyman is serving his second year as chair of the Assembly Budget Committee — putting the Democrat at the head of one of the most powerful committees in the Legislature. Outside of his work on the panel, he’s passed significant legislation addressing gun violence prevention, banning food additives, regulating social media companies and protecting reproductive health care. He is also co-chair of the California Legislative Jewish Caucus.

**13. Eleni Kounalakis**

The lieutenant governor has a strong résumé with a background in business and deep connections within the Democratic Party — former President Barack Obama appointed her U.S. Ambassador to Hungary in 2010. Also, former Secretary of State Hillary Clinton and former Sen. Barbara Boxer backed Kounalakis’ bid for governor. Everything was shaping up nearly perfectly for Kounalakis until Harris lost the presidency last fall. Now we’ll have to wait and see whether Kounalakis will pivot to an alternative next step, which could be a run for state treasurer, according to some media reports.

**14. Fiona Ma**

After time in the Assembly — including a term as speaker pro tempore — and on the Board of Equalization, Ma broke through statewide in 2018, taking over as state treasurer. It’s an under-the-radar role, but one that could help Ma climb another rung up the ladder of California leadership. She has also exited a lengthy legal battle resulting from a former staffer accusing Ma of sexual harassment. The staffer dropped her lawsuit last year and the state settled for $350,000, but Ma has always denied any wrongdoing. Now Ma’s sights are set on another statewide office, though she’s eschewing the frenzied gubernatorial race for a run for lieutenant governor in 2026.

**15. Tony Thurmond**

The state superintendent of public instruction hopes his two terms as the state’s top educational leader will help kickstart a long-shot bid for governor. After two terms in the Assembly, he was backed in 2018 by the California Teachers Association and the California Federation of Teachers; the race became a sort of referendum on charter schools as his opponent, Marshall Tuck, was a vocal charter advocate. Thurmond was criticized for hiring a “superintendent of equity” who lived and worked in Philadelphia. Will the long odds pay off for Thurmond, or will he be looking to regroup after next year’s primary?

**16. Donald Trump**

California has long been a preferred punching bag for the president: The state is a “paradise lost,” it’s “out of control,” governed by “Gavin Newscum.” Trump has already threatened to withhold federal aid in the aftermath of the January wildfires and recently targeted California’s climate policies with an executive order. But those clashes carry benefits for Democrats, too: Conflict with the president, or at least the appearance of it, can be a major boost. Although Trump has gained ground in California — his vote share increased to above 38% last November from below 32% in 2016 — many Republicans still avoid associating with the president’s policies.

**17. Cecilia Aguiar-Curry**

Just a few months into Speaker Robert Rivas’ reign, he remade his leadership team, replacing a close ally, Assemblyman Isaac Bryan, as head of the majority party in the Assembly. Aguiar-Curry is also chair of the Legislative Women’s Caucus, which reached a high-water mark last year when a record 59 women — one lawmaker shy of half the Legislature — were elected in November.

**18. Lena Gonzalez**

The Senate majority leader is in the midst of her second session as the Senate’s second-in-command. In addition to her time in Democratic leadership and work on environmental policy, Gonzalez also serves as chair of the 35-member Latino Legislative Caucus, which is backing her bill to bar ICE agents from entering schools without a warrant. Now, can she use her growing influence to finally get enough lawmakers on board with her biggest goal: forcing CalPERS to divest from fossil fuel companies?

**19. Maxine Waters**

Whether it was during the Clinton administration, when she was rebuked for repeatedly interrupting a fellow U.S. representative criticizing her on the House floor, or it was a few weeks ago, when she suggested Trump should be “tried for treason,” Waters has never shied away from speaking her mind during her long tenure in Congress. She has wielded considerable power during her three decades representing South Los Angeles, including her current appointment as ranking member of the House Financial Services Committee.

**20. Patricia Guerrero**

Within a year, Guerrero became the first Latina to serve on California’s highest court before moving into the chief justice spot. And that’s no small feat in a state where courts don’t reflect the diverse population. Since 2023, though, it hasn’t been smooth sailing for the third branch. Guerrero had to oversee nearly $100 million in cuts last year, causing court closures and furloughs. That money is back in Gov. Gavin Newsom’s budget this year and Guerrero wants to get to work addressing a slate of challenges, including an increasingly congested justice system.

**21. Ricardo Lara**

The state’s insurance commissioner has been in the hot seat recently, as lawmakers and consumers alike look to him to stabilize the FAIR Plan in the wake of the Los Angeles wildfires — the costliest natural disaster in state history. Even before the fires, he had the daunting task of regulating an insurance market in crisis. And although the job was once a stepping stone for ambitious politicians, Lara has faced criticism since he’s been at it, including claims he is too friendly with insurance companies and questions about his meeting and travel activities. His next move after he is termed out in 2026 remains unclear.

**22. Shirley Weber**

When Gavin Newsom promoted Alex Padilla to U.S. senator in 2021, the governor also picked Weber from the Legislature to become California’s first Black secretary of state. (Her daughter, Sen. Akilah Weber Pierson, was elected to replace her in the Assembly in 2021.) Weber’s time as the state’s top election official is a departure from her past work in education, both for decades as a professor and later as an advocate for school reforms during her decadelong tenure as a Democratic in the Assembly.

**23. Malia Cohen**

After serving for most of the 2010s on the San Francisco Board of Supervisors, Cohen broke onto the state scene first in 2018 as a member of the State Board of Equalization and then in 2022 by winning her first statewide race for controller. But her ties to the top of this list go back even further — Cohen worked as a field organizer and later as an aide for Gavin Newsom during his campaign for and first term as mayor of San Francisco. Two years ago, she was floated as a candidate to replace the late Sen. Dianne Feinstein. The future is bright for Cohen, but her next move is unclear: another term as controller or perhaps a run for another statewide office like treasurer?

**24. Mike Thompson**

Thompson is one of California’s longest-serving lawmakers in the U.S. House of Representatives, having first taken his seat in the chamber in 1999. He sits on the powerful Ways and Means Committee, serves as the ranking member on the Subcommittee on Tax and is the senior member of the Health Subcommittee. Over the years, he has secured major funding relevant to his district in Wine Country and the state as a whole, including wildfire mitigation, agriculture disaster relief and roads, public transit, broadband and other infrastructure projects. He’s also a co-founder of the Congressional Wine Caucus and a member of the Blue Dog Democrats.

**25. Pete Aguilar**

As the old guard of California’s congressional Democratic leadership enters its career twilight, Aguilar is angling to step up and mentor the next generation. As chair of the House Democratic Caucus, the San Bernardino lawmaker is the third-highest-ranking Democrat in the U.S. House of Representatives and the highest-ranking from California. During the last election, he raised more than $30 million for caucus members and launched an ambitious campaign to flip five California House seats for Democrats. He was partially successful — three of the five seats went to Democrats in November — and he has pledged to continue working to flip the House in 2026.

**26. Doris Matsui**

Although she may not be the most outspoken member of California’s congressional delegation, Matsui has worked behind the scenes over the last 20 years to become one of the state’s most effective lawmakers. Her district includes Sacramento, which, in addition to being home to the state capitol, is one of the most vulnerable cities in the country for flood risk, and Matsui has secured billions of federal dollars for levee improvements and other flooding infrastructure. She also sits on the Energy and Commerce Committee, including serving as ranking member of the Subcommittee on Communications and Technology, and authored the CHIPS for America Act, which invested $52 billion in domestic semiconductor research and manufacturing.

**27. Josh Lowenthal**

As Assembly speaker pro tempore, Lowenthal runs floor sessions when Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas isn’t available. It’s a new leadership role for the Long Beach Democrat, who started his second term at the beginning of this year. Lowenthal comes from a family dynasty of politicians — his mother is former Assemblywoman Bonnie Lowenthal and his father is former Rep. Alan Lowenthal; however, he resisted following in his parents’ footsteps until he was approaching his 50s and ran a number of businesses, including local restaurants, and worked as a teacher before going into politics.

**28. Mary H. Murguia**

Murguia, age 64, was appointed to the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals by Obama in 2010. The chief circuit judge became the first woman of color to lead a U.S. circuit court when she became chief judge of the appellate court, the largest in the country, in 2021.

**29. Barbara Lee**

The freshly elected Democratic mayor of Oakland hasn’t officially taken office yet — that will come in a few weeks — but she already looms large in Oakland politics, having represented the East Bay city in the U.S. House of Representatives for more than two decades, where she served as a trailblazer and staunch progressive icon. But the hard-won election victory comes just a year after she was boxed out of the U.S. Senate race, and now she has just a few short months to enact her vision for the city and address its serious budget deficit before she’ll have to start defending her seat in the 2026 election.

**30. Ami Bera**

Bera, a medical doctor, gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic and still serves on the Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Pandemic. He’s also on the U.S. House Committee on Foreign Affairs, where he sits as ranking member of the Subcommittee on the Indo-Pacific and on the Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence.

**31. Ro Khanna**

Khanna isn’t the easiest politician to briefly sum up, but one word that comes to mind — not in a pejorative sense — is idiosyncratic. The U.S. representative is a progressive capitalist; pro-labor but backed by big tech and venture capital; an Obama alum and Bernie Sanders supporter praised by Trump loyalist Steve Bannon. Now, Khanna is taking the show on the road, hosting town halls in central California and even in Ohio, where he took aim at U.S. vice president and fellow self-described populist JD Vance at an event last week. Where these paths will take Khanna is an open question, but there’s no doubt he’s taking the road less traveled.

**32. Tom Umberg**

Now in his third stint in the Legislature, Umberg is nearing the tail end of a career that’s spanned four decades in and out of the Capitol. It also took the Democratic mainstay to Washington during the Clinton administration. As chair of the Senate Judiciary Committee, the former prosecutor and retired colonel takes a no-nonsense approach, and he’s not afraid to stand up to members of his own party when necessary — and he’s always been one of the most productive lawmakers in terms of bills passed.

**33. John Laird**

During his first term in the Senate, Laird, a Democrat, had a whopping 59 of his bills signed into law, focusing on environmental issues, climate change, health care and other issues. And he remains a prolific lawmaker today, with 34 of his bills passing into law in the last session — a 77% success rate. He also chairs the influential Budget Subcommittee on Education, and co-chairs the joint legislative audit and rules committees. His current position in the Senate is the latest in a long career in public service that included serving as secretary of the California Natural Resources Agency under Gov. Jerry Brown.

**34. Mia Bonta**

Bonta replaced her husband, Rob, in the California Assembly after his ascension to the attorney general’s office in 2021 and has quickly become a force within the Assembly. The progressive Democrat rose further when she became chair of the Health Committee in late 2023, a position she still holds. Bonta’s role is one that will only gain prominence as Medi-Cal and other health care programs fall under the shadow of budget cuts.

**35. Gail Pellerin**

A longtime elections official in Santa Cruz County, Pellerin got elected to the Assembly in 2022 and immediately got to work, steering seven bills through the Legislature in her first year alone. Her work rate has continued, propelling the Democratic lawmaker into a position as one of Sacramento’s leaders on election issues — she became chair of the Assembly Elections Committee less than a year into her first term.

**36. Marc Berman**

When it comes to authoring bills, Berman is one of the Legislature’s most prolific and successful lawmakers. Last session, 40 of the bills authored by the Democrat made it into law, on topics including education, elections and artificial intelligence. He also has a high success rate when it comes to getting his bills across the finish line — just 17 of the bills he introduced last session failed to be enacted. During his first eight years in office, he has sent a total of 99 bills to the governor’s desk — where all but six of them were signed. Berman, who’s from Palo Alto, also chairs the Assembly Business and Professions Committee.

**37. Kevin McCarty**

Although he no longer works at the Capitol, McCarty has not gone far — just a few blocks away to the mayor’s office at the Sacramento City Hall. After 10 years in the Assembly, McCarty gave up his seat last year to run for mayor of the capital city, his hometown, in a tight, competitive race that he won by just 1% of the vote.

**38. James Gallagher**

As Assembly minority leader, Gallagher holds the designation of being the most powerful Republican in the Statehouse — however much power that entails in the deeply blue Legislature. The Sutter County lawmaker has led the 19-member caucus since 2022 and has consistently been one of the most outspoken critics of Democrats’ control over the state, especially of Gov. Gavin Newsom, and the state’s environmental policies.

**39. Brian Jones**

Leading the smallest of the four major caucuses — just 10 members make up the Senate GOP this session — isn’t the most glamorous job at the Capitol. But Senate Minority Leader Jones doesn’t shy away from the tough task of leading the superminority party, picking when to work across the aisle and when to pick more vocal fights, like his push this year to limit California’s sanctuary state law.

**40. Daniel Lurie**

The San Francisco mayor just hit 100 days in office after successfully ousting former Mayor London Breed and taking charge of California’s most densely populated city. The early returns have been positive, at least on the morale side of things, but murkier waters lie ahead as Lurie faces a major budget deficit and the expectation of following through on lofty campaign promises — he’s already backed off a vow to build 1,500 shelter beds in his first six months. Can the “vibe shift” continue, or will some of SF’s persistent problems prove too troublesome for Lurie?

**41. Nathan Hochman**

The Los Angeles County district attorney now sits at the head of the busiest DA  office in the country, after soundly winning the race to become Los Angeles County’s top prosecutor in November. Hochman unseated the incumbent — progressive prosecutor George Gascón — by a more-than 20-point margin, which has largely been attributed to backlash from voters against criminal justice reforms and a rise in crime. Although he previously ran for state attorney general as a Republican, Hochman ran in the DA race with an independent, “hard middle” approach, and pledged to reject mass incarceration while also undoing the “extreme pro-criminal, decarceration policies” of his predecessor.

**42. Bill Essayli**

Essayli, interim U.S. attorney for the Central District of California, wouldn’t have been on this list if it had been written a month ago. But Trump has elevated the fiery former assemblyman to lead the largest judicial district in the nation — and like Attorney General Rob Bonta’s status as a thorn in the president’s side can boost his profile, so can Essayli’s opportunities to investigate Democratic leaders in southern California and Los Angeles. He’s already announced a wide-reaching probe into alleged “fraud, waste, abuse and corruption” within programs that receive federal homelessness funding. Don’t be surprised to see Essayli become — or, well, continue to be — one of the state’s most outspoken Republicans.

**43. Dolly M. Gee**

Gee, 65, has been serving on the bench of the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California for 15 years. Originally an Obama appointee, she became chief judge of the nation’s largest district court last spring. Her notable rulings include a finding against the U.S. Justice Department during Trump’s first term, when she appointed an independent monitor to oversee migrant child detention facilities at the border over concerns that detained children weren’t being held in safe or sanitary conditions.

**44. Richard Seeborg**

Seeborg, who will turn 69 in November, is nearing the end of his time as the top judge on the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. His time on the bench since 2010 has been busy, ruling on a variety of issues including the pipeline of tech-related cases that originate from the Bay Area.

**45. Troy L. Nunley**

Nunley, who will turn 61 this year, became chief judge of the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of California last year after first being appointed to the federal bench by Obama in 2013. In the role, he oversees the largest geographical district of the four California district courts that includes Sacramento.

**46. Cynthia Bashant**

Now in her second decade on the federal bench, Bashant was first appointed to the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of California by Obama. She became the chief judge of the judicial district in January, a position the 65-year-old Bashant is eligible to hold for another five years.

**47. Patricia Lock Dawson**

Lock Dawson was reelected last fall to a second term leading Riverside, the Inland Empire’s largest city. But she’s on this list because she was also chosen to lead the “Big City Mayors,” the coalition that represents California’s 13 largest cities — a small group of local leaders who represent more than a quarter of the state’s population. She succeeds San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria in leading a group that’s lobbied for increased state funding for homelessness and housing in the past. The group could look to flex its muscle again amid future budget fights.

**48. Celeste Rodriguez**

One of a handful of mayors who joined the legislative ranks this session, Assistant Assembly Speaker Pro Tempore Rodriguez stands out. Speaker Robert Rivas backed her election bid last year and immediately appointed her to his leadership team following her victory. What’s next is uncertain, but that’s an auspicious start to Rodriguez’s time at the Capitol.

**49. Avelino Valencia**

The Anaheim assemblyman chairs the Banking and Finance Committee. He also has a knack for introducing successful bills — last year, nearly 75% of the bills he introduced were signed by the governor. The two-termer is also eyeing a move up in 2026 —he’s already announced that he plans to run for Tom Umberg’s Senate seat after he is termed-out at the next election.
