How the Food Industry's Main Lobbyist Became a Top Nuisance; Scott Faber campaigns against food chemicals, and his push is finding its moment with the rise of Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

By Jesse Newman

PHOTO: Stephen Voss FOR WSJ Scott Faber has spent more than a decade getting up every day, donning a suit and yelling into a void. Now the void—the Washington, D.C., food-policy establishment—may respond.

Faber, 58, has a reputation as a tenacious gadfly, campaigning against food chemicals, including artificial dyes like Red 3 , sweeteners and preservatives. For big food companies, he's extra troublesome: He previously spent years lobbying for the industry and knows its playbook.

Efforts to overhaul the U.S. food system have long been stymied by industry lobbying and glacial bureaucracy, said Faber, senior vice president for the Environmental Working Group, a Washington, D.C.-based nonprofit whose focus includes chemical safety and agricultural laws. Recent successes have come mostly at the state level, especially in California. The Trump administration could change that. Faber's crusade has been thrust into the national spotlight by the "Make America Healthy Again" campaign promoted by Robert F. Kennedy Jr., President Trump's new head of U.S. health policy . Kennedy has blamed food companies and federal regulators for sickening Americans.

Kennedy's views on health don't entirely align with Faber's. But Faber said it shows people of all political stripes are angry about the status quo in food: "Just because some people are wrong about most things doesn't mean they're wrong about everything."

The food industry uses compounds such as titanium dioxide to brighten colors in goods from pizza to candy, while others like potassium bromate are added to flour to strengthen dough. Food makers turn to other chemicals to help items last longer on store shelves.

Some additives are an unnecessary evil, according to Faber, who with public-health groups and nutrition experts has pressed the Food and Drug Administration to rethink approvals for some substances.

Federal law lets companies themselves determine whether many food ingredients can be considered generally safe . Because it is voluntary, many companies don't bother to notify the FDA of those ingredients.

The result, according to Faber and others, is grocery-store aisles lined with foods containing chemicals that have been associated with health problems, from increased cancer risk to developmental harm and hormone disruption. Some have been banned from food in other countries.

The FDA has said the agency gives priority to food chemical reviews based on risk, science and regulatory authority, and that companies are encouraged to report their own research findings. The agency didn't respond to requests for further comment, citing a federal pause on mass communications.

Faber said chemical manufacturers themselves often select—and pay—the experts who make safety determinations. "To call it rubber stamping is an insult to rubber stamps," he said.

'Generally recognized as safe' Born and raised in a small town outside of Boston, Faber moved to Washington, D.C., in the late 1980s, where he attended law school. He landed a job at the Environmental Defense Fund, a nonprofit advocacy group, and worked to overhaul farm policy.

In 2007, Faber became head lobbyist for the food industry's main trade group. While representing big food and beverage companies' interests, Faber said, he became aware of what critics see as a major loophole in the U.S. food-safety system. Congress in 1958 tasked the FDA with approving all food additives—except those deemed " generally recognized as safe ." The GRAS exemption was created mostly for common ingredients like oil and vinegar, so widely accepted as safe that they don't require government approval. Faber said he and others saw food companies increasingly using the exemption to incorporate new compounds into processed foods while bypassing federal safety reviews.

Some of those substances have been linked to health problems. The FDA in 2015 revoked the GRAS status of partially hydrogenated oils—the primary source of artificial trans fat in processed foods—after trans fat was linked to higher risks of heart disease. Last year the agency took a similar step for tara flour, an ingredient that sickened hundreds of people after they ate it in lentil and leek crumbles .

From his perch inside the food industry, Faber said he had hoped the trade group—now known as the Consumer Brands Association—would encourage companies to be more protective of consumers. He said the effort met with some resistance. When then-first lady Michelle Obama championed new nutrition standards for school meals, Faber said opposition from many food-company members kept the group on the sidelines. He left in 2012 to join the EWG.

There Faber joined other advocacy groups to push the FDA to narrow the GRAS loophole and reassess the safety of food chemicals in use. When those efforts bore little fruit, Faber turned his attention to California, where Gov. Gavin Newsom was signing EWG-sponsored legislation that banned dozens of chemicals from cosmetics sold in the state.

Faber and his allies decided to try something similar with food. "It became clear that the FDA wouldn't do its job and equally clear that states would need to step into the breach," he said.

PHOTO: Stephen Voss FOR WSJ Going west Faber's years inside the food industry came in handy. He parried industry arguments that reformulating products would increase food prices, proffering examples of cheaper, alternative ingredients.

"He knew what lobbyists would do before they themselves knew the arguments they'd make," said Jesse Gabriel, a California assemblyman and Democrat who worked closely with Faber on legislation to ban some food chemicals in the state.

California passed a law in 2023 banning four chemicals on the EWG's "dirty dozen" list: potassium bromate, brominated vegetable oil, propylparaben and Red 3. Since then, states from New York to Oklahoma have introduced similar bills, with the EWG's support.

Nine months after California banned brominated vegetable oil, used in beverages to keep citrus flavoring from floating to the top, the FDA also revoked authorization for its use in food. More than a year after California's ban, the agency did the same with Red 3.

The FDA has also unveiled a plan to bolster its regulation of food chemicals, including devising an enhanced process to review those already in use.

Some food-industry lobbyists acknowledged that Faber and other advocates are outgunning them at the state level, though they believe the efforts stoke unnecessary fear of chemicals. Still, concerned about a patchwork of varying state regulations, food-industry groups have generally backed the FDA's plan to more proactively review chemicals currently in the market, saying it will reaffirm federal authority over food safety.

Kennedy, who will oversee the FDA, plans more scrutiny. At Kennedy's late January confirmation hearing , he said America's food supply is the main culprit for high rates of chronic illness in the U.S. and criticized the GRAS exemption. During his swearing-in ceremony at the White House last week, Kennedy said he would end what he called corruption at the FDA and other agencies. Trump has signed an executive order creating a commission, with Kennedy as chairman, to address root causes of chronic illness.

Faber remains skeptical of major changes under the Trump administration. He is pressing forward in the states, where he sees better odds for change. But he said he is pleased that food chemicals—and the government's handling of them—are getting national attention.

"It's opened a Pandora's box of questions," he said.
