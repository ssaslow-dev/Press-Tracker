The Trump factor in the state budget

By ERIC HE, BLAKE JONES and DUSTIN GARDINER

THE BUZZ: ‘TRUMP SLUMP’ — Gov. Gavin Newsom and other California Democrats have been quick to blame this year’s anticipated budget woes on the blanket tariffs President Donald Trump has imposed and his administration’s funding cuts to safety net programs.

Newsom has taken to calling the broader economic damage a “Trump slump.” Senate Pro Tem Mike McGuire wished “good luck to anyone trying to predict what President Trump will have done to our federal budget and economy by September.” And a spokesperson for Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas said lawmakers could be forced to rejigger their budget in the fall given “the chaotic and punitive budget realities coming from Washington.”

“The whole ballgame is in Washington, D.C.,” Assembly Budget Chair Jesse Gabriel said.

Newsom presumably will keep up with the verbal assaults later this week when he is expected to announce that the state’s budget has plunged from roughly balanced into a deficit of at least $10 billion.

Along with offering the chance to toss a few more barbs at the president — a pastime of sorts for Newsom — blaming the Trump administration’s stop-and-go trade policy for battering California’s budget offers a convenient way for the governor to insulate himself and the Democratic Party from political blowback over an 11-figure shortfall.

And it’s not just a talking point.

The nonpartisan Legislative Analyst’s Office, which critiques Newsom’s budget proposals every year, noted that Trump administration’s actions on tariffs and elsewhere have already pummeled consumer sentiment and narrowed the path for California’s economy to grow. Also concerning is the drop in stock prices since Trump launched his tariff campaign, since market declines have an outsized impact on state revenues because California’s tax structure is heavily reliant on capital gains.

“Things have been pushed and pushed in the wrong direction, principally,” Department of Finance spokesperson H.D. Palmer said, “by the effects that the tariffs are having.”

California has been flush with cash of late, pulling in $4.4 billion more this fiscal year than Finance projected when it unveiled a small surplus in January. But spending has increased as well, fueled in part by a Medi-Cal budget ballooning from growing caseloads of older Californians and immigrants living in the country illegally, among other demands.

Unlike in January, Finance will incorporate the impact of tariffs into its estimates of future revenue — meaning the state will plan on having less money to cover the increasing costs.

Republican state Sen. Roger Niello, the vice chair of the Senate Budget Committee who never misses a chance to accuse Democrats of over-spending, acknowledged that tariffs — which he opposes — may hurt the state’s revenues and that “there are certainly issues at the federal level that are having an effect.” But he said Newsom is “always overly optimistic” in his budget projections, pointing to the Medi-Cal shortfall and a minimum wage increase for health care workers that took effect last year.

“Frankly, just those two items add up to about $10 billion,” Niello said. “So we have built into the budget some of our very own problems.”

Casting the blame for the budget deficit on Trump is all well and good, but Jeff Freitas, president of the California Federation of Teachers, said how Newsom addresses the shortfall is on him. Freitas wants the governor to raise taxes on the wealthy to backfill federal cuts, and reverse an 8 percent slash to both the University of California and California State University systems that he proposed in January.

“If you want to be the shadow president and be opposed to what’s happening at the national level, you can’t hit your university system,” Freitas said.

John Affeldt, managing attorney at the public interest law firm Public Advocates, sounded a similar note. He said that while Newsom has “a lot of maneuverability” to “point the finger elsewhere” for the state’s upcoming budget woes, “The question is, given these external factors that have been imposed on him, what is he going to do with it? How is he going to express his values, in terms of what he’s going to continue to support and continue to cut?”

ON THE MARQUEE — Minnesota Gov. Tim Walz will deliver a keynote address at the California Democratic Party convention in Anaheim on May 31. It will be his first major speech in California since last year’s presidential election.

FIRST IN PLAYBOOK: RIVAS’ NEW GIG — Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas has joined the board of the Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee, the party’s messaging and fundraising arm dedicated to electing more members to statehouses across the country.

Rivas comes to the DLCC at a crucial moment, as Democrats look to state legislature’s to more aggressively counter Trump’s “extremism” in Washington. In a statement, Rivas emphasized the Golden State’s status as a fundraising ATM: “As one of the world’s largest economies and a powerful donor state, California’s legislators must, can, and will leverage this to fight Trump’s dangerous policies.”

The speaker joins the DLCC board along with top legislative leaders from Colorado, Illinois, Michigan, Oregon, Virginia and Wisconsin — a mix of leaders from blue bastions and those in states where Democrats are fighting to regain power.

MIDTERM MESSAGING — Another campaign pressuring frontline House Republicans not to cut Medicaid is launching today, spending at least $2 million on digital and radio ads across six congressional districts.

In California, the Health Justice Action Fund is zeroing in on Reps. David Valadao and Ken Calvert, whose districts have the largest shares of Medicaid recipients among Democrats’ top House targets in the state. Valadao has urged Republican leaders not to slash the program as caucus hardliners push for deep reductions.

The campaign is at least the third of its kind in California this cycle, piling onto similar efforts from the coalitions Protect Our Care and Fight for Our Health. Leading the effort is St. John’s Community Health, the system of clinics in Southern California.

The first round of digital spots, reviewed by Playbook, show Medicaid recipients pleading with Congress not to take away their health care. The campaign will also circulate petitions asking members not to vote for reductions.

SEEKING A PROMOTION — Richard Barrera, senior policy adviser to state Superintendent Tony Thurmond and longtime member of the board of the San Diego Unified School District, is jumping into the race to replace his boss next year. Barrera announced his candidacy last week, joining a growing field of candidates to be the next schools chief.

Barrera told Playbook that while all Democrats in the race — which so far include Assembly Education Chair Al Muratsuchi, former state Sen. Josh Newman and Los Angeles Community College District trustee Nichelle Henderson — will likely commit to protecting California against the Trump administration’s attempts to use “federal funding as a lever to push their ideological agenda onto schools,” he hinted at backing a tax increase on wealthy Californians to help insulate the state from funding threats. It’s an idea Thurmond himself has floated.

“If you are benefiting from enormous wealth as a Californian, the public education system likely helped you create that wealth,” Barrera said. “And it’s time for you to reinvest.”

Barrera sees the role of the state superintendent as an organizer and wants the state’s Department of Education to ramp up the number of field offices to better equip local districts.

“It’s not that you have formal power to command school districts to do what you want, or certainly to command the Legislature to do what you want,” Barrera said. “It’s about helping people build coalitions, and build their own power so that they can move forward and make progress.”

FIRST IN POLITICO: NEWSOM V LOCALS CONT. — Turlock city officials refused to allocate $1 for homelessness. Now they’re paying a steeper political price.

Newsom took the Central Valley town’s leaders to task on Friday for a vote that effectively blocked state funds from flowing to a nonprofit that operates a homeless shelter. The governor’s blistering letter both warned Turlock that it could face penalties for not acting — and used the city to reinforce a larger point.

Newsom has spent years demanding cities and counties do more to build housing and get people off the streets, including by threatening to cut off state largesse. His letter cast Turlock as part of a “larger, unacceptable trend Californians are seeing across the state: even as the State delivers unprecedented resources, some local governments continue to abdicate their responsibilities in the face of a humanitarian emergency on our streets.” — Jeremy B. White

GOING NATIONAL — Joe Nation is the newest adviser to California’s cap-and-trade program — but he’s in familiar territory, having co-authored the state’s original climate law, AB 32. NOT ALL ABOARD — Former Rep. Katie Porter told KTLA “I don’t think we should BS California voters” when it comes to California’s high-speed rail project.

Porter told the station that she’d take a fresh look at the project if elected governor next year.

“Increasingly, the evidence is showing that this project is not going to be able to be completed remotely on budget or remotely on time,” Porter said. “If this high-speed rail project can get done, then let’s get it done. If it can’t get done, then stop.”

SALT OF THE DISTRICT — Orange County Republican Rep. Young Kim said a proposed $30,000 cap on the state and local tax deduction would be a “slap in the face to the hardworking taxpayers in her district.”

“I’ve become one of the saltiest members of Congress,” the OC Register quoted her saying during a roundtable with small business owners at the Yorba Linda Public Library on Friday.

‘BLUE TAPE’ — JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon slammed California’s construction regulations in an interview with FOX 11 Los Angeles in Pacific Palisades, where a Chase branch burned down earlier this year.

“I’d change the name from red tape to blue tape because it’s Democrats who want more and more regulations,” Dimon said.
