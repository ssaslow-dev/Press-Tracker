Faculty union targets Jewish political money

By DUSTIN GARDINER, ERIC HE and BLAKE JONES

SHUT OUT — Jewish advocacy groups and politicians in California are raising alarms over what they say is a growing effort by some on the left to exclude prominent Jewish donors from contributing to candidates for public office.

The issue flared in recent days over a questionnaire circulated to potential office holders from the California Faculty Association that asks candidates if they’ve ever accepted money or endorsements from the Jewish Public Affairs Committee of California, a nonpartisan coalition of community groups, or the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, the pro-Israel political lobby — a decision the faculty group’s leaders said stemmed in part from antisemitism legislation that Gov. Gavin Newsom signed into law despite CFA’s opposition.

“Do you have endorsements or take contributions from groups and sectors like AIPAC/JPAC, the Oil Industry, the Tobacco Industry, police associations, etc.?” the questionnaire reads, noting that the faculty union won’t support candidates who accept money from groups that “harm working people.”

Jewish leaders said they were shocked by the inclusion of JPAC, a coalition that includes a host of Jewish nonprofits, professional associations and several progressive-leaning groups — and which doesn’t make political contributions.

It’s the latest in a series of intraparty rifts that have bitterly divided the California Democratic Party over the Israeli-Palestinian conflict in recent years. Several Jewish state lawmakers say they worry the effort could stoke antisemitism and wrongly associate Jewish people with the humanitarian crisis in Gaza. They said it feels like a push to cancel Jewish voices.

“I can’t for the life of me figure out why some folks at CFA are choosing to spend their time and energy attacking Jewish social services organizations that have literally been at the front lines helping the poor, working families, and immigrant communities,” Jesse Gabriel, a state lawmaker who co-chairs the Legislature’s Jewish Caucus, told Playbook.

Melina Abdullah, chair of CFA’s political action and legislation committee, told Playbook that the questionnaire has “nothing to do with whether or not a candidate is Jewish” and that “nowhere in the questionnaire does it say that we’re not going to support a candidate if they’re supported by JPAC.” She added that while a candidate’s response could warrant “additional conversation,” the questionnaire is just one step of the endorsement process.

The move by the Faculty Association came after it strongly opposed a measure sponsored by JPAC aiming to clamp down on antisemitism in schools. AB 715 — which was also opposed by the California Teachers Association and nearly every other major education group over academic freedom concerns — triggered a bruising and emotional fight this year in the Legislature that spiraled into a proxy for Israel’s war in Gaza. Debate over the bill revealed fissures among Democrats, some of whom were uncomfortable with what they saw as having to choose between fighting antisemitism and not chilling teachers’ speech.

Abdullah said that the CFA’s question about JPAC was intended to “get to the bottom” of where candidates stood on the measure, which passed the Legislature without a dissenting vote after tough negotiations.

“We wanted to be clear about that and find out which candidates are affiliated in any way with that effort to censor classroom teachers,” Abdullah said. “It’s a censorship bill, which threatens the freedom of speech of educators and is also anti-union.”

Arab Resource & Organizing Center Action, a pro-Palestinian group that helped lead a coalition against AB 715, defended CFA’s candidate questions while denouncing JPAC’s backing of the antisemitism bill, which it called “one of this year’s most consequential assaults on academic freedom and public education in California.”

“In our view, CFA and other unions are not only standing on the right side of history, they are being sensible: The priorities of JPAC don’t reflect the priorities of the majority of California voters; it is common sense to withhold an endorsement of any candidate who isn’t willing to put the interests of their constituents first,” said the group’s executive director, Lara Kiswani.

But Marshall Wittmann, a spokesperson for AIPAC, accused CFA of “ostracizing fellow citizens who are engaged in the democratic process to strengthen the US-Israel partnership” and of discriminating against pro-Israel Americans.

The furor over the candidate questions was amplified over the weekend when Democratic organizers in Los Angeles County received an anonymous text message from a sender purporting to represent 15 party delegates who’ve formed a Free Palestine Caucus. The text demanded candidates not accept any money from donors affiliated with AIPAC, which has a track record of spending heavily in primaries, and called on several state lawmakers to return contributions they’ve accepted from its president, Michael Tuchin, a prominent donor and Los Angeles lawyer.

Meanwhile, David Bocarsly, JPAC’s executive director, called CFA’s action “deeply disheartening and scary,” accusing the union of playing into “a trope about Jewish money and Jewish power.” JPAC is a diverse coalition that focuses on supporting Jewish issues and values at the state level, Bocarsly said, and should not be conflated with AIPAC.

He stressed that JPAC is an “ethno-religious community” rather than an interest group and does not make political contributions or endorse candidates. The coalition, he said, includes organizations that provide social services, help asylum seekers and give legal aid to undocumented immigrants.

LURIE SPEAKS OUT — San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie on Monday afternoon posted a lengthy, yet measured, response to President Donald Trump’s threat of a National Guard deployment to his city: “I am deeply grateful to the members of our military for their service to our country,” he wrote, “but the National Guard does not have the authority to arrest drug dealers — and sending them to San Francisco will do nothing to get fentanyl off the streets or make our city safer.”

Lurie doesn’t mention Trump by name. That’s in keeping with his past practice. Lurie, a moderate Democrat, has refrained from railing against Trump, with advisers and allies describing his strategy as, in part, an effort to avoid drawing Trump’s attention to San Francisco.

TAKING ON TRUMP — Trump hasn’t threatened to send troops to San Diego, but California’s second-largest city may yet find itself in the crosshairs of the administration. On Monday, the City Council gave its initial approval to the “Due Process and Safety Ordinance,” a measure intended to set up some additional safeguards for civil liberties amid the administration’s aggressive immigration enforcement campaign. The effects of the measure are limited, and it still must undergo further review before coming back to the council for final passage. But the unanimous vote and the nearly 100 people testifying in favor of it, after a weekend “No Kings” rally that drew tens of thousands of people to the city’s downtown, suggest it has solid prospects.

The measure would require a judicial warrant for access to non-public municipal facilities or city-funded projects, making it harder to conduct immigration sweeps. It also limits participation in enforcement actions or data-sharing targeting residents based on protected personal characteristics, such as their race, or activities such as exercising free-speech. The city, for example, couldn’t share with federal authorities any data related to Saturday’s massive “No Kings” protest. The law would not prevent the police from working with federal authorities on trafficking cases or other serious matters, nor would it do anything to stop the stepped up enforcement by Immigration and Customs Enforcement.

“Sadly, we cannot control the actions of the federal government,” said City Councilmember Sean Elo-Rivera, the measure’s sponsor. “And the most obvious thing that we can do is to make sure we don’t allow a cent of our money, a moment of our time, or a single piece of our data, to be used by the federal government to violate anyone in San Diego’s rights.” — Ben Fox
