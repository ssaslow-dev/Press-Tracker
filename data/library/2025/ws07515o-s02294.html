Facing Legal Pressure, Glock Revamps Pistols To Prevent Machine Gun Conversions

The change comes after The Trace’s reporting informed California’s decision to ban the gunmaker’s products.

In September, two men outside a Walmart in Indianapolis allegedly shot three members of the same family with converted Glock pistols. A thumbnail-size device called a switch had effectively turned the weapons into machine guns, enabling them to fire as many as 20 bullets per second. One victim was hit in his hand, abdomen, legs, and chest. His brother suffered two gunshot wounds; his cousin, 11. All three survived.

Glocks outfitted with switches have turned up in crimes with devastating frequency across the country, prompting federal authorities to declare them a “serious threat to public safety.” 

Glock has for years dismissed the idea that it has any responsibility — or even the ability — to design handguns impervious to switches. But now, the gunmaker has abruptly reversed course, announcing that, come December, it will discontinue two of its signature pistols lines and replace them with a model apparently designed to resist the devices.

The about-face comes after California passed a first-of-its-kind ban on Glock handguns, a measure authored by Democratic State Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel. A spokesperson for Gabriel’s office said the ban was informed by reporting from The Trace, which last year detailed accounts from former Glock employees and firearm experts who argued Glock could alter its designs to build pistols more resistant to conversion.

Anthony Beverly, who runs Stop the Violence Indianapolis, a gun violence prevention group, said he was encouraged to hear that the gun industry had taken some accountability in the fight to combat machine gun shootings. “The industry is usually an uphill battle,” he said. “But this sounds like some good news.”

Glock did not respond to multiple requests for comment. 

The new line of pistols, known as Gen V, is virtually indistinguishable from its predecessors. But at the back of the gun, where switches are typically installed, the Gen V has a short steel rail to block switches from interfering with the weapon’s firing mechanism. Earlier Glock handguns relied only on a plastic nub, which could be easily shaved down with a file.

California’s law, passed in October, bans guns that can be converted to automatic fire with household tools. The law specifically singles out those with “cruciform trigger bars” — a reference to the construction of an internal component used in all Glocks. The Gen Vs also use a cruciform trigger bar, making it unlikely they will clear the state’s new standard. Images and videos have circulated online of Gen Vs modified to accept switches, though the changes were likely made using machining equipment, making them much harder for the average consumer to perform. 

New York, Illinois, and Maryland are mulling similar bans, though the bills will have to be considered or reintroduced in next year’s legislative session. New York State Senator Zellnor Myrie, who proposed New York’s bill, said he was confident of its chances after the Gen V announcement. “This confirms that state laws can drive changes that protect public safety — even when gun companies say it’s impossible,” he said. 

Most of the switches in the United States are illegal, and none are manufactured or sanctioned by Glock. They are mostly purchased online from China or 3D-printed at home. For years, state attorneys general and city mayors have demanded Glock overhaul its designs to more seriously address the crisis. In 2024, Chicago sued Glock, arguing that the company deceived consumers about the safety of its products and fueled the proliferation of machine gun fire on the city’s streets. Baltimore and Seattle subsequently filed similar suits, as did the attorneys general of New Jersey and Minnesota. Another 11 attorneys general signed a letter signaling their intention to sue, though no additional litigation has come. 

Glock sought to dismiss the lawsuits, but judges in state and federal court this year have allowed the Chicago, New Jersey, and Minnesota cases to proceed. (Judges have yet to rule on motions in Baltimore and Seattle.)

Chicago Mayor Brandon Johnson and New Jersey Attorney General Matthew Platkin linked Glock’s reversal to the company’s recent courtroom setbacks. Johnson told The Trace via email that Chicago “played a key role in pressuring Glock to make necessary changes.” Platkin noted that the Gen V was announced just weeks after a federal judge permitted New Jersey’s case to move forward. 

Both officials said they would press ahead with their lawsuits, which demand that Glock pay damages for the violence wrought with its products. “We will continue to fight to hold them accountable under the law,” Platkin said.
