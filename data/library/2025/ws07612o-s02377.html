An ACIP surprise

Driving the Day

COVID SHOT CHANGE — The CDC and its panel of expert advisers quietly expanded access to Covid-19 vaccination during pregnancy, undoing an earlier recommendation from HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to stop recommending that pregnant women get the shots.

The CDC’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices voted in September to recommend that adults can choose to get the Covid shot after consulting with their providers, also known as shared clinical decision-making. The panel didn’t specifically vote on whether the shot should be administered during pregnancy, yet the vote appears to encompass pregnant women, according to an update this month on the CDC website that reflects the new guidance.

That development surprised some, including legal experts.

“Covid-19 vaccine in pregnancy had been discussed initially — ACIP recommended them — then the secretary took that back,” said Dorit Reiss, a vaccine law expert at the University of California’s law school in San Francisco.“Under these circumstances, if they were going to change the decision about pregnancy, I would have expected them to address it expressly, since it was changed expressly [by Kennedy].”

It’s unclear whether all 12 panel members, handpicked by Kennedy, were aware that the move would undo his decision. Much of the panel’s two-day September meeting in Atlanta was marked by confusion as recently appointed members struggled with the procedures. ACIP Chair Martin Kulldorff did not immediately respond to a request for comment, nor did a spokesperson for HHS.

Why it matters: The new guidance for adults means that pharmacies can administer the vaccine to pregnant women, and almost all insurers must cover the shots with no cost-sharing — guaranteeing expanded access for people who want to be vaccinated.

Vaccinating children under 6 months against Covid-19 isn’t recommended, so many providers see vaccination during pregnancy as a key tool for protecting vulnerable infants from the infection.

The panel’s Covid recommendation contradicts what many public health experts had expected from the September meeting. Kennedy fired all the panel’s members in June, and some new members have argued that Covid vaccines aren’t safe, spurring concerns that the panel would vote to drastically narrow access to the shots.

AROUND THE AGENCIES

TYLENOL CONFUSION — Most people are uncertain about whether they believe the claim that Tylenol causes autism, according to a KFF poll published today.

Last month, President Donald Trump told women not to take the common pain reliever during pregnancy, linking it to an increased risk of autism, though most prominent voices in the public health establishment say there is no proven causal link between the two.

The public is unsure what to believe. Most adults say the claim is either “probably true” or “probably false,” with only 4 percent saying they believe the claim was “definitely true” and 35 percent saying it was “definitely false.” Nearly 80 percent of adults reported having heard the claim.

Republicans were much more likely to say the claim was probably or definitely true, at 57 percent, while 86 percent of Democrats said the claim was probably or definitely false.

CDC trust: Trust in the CDC’s vaccine guidance has reached its lowest level since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, KFF reports. Half of the public reported trusting the CDC “a great deal” or “fair amount” to provide reliable information about vaccines.

Kennedy support: Most people are critical of Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s performance as health secretary, according to KFF.

Around 4 in 10 adults say they “strongly” or “somewhat” approve of the way Kennedy is handling his job as HHS secretary, compared with 59 percent who say they either “somewhat” or “strongly” disapprove of his job performance.

The public was similarly critical of Kennedy’s actions on vaccines, with 37 percent of adults saying they approve of his handling of vaccine policy compared with 62 percent who disapprove.

Poll details: The nationwide KFF Health Tracking Poll was conducted September 23-2 online and by phone, with a sample of 1,334 U.S. adults.

IN THE STATES

IS GAVIN NEWSOM ‘MAHA’? California Gov. Gavin Newsom signed legislation to phase out ultraprocessed foods from California schools— drawing attention for the plan’s parallels to HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s Make America Healthy Again movement to combat chronic diseases, POLITICO’s Nicole Norman reports.

Why it matters: The policy marked a rare moment of bipartisanship in the legislature, drawing support not only from Democrats but from Republicans who saw it as an advancement of the MAHA initiative.

The bill creates the nation’s first statutory definition of ultraprocessed, initially a sticking point for the bill as it moved through the legislature. Democratic Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel said he took more than 50 amendments from lawmakers and agricultural interests who were concerned the definition would ban foods that aren’t necessarily unhealthy, like shredded cheese.

Key context: The move comes as Kennedy and the federal government appear to be working toward their own definition of ultraprocessed. In September, the MAHA Commission recommended a slew of strategies to President Donald Trump meant to address “America’s childhood chronic disease epidemic.” Among them was a recommendation to have the USDA, HHS and the FDA continue efforts to develop a government-wide definition of ultraprocessed foods “to support future research and policy activity.”

In Congress

FIGHTING WORDS — Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Senate HELP Chair Bill Cassidy finally agree on something: a push to reconsider the American Medical Association’s role in determining how doctors are paid, POLITICO’s Simon J. Levien reports.

The Louisiana Republican has harshly criticized Kennedy for firing the CDC director, replacing members of a vaccine advisory panel and reconsidering government guidance on Covid and hepatitis B shots. But the two are aligned in questioning the key role the nation’s leading physicians’ society, the AMA, plays in setting the rates doctors are paid.

In an Oct. 6 letter to AMA President Bobby Mukkamala, Cassidy attacked the AMA for its coding system, which doctors use to bill Medicare, Medicaid and private insurers for their services. Cassidy said he suspected the AMA is driving up health costs by charging fees to the doctors, hospitals, health plans and health IT vendors that must license its codes.

Kennedy has reportedly weighed a plan to reduce the AMA’s role. Besides the group’s sway over the codes — which Congress endorsed in the 1996 Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act — an AMA panel recommends reimbursement rates for medical procedures to Medicare, the insurance program for seniors, which are often adopted.

The AMA declined to comment on Cassidy’s criticism.
