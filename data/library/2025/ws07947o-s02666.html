Will California's 'Trump-proofing' proposals protect criminals from deportation?

Updated: 6:35 PM PST Jan 27, 2025Editorial Standards ⓘAshley ZavalaPolitical DirectorSACRAMENTO, Calif. —

California lawmakers in the State Assembly held their first hearing on proposals that are meant to prepare for legal battles with President Donald Trump and his administration, while also providing legal and support services to the state's immigrant population.

Gov. Gavin Newsom called a special legislative session on the issue days after the election that President Trump won.

The proposals specifically allow the California Department of Justice to use an extra $25 million for possible litigation with the Trump administration and for the department to create a website to provide public information on the lawsuits. Another provides $25 million in grants to non-profit groups that provide immigration support and legal services, especially for those facing eviction, wage theft and intimate partner violence.

In response to repeated questioning from Republicans, Erika Li with Gov. Newsom's Department of Finance said the funds are only for civil services provided by the non-profits and are not meant to protect criminals from deportation. Officials noted the fund has been around for 26 years.

"They support indigent individuals in obtaining legal aid and assistance to figure out next steps in their approach to support their safety and security in the state," Jennifer Kim, an official with the Assembly's Budget Committee staff, told Assemblyman Bill Essayli, R-Riverside.

"Would they use our tax dollars to defend illegal immigrants from deportation proceedings is that part of what they do?" Essayli asked. Kim said the fund used to do this is for civil proceedings only, not criminal.

"If someone has been convicted of a felony who warrants deportation, that's a different process," Kim said.

"You're assuring us none of this money will be used to defend criminal illegal immigrants facing deportation?" Essayli asked. The two budget officials could not make the guarantee.

Assemblyman Joe Patterson, R-Rocklin, asked the administration if the non-profit organizations had checks and balances to ensure the money was used as intended.

"Hopefully they're not already assisting people with criminal records," Patterson said. "Is there a check and balance of that already?"

"I would have to get back to you on the exact checks and balances that are required through these contracts," Li responded.

After the hearing, the committee's chairman, Assemblyman Jesse Gabriel D-Encino told KCRA 3 the funds are not allowed to be used to protect criminals from deportation.

"I think it was good that we had the conversation, I think there is a lot of confusion out there about what these proposals do," Gabriel said. "Really what we're doing with the litigation funding is putting ourselves in a position to protect California taxpayers and Californians. We're not going to spend any of this money unless and until we need to. The hope, of course, is that we don't have to spend any of it."

California sued the first Trump administration 123 times. Newsom's Department of Finance told lawmakers on Monday that the state won 2/3 of the lawsuits. Most of them were environmental-related, protecting state programs and billions in funding.

California did not have a special legislative session during the first Trump administration to authorize funds. Li noted the money was provided through California's typical budget process, but said the current effort was to proactively prepare for the possible litigation.

The State Senate approved both proposals last week. If the Assembly makes no changes, the lower house could send the bills to the governor as early as this week.
