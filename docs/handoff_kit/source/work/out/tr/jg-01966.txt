Who owns art stolen by the Nazis? Debate grows between museums and Holocaust survivors

Sept. 19, 2026, 9:18 PM EDT

Relatives of Jewish Holocaust survivors have recently filed two lawsuits in California arguing that they are the rightful owners of several pieces of art that were looted or stolen by the Nazis and are now in museums.

The lawsuits respond to a law passed in California in 2024 and a U.S. federal law passed in 2025. The state law makes it easier for survivors' descendants to file these claims. The federal law eliminated the deadline, originally set for December of this year, by which people who survived the Holocaust and later settled in the United States, or their relatives, could bring such civil claims for property taken from them between 1933 and 1945. Now the deadline, instead of a fixed date, is six years after they learn where the piece they link to their relatives is located.

The lawsuits were filed separately, though both in Los Angeles courts. One is against the Norton Simon Museum in Pasadena, and the other is against the Auschwitz-Birkenau Museum. For their part, both museums argue that the works deserve to remain on public display.

Watercolor portraits

The dispute with the Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial museum, built in remembrance on the site of the former concentration and extermination camp, concerns a series of watercolor portraits that Dinah Gottliebova Babbitt made of fellow captives. Her daughters are suing because they say their mother always wanted to recover the paintings, which she was forced to make by the Nazi Josef Mengele (nicknamed the Angel of Death for his sadistic experiments on Auschwitz prisoners).

[Caption: A watercolor portrait painted by Dina Gottliebova Babbitt, part of a lawsuit by her daughters against the Auschwitz-Birkenau Museum. Image of the work included as evidence, via the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California.]

According to Gottliebova Babbitt's daughters, she painted them "on the condition that she and her mother be spared from the gas chambers," but "Mengele took the portraits from her" afterward, and she "never sold, assigned, transferred or voluntarily gave them up."

"The ownership the museum claims rests on postwar transactions and acts by people who had no legal right to the watercolors," the lawsuit alleges, according to The New York Times.

The Auschwitz Memorial says the watercolors are technically not Nazi-looted art, because Gottliebova Babbitt painted them while already a prisoner, so ownership is more open to question.

"We understand that the family has an emotional bond to these works, which Dina Gottliebova made on the orders of Josef Mengele in circumstances in which her life was at stake," said Paweł Sawicki, a museum spokesman.

He added that "the portraits of Roma victims are among the few remaining documents made by Mengele as part of his criminal experiments," and as such "must remain" at the museum because they are "documents related to the history of Auschwitz that preserve a memory of its victims."

A piece of 'restitution'

The other lawsuit targets the Norton Simon Museum, home to "Adam and Eve" by Lucas Cranach the Elder, a Renaissance artist known for biblical scenes and portraits of figures such as Martin Luther.

[Caption: A copy of "Adam and Eve," a diptych by Lucas Cranach the Elder, at its unveiling for a 2016 exhibition at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs in Paris. Jacques Demarthon / AFP via Getty Images]

The diptych was one of several pieces that Hitler's second-in-command, Hermann Göring, took from Dutch art dealer Jacques Goudstikker. Goudstikker's collection was later recovered by Allied forces, which gave the works to the postwar government of the Netherlands. The Adam and Eve diptych was later sold to the Norton Simon Museum in 1971.

The lawsuit argues that other parts of Goudstikker's collection have already been recovered by his heir, Marei von Saher, and that she should also be entitled to the diptych, which she has been asking the Norton Simon to return for years.

In 2018, von Saher had sued directly to obtain "Adam and Eve," but a court ruled in favor of the Norton Simon Museum.

This time the lawsuit was filed by the Jewish Federation of Los Angeles, to which von Saher assigned the right to obtain the diptych on the condition that it later be sold and the proceeds used to "care for Holocaust survivors in the LA area who live in poverty."

Getting the work back would be "one piece of the puzzle in restoring my grandfather's legacy and his collection," Charlène von Saher, Marei's daughter, told the Los Angeles Times.

The Norton Simon Art Foundation said in a statement that the museum holds proper title to the work, and that a federal court established as much in 2018. The foundation added that "Adam and Eve" "have been on display at the museum for more than 50 years and will remain accessible to the public there for many years."

A complex history

The recent California and congressional laws on claims to possibly stolen art respond to a similar case.

That case centered on a masterpiece by Impressionist Camille Pissarro that shows an everyday scene in Paris and was painted in 1897.

[Caption: The painting "Rue Saint-Honoré in the Afternoon, Effect of Rain" seen at the Thyssen-Bornemisza museum in 2019. Quim Llenas / Getty Images]

In 2022, a U.S. federal court ruled that the Thyssen-Bornemisza National Museum in Madrid held title to the work, which originally hung in the Cassirer family home in 1939. The Nazis told Lilly Cassirer Neubauer that the only way she could leave Germany was to hand over the painting, and in exchange they would let her obtain visas for herself and her husband.

An art appraiser submitted documents indicating the Cassirers allegedly received $360 for the painting. Today, "Rue Saint-Honoré in the Afternoon, Effect of Rain" is estimated to be worth about $20 million.

Lilly Cassirer's grandson, Claude, lives in Los Angeles. The new California law has revived the case, and the U.S. Supreme Court ordered federal courts to review their earlier ruling. The hearing is scheduled for October.

The California law was championed by Assemblymember Jesse Gabriel, who told The Associated Press that it is time for museums around the world to "engage in some soul-searching about whether they want to be on the right side of history."
