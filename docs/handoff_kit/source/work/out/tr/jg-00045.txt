New laws spark legal battle in the US over art looted by the Nazis

LOS ANGELES (AP) — Jewish heirs of Holocaust victims and their representatives have filed two lawsuits in California to press their claims to Nazi-looted paintings that still hang on the walls of prominent museums in Los Angeles and Auschwitz.

These are the first lawsuits filed since California and Congress recently passed laws that strengthen U.S.-based claims to artworks taken by force from Jewish families during World War II.

One of the lawsuits was filed Monday in Los Angeles Superior Court by the Jewish Federation of Greater Los Angeles and its board chair, Daniel Gryczman, against the Norton Simon Museum in Pasadena, seeking the return of "Adam and Eve." These are two masterful life-size oil paintings on wood created by Lucas Cranach the Elder in 1530.

The other lawsuit was filed in federal court in Los Angeles the same day by the daughters of the late Dinah Gottliebova Babbitt, a Holocaust survivor and California-based animator who was held at Auschwitz. It demands the return of watercolors of other prisoners that the artist was forced to paint for Josef Mengele, a Nazi doctor who earned the nickname "Angel of Death."

Spokespeople for both museums say the institutions hold legal title to the works and intend to keep displaying them to the public. The lawsuits do not specify the monetary value of the paintings.

Artworks pit Jewish families against museums

The subject of Nazi-looted art is an emotional issue, loaded with legal and ethical implications, that pits Jewish families who have suffered intergenerational trauma against museums that, in their view, are trying to preserve a painful but important chapter of history.

Between 1933 and 1945, the Nazi regime orchestrated the largest art theft in world history, seizing more than 600,000 artworks across Europe belonging to Jewish families, collectors and museums. Decades later, many of these masterpieces are in prominent museums around the world, which makes the push to recover them more complex than a simple property dispute.

Rabbi Noah Farkas is president and CEO of the Jewish Federation of Los Angeles, the organization to which Marei von Saher, the sole heir of Dutch Jewish art dealer Jacques Goudstikker, has assigned her rights. The paintings were confiscated from Goudstikker's collection by Hermann Göring, Adolf Hitler's second-in-command, and passed through several hands before being sold in the 1960s to the Pasadena Art Museum, which was renamed the Norton Simon Museum in honor of its benefactor.

Farkas said the federation plans to use most of the net proceeds from recovered art to provide aid to the roughly 2,500 Holocaust survivors living in the Los Angeles area who urgently need it.

"These are incredibly beautiful European masterpieces, stolen from a Jewish family to enrich the Nazi party and finance the machinery of war and death against the people," he said. "It is an honor for us to take on this role on behalf of the community and to use most of the proceeds to help these elderly survivors live the last years of their lives with dignity, comfort and peace, something their relatives were denied during the Holocaust."

Marei von Saher said in a statement that she is grateful to the Jewish organization for taking up her family's cause. "This effort represents an important step toward justice for Holocaust survivors and victims of Nazi-era atrocities," she said.

New legislation gives the lawsuits a second push

Jesse Gabriel, a Democratic Assemblymember from Woodland Hills, California, wrote Assembly Bill 2867, signed by Gov. Gavin Newsom in September 2024, which requires that state law apply in lawsuits brought by California residents or their heirs to recover art confiscated during the Holocaust or during other acts of persecution.

Gabriel, who co-chairs the California Legislative Jewish Caucus, introduced the legislation in response to a federal court ruling that allowed the state-owned Thyssen-Bornemisza National Museum in Madrid to keep a multimillion-dollar Impressionist masterpiece by Camille Pissarro.

That painting was taken from the Cassirer family by the Nazis in 1939. The case, which prompted the new law, is expected to be heard in federal court in California next month.

Gabriel said it is time for museums to "do some deep soul-searching about whether they want to be on the right side of history."

Museums want to preserve the paintings for posterity

The Norton Simon Museum released a statement noting that a federal court unanimously found in 2018 that the museum had valid title to the Cranach paintings. In May 2019, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to take up the case, leaving the lower court rulings in place.

The museum said the Cranach paintings, which originally belonged to the Stroganoff family, were put up for sale in 1931 by the Soviet Union at an illicit auction in Berlin, where they were acquired by Goudstikker. After a complicated journey, the paintings were sold to the museum by a descendant of the Stroganoff family. The museum then restored and preserved the pieces.

"For nearly 50 years, they have been on display at the Norton Simon Museum and will remain accessible to the public for many years to come," the statement said.

Pawel Sawicki, a spokesman for the Auschwitz Memorial, said the watercolors of Roma victims that Gottliebova Babbitt was forced by Mengele to paint to document his horrific experiments and racial research "must remain at the Memorial" to document the man's crimes.

He said the paintings should not be mischaracterized as "Nazi-looted art," because Gottliebova Babbitt painted them against her will as a prisoner and therefore did not own the watercolors.

While he understands the family's feelings, Sawicki said the portraits of Roma and Sinti victims are among the few remaining documents of Mengele's criminal experiments and must be treated as unique documents related to the history of the Auschwitz concentration camp.

"While fully respecting the rights of the people who created some of the documents we hold here, we believe that any loss to the Memorial's collections would constitute irreparable damage," he said. "... The loss of even the smallest part of the documentation is an irreparable loss and a stain on the memory of the victims of Auschwitz."

Advocates hope the cases will now be judged on their merits

Joel Greenberg, founder of Art Ashes, a Philadelphia-area nonprofit that helps Jewish families trace and recover Nazi-looted art, said that in addition to the California law, President Donald Trump signed an update to the Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act of 2025. That new law permanently removed the expiration date, allowing Holocaust survivors and their heirs to pursue recovery of these artworks indefinitely.

Greenberg explained that the new federal law gave families the right to claim their art for up to six years from the time they learn who holds it.

"These new laws mean that technical defenses will no longer prevail in court," he said. "Every case will be heard on its merits."

He added that the new legislation also ensures these Jewish families are heard in court and have a fair process to determine ownership of stolen art.

"It will make sure the stories of what happened to European Jewry are not lost or forgotten," he said. "Art can still be returned to its rightful owner."
